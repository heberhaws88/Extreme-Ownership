I was sitting in sacrament meeting a few Sundays ago, and the speaker was talking about Peter's denial of Christ. He walked through the whole scene: Peter swearing he'd die before he'd betray the Savior, and then a few hours later, three times in a row, insisting he didn't even know the man. The speaker said something about how hard it must have been for Peter to face that failure. And I sat there in the pew with my wife next to me and my kids trying to stay quiet, and I thought, yeah, but at least Peter owned it. At least he didn't blame the servant girl for asking the question, or the circumstances for putting him in that spot. He wept bitterly because he knew what he'd done. He took it on himself.

And then I thought about all the things I've blamed on other people in just the last week alone.

Tonight's principle is Extreme Ownership. It comes from the book Extreme Ownership by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin, two former Navy SEAL officers who wrote about leadership principles forged in combat. The core idea is this: as a leader, you own everything in your world. Every outcome, every failure, every gap in performance or execution. You don't pass blame down to your people or sideways to circumstances or up the chain to someone else's decision. You take it. All of it. No excuses. You own it, and then you fix it.

And before I get into the story I want to tell tonight, I need to spend a minute here, because this principle sits right at the center of what it means to follow Jesus Christ, and I don't think we can talk about ownership without talking about Gethsemane.

The Savior took ownership of something that wasn't His. He took ownership of my sin, your sin, every wrong thing ever done or ever to be done. He didn't have to. He was the only perfect being to walk the earth. He had zero culpability, zero personal failure to account for. And yet He went into that garden and He said, nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done. He took it all on Himself. The weight of every broken thing in the entire human story pressed down on Him, and He chose to own it. To carry it. To pay for it.

That's leadership at a level none of us will ever match. But it's the standard. It's the pattern.

And it shows up again and again in scripture, this idea of stepping forward and owning the gap instead of pointing at someone else. You see it in the Book of Mormon when King Benjamin tells his people, "I have not sought gold nor silver nor any manner of riches of you; neither have I suffered that ye should be confined in dungeons, nor that ye should make slaves one of another." He's owning his stewardship. He's not blaming them for being hard to govern or for making his job difficult. He's saying, this is what I was called to do, and I did it. Mosiah chapter two. Or you think about Nephi, after his brothers complain for the hundredth time, and he doesn't throw up his hands and say, well, these guys are impossible, what do you want me to do? He keeps leading. He keeps trying. He owns the mission.

But here's where it gets uncomfortable for me. Because I know the doctrine. I know the examples. I teach this stuff to my kids. And I still catch myself, all the time, blaming other people when things go wrong.

A couple of months ago, I had a training night with my daughter. She's old enough now that I've started teaching her some basic electrical work, mostly because she asked and because I want her to understand how things actually function, not just flip a switch and assume magic happens. So we were in the garage one evening, and I was showing her how to wire a basic three-way switch setup. Two switches controlling one light from different locations. It's not complicated if you understand the traveler wires, but it's one of those things that trips people up if the concept doesn't click.

I explained it once. I drew the diagram on a piece of cardboard. I handed her the wire strippers and walked her through stripping the ends, connecting the hot to the common terminal, running the travelers between the two switches. She's a sharp kid. She was paying attention. And when we flipped the breaker back on to test it, nothing happened. No light.

I figured she'd reversed a traveler or missed a connection, so I said, okay, let's troubleshoot. I had her check her work. She checked. Everything looked right to her. I looked over her shoulder, and I saw it immediately: she'd landed one of the travelers on the wrong screw. Easy fix. But instead of just saying, hey, this one goes here, let me show you why, I said something like, you've got to pay closer attention to which terminal you're using, we went over this.

And she just looked down at the switch and nodded. She didn't argue. She didn't push back. She fixed it, and the light worked, and we moved on to cleaning up.

But it sat wrong with me. Because the truth is, she had been paying attention. The problem wasn't her focus. The problem was that I hadn't made it clear enough which traveler goes to which screw in a way that stuck. I assumed that because I'd said it once, she'd have it. I didn't check for understanding. I didn't have her explain it back to me. I just moved forward like the teaching had been good enough, and when the outcome proved otherwise, I put it on her.

That's the opposite of Extreme Ownership. That's me failing as a teacher and then blaming the student.

I didn't recognize it in the moment. I recognized it later that night, sitting in the living room after the kids were in bed, replaying the conversation in my head. And I felt that little twist in my chest, that quiet conviction that I know is the Spirit telling me I missed it. Because the Savior doesn't lead that way. He doesn't wash nine of the apostles' feet and then stop at the tenth and say, you know, you're not really getting this humility thing, work on that. He washes all the feet. He teaches. He corrects gently. He owns the outcome.

And when I screw up as a father, or as a husband, or as a leader in any context, the first thing I want to do is find a reason outside myself. She wasn't focused enough. He didn't follow through. They didn't take it seriously. But Extreme Ownership says no. It says the failure is mine. The gap is mine. And if I want a different result, I don't fix the other person. I fix me.

So the next night, I found my daughter before bed and I said, hey, I need to apologize. I told her I shouldn't have put that missed connection on her. I should have taught it better. I should have slowed down and made sure it clicked before we moved to the actual wiring. And she kind of shrugged and said it was fine, because she's a gracious kid, but I needed to say it. Not for her. For me. Because I needed to own it out loud.

Here's the thing about Extreme Ownership in the context of discipleship: it's not about beating yourself up. It's not about walking around in constant self-flagellation, cataloging every tiny failure and wallowing in it. The Savior didn't atone for our sins so we could carry them anyway. He took them so we could repent, adjust, and move forward.

But repentance requires ownership. You can't repent of something you're still blaming on someone else. You can't change a result you won't take responsibility for.

And I think that's why this principle feels so hard sometimes. Because ownership is uncomfortable. It's vulnerable. It means admitting that the reason my family home evening fell flat last week wasn't because my kids weren't engaged, it was because I didn't prepare, I didn't make it interesting, I didn't create an environment where they wanted to participate. It means admitting that when my wife and I have a miscommunication and something doesn't get done, my first move shouldn't be to point out what she didn't say, it should be to ask what I didn't hear or didn't clarify.

It means looking at the outcomes in my life, the ones I don't like, and asking: what did I do or fail to do that led here? Not what did they do. What did I do.

That's the question the Savior asks. Not because He's weak, not because He's shifting blame onto Himself unfairly, but because He knows that leadership, real leadership, starts with the leader. It starts with me.

In Doctrine and Covenants section four, it says, "O ye that embark in the service of God, see that ye serve him with all your heart, might, mind and strength, that ye may stand blameless before God at the last day." Blameless. Not sinless, because none of us get there on our own. But blameless in the sense that we're not pointing at someone else when we stand before the Lord. We're owning what was ours to own. We're saying, this is what I was given, this is what I did with it, I take responsibility for the outcome.

That's Extreme Ownership in a discipleship frame. It's standing before God and saying, I could have done better. I should have led better. I should have loved better. I should have taught better. And then, because of the Atonement, because of grace, being able to say: and I'm trying again.

There's a story in third Nephi, after the Savior appears to the Nephites, where He calls the twelve disciples and He gives them authority and responsibility. And then later, in fourth Nephi, you see the results. For two hundred years, because of how those twelve led, there are no contentions, no divisions, no poor among them. The society works. But it works because the leaders at every level took ownership of their stewardship. They didn't blame the people for being hard to teach or hard to unify. They led. They taught. They served. They owned the outcome.

And when it eventually fell apart, generations later, it wasn't because the people were uniquely wicked. It was because the leaders stopped leading that way. They stopped owning it. They started pointing fingers, building divisions, seeking their own gain. You see the decay happen in real time in the text. And it starts with a failure of ownership at the top.

I think about that when I'm leading in any capacity, whether it's my family, or in the Guard, or on a job site. The outcome downstream is always connected to the ownership upstream. If I want my kids to take responsibility for their own actions, I have to model it. If I want my apprentices to own their mistakes and learn from them instead of hiding them, I have to own mine first. If I want the guys in my unit to trust that we'll face problems head-on instead of covering them up, I have to be the one who steps forward and says, that was on me, here's what I'm fixing.

That's the culture Extreme Ownership builds. It's not about blame. It's about trust. It's about showing the people you lead that failure isn't fatal, that mistakes are opportunities, that the standard is honesty and growth, not perfection.

And the only way to build that culture is to live it yourself.

So here's what I'm carrying into tomorrow. There's a situation in my family right now where I've been frustrated with one of my sons. He's been dragging his feet on something I've asked him to do multiple times, and my default has been to get annoyed, to think he's not taking it seriously, to assume the problem is his attitude. But tonight, writing this, I'm asking the harder question: have I made it clear why it matters? Have I given him the tools and the time and the structure to actually succeed at it? Or have I just issued the order and expected compliance?

I think I know the answer. And I think the answer is that I need to own the outcome I'm seeing, which means I need to change my leadership, not his attitude.

So tomorrow, I'm going to sit down with him and restart the conversation. Not as a lecture. As a leader who's realizing he didn't lead well the first time.

That's Extreme Ownership. It's not complicated. It's just hard. Because it requires setting down the excuses, the justifications, the very reasonable explanations for why it's not really your fault. And it requires picking up the weight and saying, it's mine. I own it. Now I'm going to fix it.

The Savior did that in a garden two thousand years ago. He's still doing it every time we bring Him our broken things and He says, I'll take that. He owns what we can't carry. And in return, He asks us to own what we can. To lead the people He's given us with the same principle He modeled. No blame. No excuses. Just ownership, and then action.
