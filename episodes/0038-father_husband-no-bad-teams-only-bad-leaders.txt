I was standing in the kitchen about an hour ago, drying off the last of the dinner dishes, and my oldest came through looking for something in the pantry. He didn't say a word to me. Just walked past, grabbed what he wanted, and walked back out. And I thought, man, when did we stop talking? When did he start treating this house like a hotel?

And then I caught myself mid-thought, because that's exactly the wrong question. The right question is, when did I stop making time to actually talk to him? When did I start being too tired or too distracted to ask him about his day in a way that actually invited him to open up?

That's what tonight's about. The principle is called No Bad Teams, Only Bad Leaders. It's from chapter two of the book Extreme Ownership by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin. The core idea is simple and uncomfortable: when a team is struggling, underperforming, or falling apart, that's not fundamentally a personnel problem. It's a leadership problem. The team's performance is a direct reflection of the leader's standards, attitude, and example. If the team isn't performing, the leader hasn't led well enough.

And tonight I want to bring that principle home, literally, to the place where it's hardest to apply and easiest to dodge: marriage and fatherhood. Because I can own failure at work. I can own it in uniform. But at home? Man, it is so much easier to blame the team.

Let me tie this to following Jesus for a minute, because it's the foundation under everything else. Jesus didn't lead by blaming. He led by serving. He led by example. He washed feet. He carried a cross. When Peter denied him three times, Jesus didn't write him off — he restored him, he gave him responsibility, he called him to feed the sheep. Jesus looked at broken, flawed, inconsistent people and said, follow me, and then he went first.

And for a husband and a father, that's the model. Paul writes, husbands, love your wives as Christ loved the church and gave himself up for her. He doesn't say, love your wife if she's acting right. He doesn't say, lead your kids when they're behaving. He says give yourself up. Lead by going first. And that's the exact opposite of what I default to when I'm tired or frustrated or convinced I'm right — which is to stand back, point out what everyone else is doing wrong, and wait for them to get with the program.

Extreme Ownership at home means if my wife and I are disconnected, that's on me. If my kids are fighting all the time, that's on me. If the tone in this house is harsh or distant or chaotic, that's on me. Not because I'm the only one who ever makes a mistake — my wife is her own person, my kids are responsible for their own choices — but because I'm the leader, and the team's performance reflects the leader's standards and example.

That's a hard pill to swallow, but it's true.

Let me give you a specific example from this past week. Monday night, dinner was a disaster. My two boys were fighting before we even sat down. One of them said something sarcastic, the other one shoved him, my daughter started whining because she didn't like what we were having, and my wife and I were both just trying to get through it. By the time we were clearing plates, nobody had talked about anything that mattered. It felt like we were just… coexisting.

Tuesday was worse. I got home late from a job, walked in the door already annoyed because a material delivery got messed up and I had to scramble to fix it. My wife asked me to help my daughter with her math homework, and I said sure, but I was still half-thinking about the job. My daughter could tell I wasn't really present, so she got frustrated. I got frustrated that she was frustrated. It turned into this tense back-and-forth where I was trying to explain a concept she wasn't getting, and instead of slowing down and meeting her where she was, I just kept repeating the same explanation louder, like volume was going to make it click.

My wife finally stepped in and said, hey, maybe take a break. And I snapped at her. I said something like, I'm trying to help, she's just not paying attention. And my wife looked at me and said, she's nine. You're the adult.

She was right.

Wednesday, same thing. The boys were fighting again. My oldest wanted to use the computer, my younger son said he'd called it first, and it escalated into this whole shouting match. I came out of my room, told them both to knock it off, and sent them to separate corners like I was a referee. And then I went back to what I was doing.

Thursday morning, my wife and I had a disagreement about scheduling. She wanted to move something around so she could take the kids to an activity. I wanted to keep it where it was because it was easier for me. We went back and forth, and I could feel myself digging in, not because I was right, but because I didn't want to be wrong. I didn't want to be the one who had to adjust. And she finally just said, fine, we'll do it your way, and walked off. And I sat there feeling like I'd won something, but knowing I hadn't.

By Thursday night, I was sitting on the couch after everyone had gone to bed, and I realized the whole week had felt like this. Disconnected. Tense. Everyone kind of doing their own thing, bumping into each other, no real unity. And my first thought was, man, what's going on with this family? Why is everyone so on edge?

And then I remembered the principle. No bad teams, only bad leaders.

I didn't want to go there, but I made myself ask the question: what am I modeling? What's my attitude been this week? What standard have I actually set?

And the answer was pretty clear. I've been distracted. I've been impatient. I've been more focused on being right than on being connected. When my daughter needed help with her homework, I treated it like a task to get through instead of a moment to be present with her. When my boys were fighting, I didn't actually lead them toward resolution, I just refereed and sent them away. When my wife needed me to be flexible, I chose my own convenience. When she tried to call me on it, I got defensive.

I wasn't leading. I was managing. I was reacting. I was coexisting.

And the team's performance reflected that. The tone in the house, the disconnection, the constant low-level conflict — that was the fruit of my leadership, or lack of it.

So Friday, I decided to own it. Not in some big dramatic way, but just concretely. I got up early, made breakfast for everyone instead of letting it be a grab-and-go morning. I asked my oldest about something he'd mentioned earlier in the week that I'd half-listened to at the time. I sat down with my daughter and asked her to show me what she'd been working on in school, and I actually listened. I apologized to my wife for being stubborn about the schedule and told her I'd been more focused on being convenient for me than on supporting her.

I didn't fix everything. My boys still fought later that day. My daughter still got frustrated with something. My wife and I still had a moment of tension over something small. But the tone was different. There was a little more warmth. A little more connection. Because I went first.

Here's what No Bad Teams, Only Bad Leaders means at home. It means when my kids are disconnected or fighting or sullen, my first move is not to blame them or to lecture them about how they should be acting. My first move is to ask, what am I modeling? Am I present, or distracted? Am I patient, or impatient? Am I leading them toward connection, or am I just enforcing rules and hoping that produces a family?

It means when my wife and I are distant or tense, my first move is not to catalogue all the ways she's contributed to that. My first move is to ask, have I been serving her? Have I been listening to her? Have I been putting her needs ahead of my own convenience? Or have I been keeping score?

It means when the tone in my home is harsh or chaotic, I don't get to stand outside of that and blame the chaos. I set the tone. If the tone is harsh, it's because I've been harsh. If it's chaotic, it's because I haven't led us toward order.

That doesn't mean my wife and kids don't have responsibility for their own attitudes and actions. They do. But I can't control their responsibility. I can only control mine. And my responsibility as the leader is to go first. To set the standard. To model the attitude I want to see. To own the outcome.

Let me give you another angle on this. Teaching my oldest to change the oil in the truck a few months ago. I was trying to show him how to do it, and I was moving too fast, assuming he was picking up things I hadn't actually explained. He kept asking questions, and I kept getting impatient because in my head it was simple, why wasn't he getting it? And finally he just stopped asking and said, never mind, I'll figure it out later.

And I realized, I just failed to teach him. Not because he's not capable. Not because he doesn't want to learn. But because I didn't slow down. I didn't meet him where he was. I didn't lead him well.

So we started over. I apologized for moving too fast. I explained each step, let him do it, let him ask every question without acting like it was a dumb question. And he got it. And the next time, he did it himself.

The team's performance reflected my leadership. When I led poorly, he checked out. When I led well, he learned.

Same thing with my younger son and chores. For weeks, I'd been frustrated that he wasn't doing them without being reminded. I'd nag him, he'd drag his feet, I'd get annoyed, and nothing changed. And I finally asked myself, have I actually trained him, or have I just told him? Have I set a clear standard and held it consistently, or have I been inconsistent and then blamed him for the inconsistency?

Turned out, I'd been inconsistent. Some weeks I'd enforce it, some weeks I'd let it slide. Some days I'd help him, some days I'd just criticize him for not doing it right. I wasn't leading. I was nagging.

So I sat down with him, we made a simple chart, we agreed on what done looks like, and I told him I'd check it with him every day for two weeks until it became a habit. And I actually did it. I didn't let it slide. I didn't nag, I just checked, and when he did it, I noticed. When he didn't, we did it together and talked about why it mattered.

Two weeks later, he was doing it on his own. Not because he suddenly became a different kid, but because I actually led him instead of just expecting him to figure it out.

No bad teams. Only bad leaders.

Here's what this doesn't mean. It doesn't mean I'm responsible for every bad choice my kids make. It doesn't mean if my wife is having a hard day, that's my fault. It doesn't mean I beat myself up for not being perfect. Nobody's perfect. I fail at this all the time.

What it means is when things aren't going well, my default can't be to blame the team. My default has to be, what do I own here? What can I do differently? How do I go first?

And that's hard, because at home, you're tired. You've been leading all day at work or in uniform. You come home and you just want to coast. You want the team to run itself. You want your wife to be easy and your kids to be compliant and everything to just work without you having to engage.

But that's not how teams work. That's not how families work. The leader sets the tone. The leader goes first. Always.

What standard have I actually set? What's my attitude been? What have I owned, and what have I blamed on everyone else?
