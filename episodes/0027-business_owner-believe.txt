I spent most of today in the truck between job sites. One crew finishing up a restaurant remodel downtown, another roughing in a new build out in the county, and me bouncing between them trying to keep everything on track. And somewhere around the third stop I realized I was annoyed at both crews for basically the same thing — they weren't doing what I would have done if I'd been there start to finish. Which is ridiculous, because I can't be there start to finish anymore, and that's the whole problem.

I've been thinking about this all week. About why some jobs just seem to flow and why others turn into a mess even when the crew's capable, even when the plan was solid. And I keep coming back to one thing. It's not usually skill. It's not even effort most of the time. It's belief. Whether the people doing the work actually understand why we're doing it the way we're doing it, and whether they believe it matters.

Tonight I want to talk about chapter three of Jocko Willink and Leif Babin's book Extreme Ownership. The principle is called Believe. And the core idea is this — you cannot expect people to execute a mission with full commitment unless they truly understand and believe in the reason behind it. Not just what they're supposed to do. Why it matters. Why it's worth doing right. A leader's job isn't just to issue orders. It's to communicate the why clearly enough and deeply enough that the people following you buy in. And if you can't do that, if you don't believe it yourself or you can't articulate it in a way that connects, the mission is already in trouble before anyone picks up a tool.

That's the principle. Let me sit with it for a minute.

Because before I apply this to running the business, I need to say something about following Jesus Christ. This matters to me, and it matters to how I lead. I'm a member of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, and my faith shapes everything I do, even when I'm not talking about it directly. And here's the thing — belief is central to discipleship. Not just intellectual agreement that Jesus is the Christ. Actual belief. The kind that changes how you act when no one's watching. The kind that makes you willing to do hard things because you trust Him.

Jesus didn't just tell the twelve apostles what to do. He spent three years teaching them why. Helping them understand who He was, what the Father's plan was, why it mattered that they go into all the world after He was gone. And even then, even with the Savior Himself teaching them, some of them struggled to believe until after the resurrection. Belief is hard. It requires something from us. It requires us to wrestle with the why until it becomes our own.

And that means as a leader, if I'm asking people to believe in a mission, I better believe it first. Deeply. Honestly. Not just in a surface way where I can repeat the company line. I have to know why we're doing what we're doing well enough that I can help someone else see it. And if I can't do that, if I'm just going through the motions myself, I have no right to expect anyone else to care.

Alright. Let's talk about the business.

So here's the story. About two months ago we landed a commercial job that I was really excited about. A small medical office build-out. Good contract, decent schedule, client who seemed reasonable. The scope was clear. We were doing all the electrical from the panel to the fixtures, plus some low-voltage work for their network and security. Standard stuff, but a nice size. Big enough that I couldn't do it solo. I put one of my lead guys on it with an apprentice, gave them the plans, walked the site with them, and sent them off.

And from the start, it felt off. Little things. They were moving slower than I expected. The apprentice kept texting me questions I thought should've been obvious. My lead guy seemed kind of checked out. I'd stop by to see how it was going and everything looked fine on the surface, but there was no momentum. No energy. They were doing the work, but barely. Just enough to stay on schedule. And I couldn't figure out why.

Then we hit the rough-in inspection. And the inspector flagged three things. None of them were code violations, exactly, but they weren't great. Conduit runs that were technically acceptable but sloppy. A panel layout that met code but was going to be a pain for anyone servicing it later. Junction boxes placed in spots that were legal but inconvenient. The inspector passed us, but he made a comment I'll never forget. He said, "This'll work, but it doesn't look like you guys cared much."

That stung. Because he was right.

I didn't say anything to the crew that day. I just stewed on it. And the more I thought about it, the more I realized the problem wasn't them. It was me. I had told them what to do. I'd given them the plans, the schedule, the scope. But I had never told them why this job mattered. I hadn't explained that this client was someone I was hoping to build a long-term relationship with. I hadn't mentioned that medical offices are some of the best word-of-mouth generators in this town if you do them right. I hadn't talked about how a clean, thoughtful rough-in makes the trim-out easier and makes us look like pros when the client's contractor walks through. I just assumed they'd care because I cared. And that was lazy leadership.

I called a meeting the next morning. Not at the shop. At the job site. I brought coffee, and I sat down with both of them before we started the day. And I owned it. I said, "I need to apologize. I sent you out here without giving you the full picture. You've been doing what I asked, but I didn't tell you why it mattered. And that's on me."

Then I explained it. I told them about the client, about the potential for more work, about how medical clients tend to know other medical clients. I walked them through why the panel layout mattered, why conduit routing wasn't just about meeting code but about making the next guy's job easier. I told them that when an inspector says we didn't care, that reflects on all of us, and it reflects on every job we've ever done. I told them I needed them to take ownership of this job the way I would if I were the one bending pipe. Not because I said so. Because they believed it was worth doing right.

And here's what happened. The job didn't suddenly become perfect. We still had challenges. But the energy changed. My lead guy started catching little things before they became problems. The apprentice stopped texting me every question and started thinking through solutions himself. When we got to trim-out, they installed the fixtures and devices like they were finishing their own house. The client noticed. The general contractor noticed. And when we wrapped, the client asked if we'd be interested in bidding two more locations they were opening next year.

That's belief. That's what happens when people understand the why.

But here's the other side of it. The harder side. Because belief only works if it's real. If the leader actually believes in the mission. And there've been times in this business where I didn't. Where I took a job because I needed the cash flow, or because I didn't want to lose a client, and I knew going in it was going to be a nightmare. And you can't fake belief. People can smell it when you don't care. When you're just trying to get through it.

There was a residential remodel last year. Big house, expensive finishes, client who changed their mind every other day. I bid it too low because I was worried about losing the job to another contractor, and from day one I regretted it. Every change order was a fight. Every decision took three times as long as it should have. And I was miserable. And because I was miserable, my crew was miserable. I'd show up and they'd see it on my face. I didn't believe this job was worth the hassle, and neither did they. We got through it, but barely. And I swore I'd never do that again.

Because here's the thing. Belief starts with the leader. If I don't believe in the mission, I can't ask my crew to. And if I can't believe in it, I shouldn't take the job. That's hard in a small business. There's always pressure to say yes, to keep the revenue coming, to avoid the gap in the schedule. But a job you don't believe in will cost you more than the money. It'll cost you morale. It'll cost you quality. It'll cost you the trust of the people working for you.

So now when I'm looking at a new project, I ask myself — do I believe this is worth doing? Not just can we do it. Not just will it pay the bills. Do I believe in it enough that I can stand in front of my crew and explain why it matters? And if the answer's no, I'm learning to walk away. Or at least to be honest about it. To say, "Look, this one's a grind, but here's why we're doing it anyway." That's not the same as belief in the mission, but at least it's honest. At least it's not pretending.

But when I do believe, when I really believe the work matters, that changes everything. I had a service call a few weeks ago. Just a service call. An older couple, their kitchen outlets kept tripping, they were worried about a fire. Small job, maybe two hours of work. I sent one of my younger apprentices. And before he left I took five minutes to explain something. I said, "These folks are scared. They don't understand what's happening, and they're imagining the worst. Your job isn't just to fix the outlet. It's to give them peace of mind. Explain what you find. Show them it's not dangerous. Leave them feeling like they can trust us." 

He came back three hours later. Turned out it was a loose neutral, easy fix. But he'd spent an extra hour walking them through what he did, checking a few other things while he was there, making sure they felt safe. They called me the next day to say thank you. And my apprentice told me later that was the first service call where he really got why we do this work. Not just the technical side. The human side. He believed it mattered.

That's what I'm after. That's what belief does. It turns a task into a mission. It turns a job into something worth caring about.

Here's what I'm learning to do. When I'm assigning work now, especially if it's something bigger than a quick service call, I take the time to explain the why. Not in some long motivational speech. Just a couple of minutes. This is the client. This is what they need. This is why it matters. This is what success looks like. This is how it fits into the bigger picture. And then I ask — does that make sense? Do you have what you need? Do you believe we can do this right?

And if they don't, if I see hesitation or confusion, I don't just send them anyway. I stop and figure out what's missing. Because sending someone out to do a job they don't believe in is setting them up to fail. And that's on me.

The other thing I'm learning is to check myself. To ask whether I believe in what I'm asking people to do. Because if I don't, I need to either figure out why or admit I'm wrong. There've been jobs where I changed direction halfway through because I realized the plan wasn't going to work and I didn't believe in it anymore. That's uncomfortable. It feels like failure. But it's better than dragging everyone through something I know is broken just because I'm too proud to admit I was wrong.

Belief isn't about certainty. It's not about having all the answers. It's about being honest. About knowing why you're doing what you're doing, and being able to say it out loud in a way that makes sense. And if you can't, you stop and figure it out before you ask someone else to follow you.

So here's what I want you to think about tonight, whether you run a business or you lead in some other way. Do the people who follow you actually understand why you're asking them to do what you're asking them to do? Have you taken the time to explain it? And do you believe in it yourself, deeply enough that you could convince someone else it's worth doing?

If the answer's no, that's not a failure. That's just information. That's a starting point. But you can't skip this. You can't expect people to care if you haven't given them a reason to. And you can't give them a reason if you don't have one yourself.

Belief starts with you. And it spreads from there.
