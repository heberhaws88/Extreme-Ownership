I'm standing in a supply house parking lot right now, waiting for one of my guys to come grab the wire and breakers we need to finish a job that should have been done yesterday. It's the third time this week I've had to run parts because somebody on the crew forgot to check the material list before they left the shop. And I'm frustrated. I'm thinking the thought you're not supposed to think if you've read any leadership books at all — maybe I've just got the wrong guys.

Except here's the problem. I know better. I've read the book. I teach this stuff. And the principle I'm staring at tonight, the one that's making me uncomfortable in this parking lot, is this: there are no bad teams, only bad leaders.

That's chapter two of Extreme Ownership by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin. The core idea is simple and brutal — when a team isn't performing, the problem isn't the team, it's the leader. The standards the team operates at, the attitude they carry, the habits they build, all of that flows downhill from whoever's in charge. If my crew is sloppy, distracted, or underperforming, I need to look in the mirror before I look at them. A struggling team is a leadership problem before it's a personnel problem.

And I hate that. Not because it's wrong, but because it's right.

Let me connect this to following Jesus for a minute, because it sits underneath everything I do, even when tonight's lens is the business. Jesus didn't look at the twelve guys he picked and say, well, I got a bad draw, guess I'll find better disciples. He got a hothead who wanted to fight everyone, a guy who'd sell him out for forty bucks, a tax collector nobody trusted, a bunch of fishermen who kept missing the point — and he led them anyway. He didn't outsource the responsibility. He taught them. He corrected them. He modeled what he wanted them to become. When Peter screwed up, Jesus didn't write him off. He rebuilt him. That's leadership. The standard Jesus sets is this: you don't get to blame the people you're leading for not becoming what you haven't shown them how to be.

That's the part that makes this principle cut so deep. It's not just a management technique. It's a reflection of how God leads us — he doesn't give up when we're slow, stubborn, or forgetful. He keeps teaching. He keeps correcting. He keeps showing us the way. And if I'm trying to follow him, I don't get to do less for the people I'm responsible for.

Alright. Let me tell you about a job that went sideways about six months ago, because this is where the principle stopped being theory and started costing me money.

We bid a small commercial remodel, offices in an older building, nothing fancy. Straightforward rough-in, panel upgrade, new lighting, some data drops. I gave the bid to a crew I've run for a couple years now — a solid journeyman and two apprentices, guys who've done this kind of work before. I figured I didn't need to be on-site much. I had three other jobs running that week, and I spent most of my time bouncing between those and catching up on paperwork and estimates in the evenings. I checked in on the remodel once, maybe twice. Everything looked fine.

Until it wasn't.

We were supposed to be done in eight days. On day nine, I got a call from the general contractor. The drywall guys were held up because we hadn't finished. I called my journeyman. He said they'd hit a few snags, needed another day, maybe two. I wasn't happy, but okay, it happens. Two days later, still not done. Now the GC's mad, the owner's asking questions, and my profit margin on the job is evaporating because I'm paying guys to finish work that should've been buttoned up a week ago.

I finally carved out a morning and went to the site myself. And what I found was a mess. Not dangerous, not code violations, just inefficiency everywhere. Wire pulls that should've taken an hour had taken three because nobody staged the material right. Boxes were placed inconsistently, some too high, some too low, because nobody checked the print closely. The apprentices were standing around half the time waiting for direction. The journeyman was doing good work, but slow, methodical, no sense of urgency. There was no plan for the day. They showed up, looked at what was left, and kind of wandered into it.

I was furious. I wanted to blame them. I wanted to say, how do you not know how to run a job like this? How do you let it drag out like this?

And then I realized — I never told them the deadline mattered. I never walked the scope with them on day one and made sure they understood the sequence. I never established the standard for how this job should flow. I just assumed they'd figure it out, because they're not brand new, and it wasn't a complicated job. I bid it, I assigned it, I walked away. I treated them like they didn't need leadership because the work was simple.

But here's the thing. Every team needs leadership. It doesn't matter if the job is simple. It doesn't matter if they've done it before. If I don't set the standard, somebody else will — maybe the laziest guy on the crew, maybe just the path of least resistance. And that's what happened. I gave them no vision for what success looked like on this job, no clear expectations, no accountability. So they defaulted to showing up and doing work, but not executing a plan.

The problem wasn't them. It was me.

So I stopped being mad at them and started leading. I sat down with the three of them that morning, and I said, here's where we are, here's where we need to be, here's the plan to get there. I walked the remaining scope with the journeyman and we built a task list for the next two days. I clarified who was doing what. I told them the GC was frustrated and the client was asking questions, so we needed to move with purpose. And then I stayed on that site for the rest of the day, not doing the work, but making sure the plan held, answering questions, keeping the momentum up.

We finished the next day. It cost me margin. It cost me time I didn't have. But we finished, and the client was happy, and the GC calmed down. And I learned something I should've already known — you can't lead from a distance and expect excellent results. You can manage from a distance, maybe. You can delegate tasks. But leadership requires presence, clarity, and accountability. And I hadn't given them any of that.

The book talks about this exact thing. Jocko tells the story of the boat crew races in SEAL training, where one boat crew was always winning and another was always losing, and the instructors swapped the leaders. Immediately, the losing crew started winning and the winning crew started losing. Same guys. Different leader. The principle held — it's not about the people, it's about the leadership.

And here's the hard part for me as a business owner. I can't be on every job anymore. The company's grown past that point. I've got four or five jobs running at any given time, plus estimating, plus billing, plus dealing with suppliers and clients and everything else that comes with owning the business. So I have to lead through other people now. I have to set the standard clearly enough that it holds even when I'm not standing there. And that requires a level of intentionality I didn't need when I was just running one crew myself.

It means I can't assume people know what I expect. I have to say it. I have to show it. I have to check that they've got it. It means I can't just assign a job and hope for the best. I need to make sure the leader on that job — usually a journeyman — understands the scope, the timeline, the standard, and has what he needs to execute. And then I need to follow up. Not micromanage, but follow up. Are we on track? Do you need anything? Is there a problem I need to know about?

It also means I have to own it when I set somebody up to fail. If I give a guy a job he's not ready for and don't support him, that's on me. If I don't train my apprentices well and then get frustrated when they're slow, that's on me. If I let a standard slip on one job and then wonder why it's slipping on others, that's on me.

There are no bad teams. Only bad leaders.

Now, does that mean every person is a fit for every role? No. Does it mean you never make personnel changes? Of course not. Sometimes you realize somebody's in the wrong seat, or they're not willing to meet a standard no matter how much support you give them, and you have to make a call. But that decision should come after you've honestly asked yourself — have I led this person well? Have I given them what they need to succeed? Have I been clear, consistent, and present?

Because most of the time, if I'm honest, the answer is no. Most of the time, the gap between where my crew is and where I want them to be is a gap I created by not leading well enough.

Here's another example, smaller but just as telling. A few months ago I noticed one of my apprentices was consistently showing up ten, fifteen minutes late. Not every day, but enough that it was a pattern. And it annoyed me. We start at seven. That's the standard. If you're late, you're holding up the crew.

I was about to sit him down and give him the speech about professionalism and respect for other people's time. And then I realized — I'd been late to jobs myself twice that month. Not by much, ten minutes here, twenty minutes there, but late. I'd been letting myself slide because I'm busy, I've got a lot to juggle, and if I show up a little behind, well, I'm the owner, I've got reasons.

Except my apprentice doesn't see the reasons. He sees the standard. And the standard he sees is that showing up late is tolerable.

So before I talked to him, I fixed my own problem. I started showing up early again, every time, no excuses. And then I had the conversation with him, but from a different posture — not as the guy who's annoyed that his employee's late, but as the leader who's responsible for setting a standard and holding it. I told him, hey, we start at seven, that's non-negotiable, I need you here on time. And I told him I'd been letting myself slide and I wasn't doing that anymore, because the standard applies to me first.

He hasn't been late since.

That's the principle in the small stuff. If I want my guys to care about the details, I have to care about the details. If I want them to show up on time, I show up on time. If I want them to clean up the job site at the end of the day, I'd better not be the guy leaving a mess. The standard I set isn't what I say, it's what I do. And if my team's not meeting the standard, I need to ask what I'm modeling that's producing that.

I think about my kids sometimes in this same frame. My oldest is starting to push back on some of the household expectations we've set, and my first instinct is to get frustrated with him. Why is this so hard? Why do I have to remind you every single time?

And then I think — what am I modeling? Am I keeping the standards I'm asking him to keep? Am I consistent? Am I present? Or am I just assigning expectations and getting annoyed when they're not met?

It's the same principle. There are no bad teams, only bad leaders. It applies at home just as much as it applies in the shop or on the job site.

So here's what I'm carrying into tomorrow, and here's what I'm asking you to carry with you too.

If there's a place where your team, your crew, your family, whoever you're responsible for, is underperforming — ask yourself this: what am I modeling that's producing that? Not what are they doing wrong. What am I doing, or not doing, that's created the conditions for this to happen?

And then ask: have I been clear? Have I been consistent? Have I been present? Have I set a standard and held it, or have I assumed people would just figure it out?

Because most of the time, the gap isn't a people problem. It's a leadership problem. And the only person who can fix a leadership problem is the leader.
