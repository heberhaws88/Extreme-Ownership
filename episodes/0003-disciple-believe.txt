I was sitting in a meeting at church a few weeks ago, and the conversation had turned — as it often does — to missionary work. Someone asked the question, and I've heard it a hundred times, why is it so hard to get members excited about sharing the gospel? And someone else said, well, people are busy, they're uncomfortable, they don't know what to say. And all of that's true enough. But I sat there thinking, no, that's not it. Or at least, it's not all of it. The real reason is simpler and harder than that. Most of us don't actually believe it matters.

Not in the way we'd say we do if someone asked us point-blank. We believe it in theory. We believe it as a doctrine. But we don't believe it the way you believe something when your house is on fire and you start grabbing your kids. And if we don't believe it — really believe it — then we're never going to act like it matters, no matter how many talks we hear or goals we set.

Tonight's principle from Extreme Ownership is Believe. And the core idea is this — people will not fully commit to a mission until they understand and believe in the reason behind it. A leader must know the why deeply enough to convince others of it. Jocko Willink and Leif Babin lay this out in chapter three of the book, and they're talking about combat, about getting a SEAL platoon to execute a mission even when it's dangerous and complicated and exhausting. But the principle runs deeper than tactics. It runs all the way down to the foundation of leadership itself. If you don't believe in the mission, your people won't either. And if they don't believe in it, they won't give it everything they have.

And here's the thing — you can't fake it. You can fake a lot of things as a leader. You can fake confidence for a while. You can fake energy. But you cannot fake belief. People can tell. They can hear it in your voice, see it in your eyes, feel it in the way you prioritize or don't prioritize what you're asking them to do. Belief is the bedrock. Without it, everything else is just performance.

Now, before I get into the story I want to tell tonight, I need to take a minute here, because this principle connects directly to following Jesus Christ, and not just in the obvious way. Yes, Christ gave us a mission — go into all the world, teach all nations, bring souls unto Him. And yes, we're supposed to believe in that mission. But I think the deeper connection is this — Christ didn't just tell us what to do. He showed us why it mattered by doing it Himself. He took ownership of a mission that wasn't His fault. He went into Gethsemane and took on the weight of every sin, every sorrow, every broken thing in the history and future of the world, and He did it because He believed — not just intellectually, but with everything He had — that it was the only way to bring us home.

That's the ultimate picture of belief driving action. He didn't just know the doctrine of the Atonement. He enacted it. And because He believed it mattered, He did the hardest thing anyone has ever done.

And then, after all of that, after the cross and the tomb and the resurrection, what does He do? He appears to His apostles. He shows them His hands and His side. He eats fish with them. He walks with them on the road to Emmaus. He teaches them again, patiently, carefully, making sure they understand the why. Because He knew they were about to go out and lead a church that would be persecuted and scattered and tested in every possible way, and if they didn't believe — really believe — they'd never hold together.

There's a verse in the Doctrine and Covenants, section four, verse five, where the Lord lists the qualities a servant in His kingdom needs — faith, hope, charity, love. And then it says, with an eye single to the glory of God. An eye single. Focused. Undivided. That's belief. That's not just agreeing with a statement of doctrine. That's orienting your whole life around the truth of it.

And I think that's where I keep running into trouble. Not because I don't know the doctrine. I know it. I've taught it. I've borne testimony of it. But knowing it and believing it with an eye single to it are two different things. And the moments when I fail as a leader — at home, at church, anywhere — are almost always the moments when I've lost that belief somewhere along the way, or maybe never really had it in the first place.

So here's the story. A few months ago I was asked to help organize a service project for our ward. Nothing huge, just a Saturday morning thing, cleaning up around an elderly sister's property, doing some yard work, fixing a few things around her house. Standard stuff. And I said yes, because that's what you do when you're asked. I coordinated with a few other guys, sent out some messages, set a time, lined up tools and a trailer. Did all the logistical things you're supposed to do.

And the day came, and we had maybe five people show up. Five. Out of a ward of a hundred and fifty, a hundred and sixty active adults. Five. And I was frustrated. I was irritated. I spent the morning working alongside these other guys, and we got the work done, but the whole time I was stewing about it. Why didn't more people show up? I sent the messages. I made it easy. I gave them plenty of notice. What's wrong with everyone?

And then, driving home afterward, still annoyed, it hit me. I didn't believe in it either.

I don't mean I thought it was a bad idea to help this sister. Of course it was a good thing to do. But I didn't believe it mattered in the way that makes you move heaven and earth to be there. I treated it like an item on a list. I organized it because I was asked to organize it, not because I was on fire with the conviction that serving her was an urgent, sacred, necessary thing. And because I didn't believe it that way, I didn't communicate it that way. I sent logistical messages. I told people when and where. I didn't tell them why. I didn't paint the picture. I didn't help them see her — this woman who's been in the ward for thirty years, who's served in every calling they've ever given her, who's alone now, whose health is failing, who needed help and couldn't ask for it herself. I didn't help them see that serving her was serving Christ. I didn't help them feel what I should have felt.

And the reason I didn't do any of that is because I didn't feel it myself. I was going through the motions. I was checking a box. And you can't lead people to believe in something you don't believe in. It doesn't work. They'll do what I did — they'll show up if it's convenient, if nothing better comes along, if they remember. But they won't prioritize it. They won't rearrange their Saturday for it. Because I didn't give them a reason to.

That's on me. That's not on the people who didn't show up. That's on me for not believing deeply enough to make them believe.

And the hard part is, I can't go back and fix that one. It's done. The project happened. The work got done. The sister was grateful. But I missed the opportunity to lead those people into something that mattered, because I didn't see it as something that mattered myself. I saw it as a task.

Here's what I've been wrestling with since then. How much of my discipleship is like that? How much of what I do as a follower of Jesus Christ is just task completion? I go to church — check. I pay tithing — check. I do my home teaching, or ministering, or whatever we're calling it now — check. I read my scriptures — most days, check. But do I believe? Do I believe the way Christ believed when He sweat blood in the garden? Do I believe the way Peter believed when he left his nets and followed a man he'd just met? Do I believe the way Joseph Smith believed when he walked into a grove of trees as a fourteen-year-old boy and asked a question that would cost him his life?

I don't. Not most of the time. Most of the time I believe in a comfortable, manageable, fit-it-into-my-schedule kind of way. And that's not the belief that changes the world. That's not the belief that builds Zion. That's not the belief that brings souls unto Christ.

And I think — I think this is the thing I'm still working through — I think the reason I struggle with this is because I haven't done the work to understand the why deeply enough. I know the gospel is true. I've had experiences that confirm it. But I haven't spent enough time sitting with the weight of what that means. I haven't let it wreck me. I haven't let it reorder everything. I've kept it in a box labeled Sunday, or labeled church stuff, and I've let the rest of my life run on different fuel.

And you can't lead from that place. You can manage from that place. You can organize. You can execute tasks. But you can't lead. Because leadership requires belief, and belief requires wrestling with the why until it's not just an idea in your head but a fire in your chest.

There's a moment in Third Nephi, after Christ appears to the Nephites, where He invites them to come forward one by one and feel the prints of the nails in His hands and feet and the wound in His side. And it says they did go forth, and did thrust their hands into His side, and did feel the prints of the nails. And they knew of a surety that it was He. That's belief. That's not secondhand. That's not borrowed. That's belief that comes from contact with the real thing.

And I think that's what I'm after. I don't want a borrowed testimony. I don't want a hand-me-down belief system that I maintain out of habit or obligation. I want to know Him. I want to feel the prints of the nails. I want to believe in the mission — His mission, the one He gave us — the way you believe something when you've seen it with your own eyes and touched it with your own hands.

Because here's the thing. If I believe that way, then I can lead that way. If I believe the gospel is the answer to every problem the world has — and I mean really believe it, not just agree with it — then I can help other people see it too. I can talk about it without embarrassment. I can invite without hesitation. I can organize a service project and communicate the why in a way that makes people want to drop everything and show up. Because it won't just be a project to me. It'll be a chance to do what Christ did. To serve. To lift. To love. To take ownership of someone else's burden because that's what He did for us.

But if I don't believe it, if I'm just maintaining a cultural identity or going through religious motions, then nothing I do will have that kind of weight. And the people I'm supposed to lead — my kids, my wife, the people in my ward, the guys I work with, the soldiers I serve alongside — they'll see right through it. And they should. Because leadership without belief is just noise.

So here's what I'm carrying into tomorrow. I'm asking myself this question, and I'm asking it honestly — do I actually believe the mission I'm supposed to be on? Not do I think it's a good idea. Not do I agree with it. Do I believe it? Do I believe it enough to reorder my priorities around it? Do I believe it enough to have hard conversations? Do I believe it enough to spend my time and my energy and my resources on it even when it costs me something?

And if the answer is no, or if the answer is I'm not sure, then I need to stop pretending and start doing the work to find out. I need to go back to the scriptures. I need to go back to prayer. I need to go back to the temple. I need to sit with the why until I understand it, until I feel it, until it's not just a doctrine I know but a truth I've staked my life on.

Because if I can get there — if I can believe the way Christ believed, the way Joseph believed, the way Peter and Paul and Nephi and Moroni believed — then maybe I can lead the way they led. Maybe I can help the people around me see what I see. Maybe I can be the kind of leader who doesn't just issue instructions but inspires belief.

That's the work. That's the standard. And I've got a long way to go.

But I know this much. The mission is real. Christ is real. The gospel is real. And if I can hold onto that, if I can let that truth dig down deep enough into my life that it changes the way I see everything, then maybe — maybe — I can become the kind of leader He needs me to be.
