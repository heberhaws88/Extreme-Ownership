I got the call on a Thursday afternoon. Weekend drill was coming up and one of the other Warrant Officers, a guy who handles maintenance, had a family emergency. Could I step in and run the Saturday morning briefing for a project we'd been assigned — a site survey and prep work for a field water-purification setup at a Guard training facility about ninety miles south. I said sure, no problem. I'd been read in on the tasking already. Seemed straightforward.

I showed up Saturday morning with the slides somebody else had built, read through them once in the truck on the drive in, and stood up in front of about twenty soldiers — mix of NCOs, junior enlisted, a couple of other Warrants — and started walking through the plan. We'd convoy down Sunday morning, do the survey, mark locations for the purification units, coordinate with the facility staff, be back by eighteen hundred hours. Simple. I hit all the logistical points. Convoy order, timeline, safety brief, who's responsible for what equipment. I asked if there were questions. A couple hands went up, mostly clarifications on load-out. I answered them. Dismissed everyone to prep their gear.

And I could feel it in the room. Nobody was actually with me. They were compliant, sure. They'd show up Sunday morning, do what I told them to do, check the boxes. But there was no energy, no buy-in. It felt like I was handing out homework nobody wanted to do.

One of the senior NCOs, a Staff Sergeant who's been in the unit longer than I have, caught me afterward by the Humvees. He didn't say it directly, but he didn't have to. He just looked at me and said, "So, uh, sir, why are we doing this again? I mean I know we got tasked, but what's the actual point?" And I realized I didn't have a good answer. I gave him some vague response about supporting readiness and maintaining relationships with the facility staff. He nodded politely and walked off. I stood there feeling like an idiot.

Because I didn't believe in the mission myself. Or more accurately, I hadn't taken the time to understand it well enough to believe in it. I'd been handed a task, accepted it, and tried to lead it without ever asking why it mattered.

Tonight's principle is Believe. It's chapter three in Extreme Ownership, by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin. The core idea is this: people will not fully commit to a mission until they understand and believe in the reason behind it. And it's the leader's job to know the why deeply enough to communicate it, to convince others of it, and if the leader can't do that — if the leader doesn't believe in the mission himself — the team will sense it immediately and their commitment will be shallow at best.

You can order people to do things. You can rely on discipline and chain of command to get compliance. But compliance is not the same thing as commitment. And when things get hard, when the mission gets complicated or uncomfortable or tedious, compliance evaporates. Commitment holds. But commitment requires belief, and belief requires understanding.

Before I go any further, I need to say this. I follow Jesus Christ. I'm a member of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. And when I think about belief, about truly believing in a mission, I can't separate that from what it means to be a disciple. Because discipleship is fundamentally about belief. Not just intellectual agreement, not just nodding along to doctrine, but deep, soul-level belief that changes how you act.

Jesus didn't ask people to comply. He asked them to follow. To leave their nets, leave their tax booths, leave their old lives, and follow him. And the people who did that, the ones who really committed, were the ones who came to believe not just that he was a good teacher, but that he was the Son of God, that his mission was their mission, that the kingdom he was building was worth everything they had.

When I think about belief in the Gospel, I think about Peter stepping out of the boat. He didn't do that because Jesus ordered him to. He did it because he believed, in that moment, that Jesus could hold him up. And when his belief wavered, when he stopped focusing on the why and started focusing on the waves, he started to sink. The mission didn't change. The Savior didn't change. Peter's belief did.

And here's the thing — belief isn't just passive. It's not enough to quietly hold a testimony in your heart and never articulate it. If you're a leader, if people are following you, you have to be able to communicate what you believe and why you believe it. You have to be able to say, "Here's why this matters. Here's why this mission is worth the cost." That's true in the Guard, it's true in business, it's true in a family, and it's absolutely true in the Church. If I can't explain to my kids why we live the way we live, why we keep the commandments, why we go to church even when it's inconvenient, they're not going to commit. They'll comply for a while, maybe, out of obedience or habit. But eventually they'll drift. Because compliance isn't enough.

So back to that weekend drill. Sunday morning came and we convoyed south. Everyone did their job. The survey got done. We marked the locations, coordinated with the facility, convoyed back, and filed the paperwork. Mission complete. But it felt hollow. I knew it, the NCOs knew it, probably half the junior enlisted knew it. We'd gone through the motions.

About a week later I was still chewing on it. So I did what I should have done in the first place. I went back to the tasking order, read the whole thing, not just the logistical bullet points. I called the officer who'd originally coordinated the project, asked him what the bigger picture was. Turns out this wasn't just some random site survey. The training facility was being set up to host a multi-state exercise in about six months — a large-scale disaster-response scenario involving Guard units from three states, local emergency management, FEMA coordination, the whole nine yards. The water-purification setup we'd surveyed wasn't a standalone project. It was a critical piece of infrastructure for that exercise. If we didn't get it right, if the units and locations weren't properly coordinated, the whole exercise could be compromised. And that exercise, in turn, was designed to validate our ability to respond to a real-world disaster in our region. Flooding, earthquake, whatever. This was rehearsal for saving lives.

Nobody had told me that. Or maybe somebody had mentioned it in passing and I hadn't paid attention. Either way, I didn't know it, so I couldn't communicate it. And because I couldn't communicate it, my soldiers didn't know it either. They thought they were doing busywork. They were actually doing something that mattered.

I brought it up at the next drill. Didn't make a big speech, didn't call everyone together for a formal AAR. I just found a moment when most of the same group was in the motor pool prepping vehicles for an upcoming convoy, and I said, "Hey, I owe you guys an explanation about that site survey we did a couple weeks ago." And I told them what I'd learned. I explained the exercise, the multi-state coordination, the disaster-response validation, why getting the infrastructure right on this mattered. I told them I should have known that before we rolled out, that I'd failed to do my homework, and that I was sorry I'd wasted their time making them do something they didn't understand.

The Staff Sergeant, the same one who'd asked me why we were doing it in the first place, grinned and said, "Well, sir, that makes a hell of a lot more sense." A couple of the younger guys nodded. One of them said, "Yeah, I was wondering why we were driving three hours round trip to put stakes in the ground." I said, "Because those stakes are going to keep a field hospital from running out of clean water when there's fifteen hundred people on site. That's why."

The tone shifted. Not dramatically, not like a movie moment. But enough. Because now they knew. And when the follow-up tasking came through a month later — additional coordination, some equipment pre-positioning, more tedious logistical work — those same soldiers showed up with a different attitude. They understood the mission now. They believed it mattered. And so they committed to doing it right.

Here's the thing about being a Warrant Officer. Especially as an engineer. Your job is to be the technical expert. You're the guy who knows how the equipment works, how to solve the complex problems, how to keep systems running when everything's going sideways. And a lot of Warrants, myself included sometimes, get so focused on the technical side that we forget we're still leaders. We forget that people are watching us, taking cues from us, and if we're just executing tasks without understanding or communicating the why, we're failing them.

You can be the best engineer in the unit. You can know every system inside and out, troubleshoot anything, build anything. But if the soldiers working under you don't understand why the work matters, if they don't believe in the mission, they're going to do the minimum required and nothing more. And when the mission gets hard, when the timeline compresses or the equipment fails or the conditions get miserable, they're going to fold. Because they were never really committed in the first place.

And here's the uncomfortable truth — if you don't believe in the mission yourself, you can't fake it. Your people will know. They'll see it in your body language, hear it in the way you brief, feel it in the way you lead. You can say all the right words, hit all the talking points, but if you don't actually believe what you're saying, it won't land. Belief is not a performance. It's a posture. And people can sense the difference.

So what do you do when you're given a mission you don't understand? When you're handed a tasking that seems pointless, or you're told to execute something that doesn't make sense to you? You don't just shrug and pass it down the chain. You go find the why. You ask questions. You dig into the bigger picture. You go up the chain of command if you have to and say, "I need to understand the strategic intent here, because I need to be able to explain this to my soldiers in a way that makes them want to do it, not just comply with doing it."

And if you do that, if you really dig, and you still can't find a why that makes sense, if the mission truly is pointless or poorly conceived, then you have a different responsibility. You have to push back. Respectfully, professionally, but firmly. You have to say, "Sir, I don't think this mission is sound, and here's why." That's extreme ownership too. Because sometimes the best way to lead your people is to protect them from a bad mission. But you can't know whether a mission is bad or just misunderstood until you've done the work to find out.

In my case, the mission was good. I just hadn't done my homework. I'd accepted the tasking at face value, assumed it was what it looked like on the surface, and led my soldiers into something I didn't believe in because I didn't understand it. That's on me. Not on the guy who handed me the slides, not on the officer who assigned the tasking, not on the soldiers who showed up and did what I told them. On me.

The other piece of this is that belief isn't static. You don't just come to believe in a mission once and then you're set. You have to maintain it. You have to keep connecting your people back to the why, especially when the mission drags on or gets repetitive or stops feeling urgent. Because belief erodes under friction. Tedium kills it. Frustration kills it. If you're not constantly reinforcing the why, constantly reminding people what they're working toward and why it matters, they'll drift back into compliance mode. And compliance is fragile.

I think about this with my own guys in the unit. We do a lot of training that feels repetitive. Convoy operations, equipment maintenance, comms checks, rehearsals. Same stuff, different month. And it's easy for soldiers to start thinking, "Why are we doing this again? We just did this last drill. We did this six months ago. We know how to do this." And if I just bark orders and run them through the paces without ever stopping to say, "Here's why we're rehearsing this again, here's what we're refining, here's what we're getting better at, here's what happens if we don't," they'll go through the motions and learn nothing.

But if I take two minutes to say, "Look, we're running this convoy op again because the last time we did it, our comms discipline was sloppy and if this had been real-world, we'd have lost situational awareness and put people at risk. So today we're focused on fixing that. This isn't busywork. This is us getting better at keeping each other alive," suddenly it's not just another drill. It's a mission with a purpose. And people commit to that.

So here's what I'm asking you to think about tonight, whether you're in the Guard or not. Who are you leading? Maybe it's soldiers, maybe it's employees, maybe it's your kids, maybe it's just yourself. Whoever it is, do they understand why they're doing what they're doing? Or are they just complying because you told them to?

And more importantly, do you believe in the mission yourself? Can you articulate why it matters? Can you convince someone else that it's worth their time, their effort, their commitment? If you can't, if you're just going through the motions, expecting other people to follow you on blind faith, you're not leading. You're managing. And management gets you compliance. Leadership gets you commitment.

So tomorrow, pick one thing you're asking someone to do. Could be a task at work, a responsibility at home, a standard you're holding someone to. And ask yourself, do they know why this matters? Have I explained the why, or have I just given the what? And if you haven't explained it, explain it. If you don't know the why yourself, go find it. Do the homework. Dig into the bigger picture. And then communicate it. Clearly, honestly, and with conviction.

Because people will not commit to a mission they don't believe in. And they can't believe in a mission they don't understand. And if you're the leader, it's your job to make them understand.
