I was sitting in sacrament meeting a few months ago, and the speaker stood up and said something I will never forget. He said, I want to talk to you tonight about five keys to developing faith. And then he proceeded to give us seven bullet points under each of those five keys. Thirty-five things to remember. Thirty-five action items to take home and implement in our spiritual lives. And I sat there with my notebook, dutifully writing it all down, and about halfway through I realized I couldn't remember what key number two even was. I looked over at my wife. She had stopped writing. She was just listening politely. My oldest, sitting next to me, was folding the program into an airplane.

By the time the closing hymn started, I could maybe recall three of the thirty-five points. Maybe. And I remember thinking, this brother means well, he prepared hard, he cares about us — but I'm walking out of here with nothing I can actually use.

That's the thing about complexity. It doesn't just fail to help. It actively gets in the way.

Tonight I want to talk about chapter six of Extreme Ownership by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin. The principle is called Simple. And the core idea is this — plans, orders, instructions, guidance, all of it has to be ruthlessly simplified down to what is essential and easily grasped, because when things get hard, when the pressure is on, when people are tired or scared or distracted, complexity becomes the enemy. Confusion kills. And the leader's job is to cut through the noise and make the mission so clear that even someone exhausted and overwhelmed can understand it and execute it.

Willink and Babin came to this the hard way in Ramadi. They watched missions fall apart not because the strategy was wrong, but because the plan was too complicated. Too many contingencies, too many signals, too many moving parts that nobody could keep straight once the shooting started. The SEAL teams that won were the ones that could explain the entire concept of the operation in a couple of sentences. Get in, hit this building, pull this target, get out. Everything else was stripped away.

And here's what I've been wrestling with the last few weeks. I think we do this to ourselves all the time as disciples of Jesus Christ. We complicate what He made simple. We add layers and requirements and subclauses to the gospel until people can't see it anymore. And I don't mean that we should dumb down doctrine or pretend the mysteries aren't there. I mean we lose people — we lose ourselves — when we can't answer the basic question, what does the Lord want from me right now, in a way that's clear enough to actually do.

Because the Savior Himself was a master of simplicity. When the lawyer stood up and tried to trap Him with a complicated question about the law, Jesus gave him two commandments. Love God. Love your neighbor. That's it. All the law and the prophets hang on these two. He didn't give him seventeen principles and a five-point action plan. He gave him the mission.

When He called the apostles, He didn't hand them a three-ring binder full of standard operating procedures. He said, follow me. Two words. And they did.

When He taught the multitudes on the mount, He gave them the Beatitudes. Blessed are the poor in spirit. Blessed are they that mourn. Blessed are the meek. Simple declarations. Easy to remember. Hard to live, yes, but not hard to understand.

And in the Book of Mormon, when the people are tangled up in arguments and losing their way, again and again a prophet stands up and brings it back to the simplest truth. Nephi says, we talk of Christ, we rejoice in Christ, we preach of Christ, we prophesy of Christ. One focus. Christ.

King Benjamin gathers his people and gives them a sermon that changes their hearts, and at the end of it, what does he tell them to remember? Always retain in remembrance the greatness of God and your own nothingness and His goodness and long-suffering towards you. Remember who He is, remember who you are, and let that shape everything. That's the whole plan.

The Lord doesn't deal in clutter. He deals in clarity. And I think that's because He knows us. He knows we are dust. He knows we get tired and distracted and overwhelmed. He knows that under pressure, we fall back on what we can remember, and if we can't remember it, we won't do it.

So why do I keep making it complicated?

Here's the story I've been sitting with. A little over a year ago, my wife and I were struggling. We've got three kids, I've got the business, I've got Guard obligations, she's managing the home and her own work, and we were missing each other. Passing like ships. Going through the motions. I could feel the distance growing, and I knew it, and I cared about it, but I didn't know what to do about it. So I did what I always do when I don't know what to do. I made a plan.

I sat down one night after the kids were in bed and I wrote out this whole thing. I was going to fix our marriage. I had it all mapped out. Weekly date nights, daily check-ins, a shared calendar so we could sync our schedules better, a list of conversation starters so we wouldn't just talk about logistics, time blocked out every morning for scripture study together before the kids woke up, a monthly review where we'd assess how we were doing on all these fronts. I was so proud of this plan. It was thorough. It was detailed. It was airtight.

I presented it to her the next evening. I walked her through the whole thing. And she just looked at me. And then she said, babe, I don't need a project plan. I just need you to be here when you're here.

And I felt like an idiot. Because she was right. I had taken something simple — love your wife, be present with her, pay attention to her — and I'd turned it into a tactical operation with five lines of effort and measurable outcomes. I'd made it so complicated that it wasn't even about her anymore. It was about me executing a system.

The mission was simple. Love her. Be with her. Listen to her. I didn't need seventeen action items. I needed to do the one thing.

We ended up scrapping the whole plan. We kept one piece. Before bed, no phones, ten minutes, we just talk. That's it. No agenda. No list. Just connection. And it's been better. Not perfect, but better. Because it's simple enough that even on the hard days, even when I'm fried from a job that went sideways or a drill weekend that ran long, I can still do it. I can still show up for ten minutes and be present.

I think about that when I read Doctrine and Covenants section four. It's one of the great missionary sections, and it's short. The Lord lays out what He's looking for. Faith, hope, charity, love, an eye single to the glory of God. Remember faith, virtue, knowledge, temperance, patience, brotherly kindness, godliness, charity, humility, diligence. Ask and ye shall receive, knock and it shall be opened. That's the mission. It's clear. It's not buried under a hundred qualifications and footnotes. It's right there.

But here's where I fail. I read that, and then I build this massive scaffolding around it. I start adding requirements. I tell myself, okay, faith means I have to read for thirty minutes every day and I have to pray morning and night and I have to do family scripture study and I have to magnify my calling and I have to do my ministering and I have to attend the temple once a month and I have to, I have to, I have to. And then when I can't keep up with all of that, when I miss a day or drop a ball, I feel like I've failed the whole thing. I feel like I'm not measuring up as a disciple. And the irony is, I've lost sight of the actual mission. The mission wasn't to execute a perfect checklist. The mission was to have faith. To develop charity. To serve with an eye single to His glory.

The Savior doesn't need me to be complicated. He needs me to be faithful.

And I see this in the way He led. Gethsemane is the ultimate example of extreme ownership — He took on everything, all of it, the weight of sin and suffering that wasn't His, because that was the mission and He owned it completely. But before He went there, He made sure the mission was clear. He spent the Last Supper preparing the apostles. He didn't give them a contingency matrix. He told them, love one another as I have loved you. He washed their feet and showed them what that love looks like. He gave them the sacrament and told them, remember me. Simple. Profound. But simple.

And then, even in the garden, even in that moment of unimaginable weight, His prayer was simple. Not my will, but thine. Six words. The whole plan of salvation in six words.

I'm not saying discipleship is easy. It's the hardest thing there is. But hard doesn't mean complicated. And I think sometimes we hide behind complexity because it feels safer. If I've got a thirty-five-point plan, I can stay busy. I can check boxes. I can avoid the deeper, simpler, scarier question, which is, am I actually doing the one thing He asked me to do?

What is that one thing right now for you? Not the whole list. Not everything you think you should be doing or everything someone else is doing or everything the handbook says is available. What is the one thing the Lord is asking of you tonight?

For me, right now, it's this. Be present with my family. That's it. I can dress it up a hundred different ways, but that's the mission. When I'm home, be home. When my daughter wants to show me something, stop and look at her. When my boys are talking, listen. When my wife needs me, be there. I don't need a system for that. I just need to do it.

And in my walk with Christ, the mission right now is simpler than I want it to be. It's repent. Daily. Hourly. Come to Him with what I've screwed up, let Him heal it, and try again. I keep wanting to build this elaborate plan for spiritual growth, and He keeps bringing me back to the same simple invitation. Come unto me. Trust me. Follow me.

I was talking to one of my apprentices a while back. He's a good kid, trying to figure out his life. He was asking me about the Church, about what we believe, and I started to go into this whole explanation of the plan of salvation, premortal life, the creation, the fall, the atonement, the restoration, and I could see his eyes glazing over. So I stopped. And I said, look, here's the simplest version. God is your Father. He loves you. He sent His Son to save you because you can't save yourself. And He's asking you to come home. That's it. Everything else is detail.

And the kid said, okay, I can understand that.

That's what simple does. It lets people in. Complexity keeps people out.

So here's what I'm taking into tomorrow, and here's what I'm asking you to think about too. What's one thing you've made too complicated? Maybe it's your relationship with the Savior. Maybe it's your calling. Maybe it's how you're trying to serve your family or your neighbor. What would it look like to strip it down to the mission? To the one clear, essential thing He's asking of you right now?

And if you had to explain that mission in one sentence to someone who's tired and distracted and barely holding on — because let's be honest, that's all of us some days — could you do it? Could you make it simple enough that they could actually hear it and hold onto it?

Because that's what He does for us. He makes it simple. Not easy. But simple. Come unto me. Take my yoke upon you. Learn of me. Love one another. Remember me. Do this in remembrance of me.

The mission is clear. We're the ones who clutter it up.

I'm trying to do less of that. I'm trying to own the fact that when my plans are too complicated, when my explanations are too convoluted, when I can't boil it down to something someone could actually execute under pressure — that's on me. That's my failure as a leader, as a disciple, as a husband and father. The Lord has given me the simple truth. My job is to live it and share it without burying it under a pile of good intentions and overcomplicated systems.

So tonight, before you go to bed, ask yourself, what's the mission? The real mission. And is it simple enough that you can actually do it tomorrow when tomorrow gets hard?
