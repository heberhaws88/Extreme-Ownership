I pulled into the church parking lot early one Sunday morning last month, sat there in the truck with the engine off, and realized I'd been avoiding a conversation for three weeks. 

One of the young men in our ward, a kid I'd known since he was twelve, had come to me after mutual one night and said he was struggling, that he didn't know if he believed anymore, that he didn't want to go on a mission but didn't know how to tell his parents. He asked if we could talk. I said of course, anytime, and then I did nothing. I told myself I was waiting for the right moment, for clarity on what to say, for some kind of spiritual download that would give me the perfect words. What I was really doing was stalling because I was afraid of saying the wrong thing.

Three weeks. The kid stopped making eye contact with me. And I sat in that truck knowing that my indecision, my waiting for certainty, had already made the decision for me. He'd heard my silence as an answer.

Tonight I want to talk about decisiveness amid uncertainty. It's chapter eleven in Extreme Ownership by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin, and the core idea is this: leaders almost never have complete information. You can wait for perfect clarity, but that waiting is itself a decision, and it's usually the wrong one. A leader gathers what information is available, makes the call, and then adjusts as reality unfolds. Indecision doesn't protect you from making the wrong choice. It just guarantees you make it too late.

Now, here's where this cuts deep for me as a disciple of Jesus Christ. The Savior is the greatest example of leadership we have, and if you look at His mortal ministry, He moved with this remarkable decisiveness even when the path ahead was uncertain, even when He knew the cost would be staggering. Think about Gethsemane. He knew in broad strokes what was coming, but the actual weight of it, the reality of taking on every sin, every pain, every injustice that ever was or ever would be? He couldn't have known the full measure until He was in it. And yet He didn't wait. He didn't stall. He moved forward into the most agonizing decision in all of human history because that's what the Father asked, and because people like me were depending on it.

And before that, all through His ministry, He made calls without complete information. He called twelve apostles, knowing one would betray Him, knowing another would deny Him, knowing all of them would scatter when it got hard. He could have waited, could have vetted them more, could have built in safeguards. But He called them anyway, and then He worked with what unfolded. He healed on the Sabbath knowing it would provoke the Pharisees. He taught in parables knowing most people wouldn't get it. He touched lepers, ate with sinners, elevated women and children in a culture that dismissed them, all without polling the crowd first to see if it would play well. 

Following Jesus Christ means learning to move forward in faith when you don't have all the answers. It means making decisions that align with what He's taught and what the Spirit confirms, and then trusting that He'll make up the difference as you go. Indecision, on the other hand, is often just fear dressed up as prudence. And I know that because I've done it a hundred times.

So back to that kid. I finally had the conversation, but it was late, and awkward, and I could feel the distance my delay had created. I didn't have perfect words. I told him I didn't have perfect words. I said I was sorry for making him wait, that I should have reached out sooner, and that I didn't know how to fix his doubts but I cared about him and I wanted to walk through it with him however I could. We talked for an hour. It wasn't some movie moment where everything resolved beautifully. He's still figuring it out. His parents still don't know. But at least he knows I'm not avoiding him anymore. At least the door's open.

Here's what I realized afterward. I'd been waiting for certainty about what to say, but the decision wasn't really about having the right words. The decision was whether to show up or not. And by waiting, I'd already made the call. I'd chosen absence. The Savior didn't wait for Peter to have his testimony fully sorted before He called him. He called him, walked with him, let him fail, and then called him again after the resurrection. The decisiveness was in the engagement, not in having a foolproof plan.

There's a verse in Ether, chapter twelve, verse six, that's been rattling around in my head since this whole thing. It says, "I would show unto the world that faith is things which are hoped for and not seen; wherefore, dispute not because ye see not, for ye receive no witness until after the trial of your faith." That's Moroni, quoting the Lord. And what strikes me about it is the sequence. You don't get the witness first. You don't get certainty and then act. You act in faith, and the confirmation comes after. Indecision is basically a refusal to let that sequence play out. It's a demand that God give you the witness before the trial, which is not how any of this works.

I see this same pattern in Doctrine and Covenants, section nine, where Oliver Cowdery wants to translate but he's not doing it right. The Lord tells him, "You must study it out in your mind; then you must ask me if it be right, and if it is right I will cause that your bosom shall burn within you; therefore, you shall feel that it is right." Study it out first. Make the best call you can with what you have. Then ask. The Spirit confirms or redirects, but you have to move first. Oliver's mistake, at least in part, was waiting for God to do the deciding for him. That's not how the Lord leads. He expects us to engage, to think, to choose, and then He works with what we bring Him.

Now here's the tricky part. Decisiveness doesn't mean recklessness. It doesn't mean ignoring counsel or plowing ahead without prayer. In the Guard we call it the OODA loop: Observe, Orient, Decide, Act. You gather intel, you orient yourself to the situation, you decide, and then you act. But the whole loop has to keep moving. If you get stuck in the observe phase, you've stopped leading. And in the kingdom, it's the same. You pray, you study, you counsel with people you trust, you seek the Spirit, and then you make the call. And if it's wrong, you own it and adjust. But you don't just freeze.

I was talking to my wife about this a few nights ago. We've been going back and forth for months about whether to make a change in our family scripture study. The kids are getting older, and the current approach isn't really working anymore, but we kept second-guessing ourselves. Should we do something different? What if we mess it up? What if we go too hard and they resent it, or too soft and they don't take it seriously? We had all these reasonable concerns, and we kept tabling the conversation, waiting for some clear answer to drop out of the sky.

Finally my wife said, "We're not actually waiting for an answer. We're just afraid of being wrong." And she was right. We were treating this decision like it was some irreversible life-or-death call, when really it was just a course correction we could adjust as we went. So we decided. We're trying something new starting this week. Maybe it'll work, maybe it won't, but at least we're moving again. And honestly, the relief I felt just making the call was evidence enough that we'd been stuck too long.

Here's what I think happens when we stall as disciples. We tell ourselves we're waiting on the Lord, but sometimes we're really just afraid to take responsibility for the outcome. If I don't decide, then I can't be wrong. If I wait long enough, maybe someone else will decide for me, or the situation will resolve itself, or the pressure will go away. But that's not faith. That's abdication. And the Savior didn't model abdication. He modeled decisive, forward-moving trust in the Father, even when the immediate future was unclear.

Think about Nephi. His father has this vision, gets the family to leave Jerusalem, and then sends his sons back to get the brass plates. Nephi doesn't have a detailed plan when he says, "I will go and do the things which the Lord hath commanded, for I know that the Lord giveth no commandments unto the children of men, save he shall prepare a way for them that they may accomplish the thing which he commandeth them." That's first Nephi, chapter three, verse seven. He's not saying, "I have total clarity on how this will work." He's saying, "I know God will provide a way, so I'm moving forward now." And then he tries multiple approaches. He doesn't get it right the first time. Laban refuses. The second attempt almost gets them killed. The third time, Nephi finds Laban drunk in the street and the Spirit prompts him to kill him, which is about the last thing Nephi expected. The point is, Nephi stayed decisive. He kept acting, kept adjusting, kept trusting that the Lord would make up the difference.

Indecision, on the other hand, is a kind of paralysis. And paralysis in leadership is catastrophic because it spreads. If I won't make the call, the people looking to me for direction are stuck too. In the church context, that means my kids, the people I home teach, the callings I hold. If I model indecision, I'm teaching them that discipleship is about waiting for perfect clarity before you act. And that's not the gospel. The gospel is about moving forward in faith, making covenants, repenting when you miss, and trusting that the Atonement covers the gap between your best effort and what's actually required.

There's a story in third Nephi that I come back to a lot. After the Savior appears to the Nephites, He calls disciples and gives them authority to baptize. And then people immediately start arguing about how to do it, what words to use, all this contention. So the Savior appears again and clears it up. He gives them the exact words, the exact process. But here's what strikes me: He doesn't rebuke them for acting in the first place. He corrects the method, but the fact that they were out there trying to do the work? That wasn't the problem. The problem was the contention, the refusal to unify once the direction was given. But they were decisive enough to start, and He worked with that.

I think sometimes we're so afraid of doing it wrong that we don't do it at all. And the Savior's example is, do it, and I'll help you course correct. Just don't stand still.

So here's where it lands practically for me. There's a decision I've been avoiding at work, about whether to take on a commercial project that's bigger than anything we've done before. It's a good opportunity, but it would stretch us, require hiring more people, probably mean longer hours for a while. I've been waiting to feel sure about it. Waiting for the risk to feel smaller. But the risk isn't going to get smaller, and the opportunity's not going to wait forever. I need to either commit or pass, and then adjust as I go. Indecision is costing me more than a wrong call would.

And there's another one, closer to home. My oldest has been asking about some things, some questions about the church, about what we believe and why. And I've been giving him surface answers, waiting for him to be older, waiting for I don't know what. But he's asking now. And if I keep stalling, the message I send is that his questions don't matter or that I don't trust him with real answers. I need to engage. I need to decide that he's ready for deeper conversations, even if I don't have perfect answers, and trust that the Spirit will guide us both as we go.

The Savior didn't wait for His apostles to be ready. He made them ready by calling them and then walking with them through the uncertainty. That's the model. That's the leadership. And it requires decisiveness.

So here's what I'd ask you to carry into tomorrow. What decision have you been stalling on, telling yourself you're waiting for clarity, when really you're just afraid of owning the outcome? Where has your indecision already made the call for you? And what would it look like to move forward in faith, to make the best choice you can with what you know, and trust that the Lord will make up the difference as you go?

Because He will. That's the promise. Not that you won't mess up. Not that the path will be clear. But that He'll walk it with you. And that's enough.
