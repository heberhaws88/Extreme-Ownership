I was standing in the motor pool last drill weekend watching three separate fires burn at once, and I mean that almost literally. One of my NCOs had just called me over because a generator we were supposed to have online for training wasn't starting. At the same time, my phone was buzzing because the officer I answer to needed a brief on our equipment readiness that was now overdue. And about twenty feet away, two soldiers were standing next to a trailer full of engineer equipment arguing about whether we'd loaded the right configuration for the next day's mission. All of this happening at seven in the morning, and we were supposed to roll out for a training exercise in less than three hours.

My first instinct was to try to handle all of it at once. Answer the text while walking toward the generator while yelling over my shoulder about the trailer. I actually started doing exactly that. I made it about ten steps before I stopped myself, because I realized I was about to do the one thing that guarantees nothing gets solved. I was about to split my attention three ways and handle none of it well.

Tonight I want to talk about prioritize and execute. It's chapter seven in Extreme Ownership, written by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin, and the core idea is this: when multiple problems hit you at the same time, you can't solve them all simultaneously. You have to identify the single highest priority, communicate that clearly, execute on it, then move to the next problem. Trying to fight on every front at once just means you lose on every front at once. It sounds simple. It's the hardest thing to actually do when the pressure's on.

The book lays this out from a SEAL platoon perspective, where the chaos is bullets and objectives and split-second calls. In the Guard, especially as an engineer, the chaos usually looks different. It's equipment deadlines and readiness reports and whether we've got the right gear staged for a mission. But the principle works exactly the same. When everything's on fire, you don't grab every hose. You identify the one fire that's actually going to burn the house down, and you put that out first.

Before I get into what happened that morning at the motor pool, I want to take a minute on what this looks like as someone trying to follow Jesus Christ, because that's the foundation underneath all of this for me. Prioritize and execute isn't just a tactical principle. It's a spiritual one. Christ taught pretty clearly that you can't serve two masters. You'll love one and hate the other, or you'll be devoted to one and despise the other. That's not just about money. It's about attention. It's about what you actually put first when the moment comes and you have to choose.

There's a moment in the Gospels where Martha is running around trying to get everything done at once, frustrated that her sister Mary isn't helping, and she complains to Jesus about it. And Jesus tells her, Mary has chosen the better part, the one thing that's needful. He doesn't say Martha's wrong for serving. He says she's worried and troubled about many things when there's one thing that actually matters most. That's prioritize and execute in a first-century kitchen. Identify what's actually most important right now. Do that. Let the rest wait.

That principle runs all the way through scripture. When a lawyer asks Christ what the greatest commandment is, Jesus doesn't give him a list. He gives him one: love God with everything you are. Then he adds a second that's like it: love your neighbor as yourself. He doesn't say those are two equal priorities fighting for attention. He says the second one flows out of the first. Get the highest priority right, and it shapes everything else.

I find this harder to live out than I want to admit. My natural mode when things get chaotic is to try to keep every plate spinning. I'll try to be fully present with my kids while also checking work messages while also planning what I need to prep for drill while also feeling guilty that I'm not doing any of those things well. And the result is I end up scattered and ineffective across all of it. What I've had to learn, slowly, is that if I actually stop and ask what the most important thing is right now, the answer is usually pretty clear. It's just uncomfortable, because it means letting other things wait, and that feels like failure. But it's not. It's leadership. Of myself, first, and then of whatever situation I'm in.

Alright. Back to the motor pool, seven in the morning, three problems, not enough of me to go around.

Here's what I did. I stopped walking. I took about five seconds, which felt like an eternity with all the noise and movement around me, and I asked myself the question: what is the highest priority right now? Not what's loudest. Not what's going to make someone annoyed if I don't handle it immediately. What actually matters most for the mission and the people I'm responsible for?

The readiness brief was overdue, but it wasn't due until end of day. The officer I answer to needed it, but he didn't need it in the next three hours. That could wait.

The two soldiers arguing about the trailer configuration, that felt urgent because it was right in front of me and it was loud. But when I actually thought about it, we had time to sort that out before we rolled. It wasn't going to break the training mission if it took another hour to settle.

The generator, though. That generator was the lynchpin. We needed it online for the training exercise we were running the next day. If it didn't work, the whole exercise got compromised. And more than that, the NCO who'd called me over was a junior guy, relatively new in his role, and if I didn't handle this with him now, he wasn't going to know how to troubleshoot it himself next time. That was the highest priority. Fix the generator, train the NCO in the process.

So I walked over to the NCO, ignored my phone, and told the two soldiers by the trailer I'd be with them in twenty minutes. I didn't explain. I didn't apologize. I just told them to hold what they had and I'd be there shortly. Then I focused entirely on the generator.

Except here's the part where I have to own what I did wrong first, because this isn't a story about me getting it right out of the gate. The reason that NCO had called me over in the first place was because I hadn't set him up for success earlier. We'd talked about the generator two drills ago. I'd told him it needed a full preventive maintenance check before this exercise, and I'd told him to let me know if he ran into anything he couldn't handle. What I didn't do was follow up. I didn't check in with him between drills. I didn't ask him during the previous day's battle assembly whether he'd actually done the PM or if he'd hit any snags. I assumed he had it handled, and I moved on to other things.

So when he called me over that morning and the generator wouldn't start, it wasn't just a mechanical problem. It was a leadership problem. Mine. I hadn't prioritized his development two weeks ago when I should have. I'd let it slide because I was busy with other tasks that felt more urgent at the time, and now we were up against a deadline with a piece of equipment that wasn't ready and a soldier who didn't have the confidence to troubleshoot it alone.

I squatted down next to him by the generator and I owned that. I didn't make a big speech about it. I just said, this is on me, I should have checked in with you before now, let's figure this out together. And then we went to work. Fuel, air, spark. We started with the basics. Turned out the fuel line had a clog. Not complicated, but if you've never diagnosed a small engine problem before, it's not obvious where to start. I walked him through it. Didn't do it for him. Walked him through it. Took about forty minutes, which was longer than if I'd just fixed it myself, but that wasn't the point. The point was the next time this happens, he knows what to do.

While we were working, my phone buzzed twice more. I ignored it. One of the soldiers from the trailer argument walked over and started to ask me a question. I held up a hand, not rudely, just clearly, and I said, I'll be with you in fifteen minutes. He walked away. That felt hard in the moment. It felt like I was being unresponsive. But here's what I've learned: if you let every new problem pull you off the highest priority, you never actually finish anything. You just move from crisis to crisis and nothing gets solved.

We got the generator running. I made sure the NCO understood what we'd done and why. Then I moved to the next priority, which was the trailer configuration. Turned out the argument wasn't really about the equipment at all. It was about two soldiers who didn't have clear guidance on what the mission actually required, because the task hadn't been briefed well. That was my fault too, indirectly. I'd delegated the packing list to a sergeant, but I hadn't made sure he understood the intent behind it. So the soldiers were guessing, and when people guess, they argue. I clarified the requirement, we reconfigured the load, it took twenty minutes, done.

Then I sent the readiness brief. Took me ten minutes to compile because I'd been keeping the data updated as we went. Got it in before lunch. The officer I answer to didn't even mention that it was late, because from his perspective, it wasn't. It was in when he needed it.

All three problems got solved. But they didn't get solved simultaneously. They got solved in order. And the order mattered. If I'd answered the text first, or jumped into the trailer argument first, the generator would still be sitting there dead and we'd have been scrambling the next morning. But because I identified the actual highest priority and stayed on it until it was done, everything else fell into place.

Here's what I had to fight against in myself the whole time: the feeling that I was letting people down by not responding immediately to every demand. The officer waiting on the brief. The soldiers by the trailer. The phone buzzing in my pocket. All of that created pressure to split my focus, to try to make everyone happy at once. And if I'd given in to that pressure, I would have made no one happy and solved nothing.

Prioritize and execute doesn't mean you ignore everything except the top priority forever. It means you stay on the top priority until it's handled or until the situation changes enough that something else becomes more critical. Then you shift. But you shift decisively. You don't halfway finish one thing and then halfway start another and then circle back. You finish, then you move.

The other thing I had to fight was my own ego about being the guy who can handle everything at once. I'm a Warrant Officer. I'm supposed to be the technical expert. I'm supposed to know how to manage complex problems. And there's a part of me that wants to prove I can juggle it all, that I don't need to slow down and prioritize because I'm competent enough to just keep all the balls in the air. That's pride. And pride gets people hurt. In a combat zone, it gets people killed. In a training environment, it breaks trust and wastes time and teaches junior soldiers that chaos is normal and acceptable. It's not.

What I should have done, two weeks earlier, was prioritize that NCO's training over whatever else I thought was urgent that day. If I'd spent thirty minutes with him then, walking through the PM checklist and making sure he understood the equipment, we wouldn't have been troubleshooting a dead generator under time pressure the morning of the mission. But I didn't. I let other things crowd it out. And that's on me.

The principle applies whether you're running a platoon in combat or managing a motor pool on a drill weekend or trying to get your household through a Tuesday night. When multiple problems hit at once, you can't fight them all. You have to step back, even if it's just for five seconds, and ask: what is the highest priority right now? What's the one thing that, if I don't handle it, everything else falls apart? Then you communicate that priority clearly to everyone involved, you execute on it, and you don't let yourself get pulled off it until it's done or the situation changes. Then you move to the next one.

It's simple. It's not easy. Especially when people are waiting on you and expectations are stacking up and your phone won't stop buzzing. The pressure to try to do everything at once is enormous. But trying to do everything at once is how you guarantee you do nothing well.

So here's what I'd ask you to carry into tomorrow: when multiple demands hit you at the same time, and they will, stop for five seconds. Not five minutes. Five seconds. And ask yourself, what is the actual highest priority right now? Not what's loudest. Not what makes someone else happy fastest. What actually matters most for the mission and the people you're responsible for? Name it. Say it out loud if you have to. Then put everything you've got into that one thing until it's handled. Let the other stuff wait. It'll still be there. And if you've handled the highest priority first, you'll be in a better position to solve the rest.

Prioritize and execute. It's the difference between leading through chaos and just surviving it.
