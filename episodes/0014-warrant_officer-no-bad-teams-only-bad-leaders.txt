You know what's funny about being a Warrant Officer? Most people outside the military have no idea what that even means. We're not quite enlisted, not quite a regular commissioned officer — we're technical experts who also happen to wear brass. The Army promotes us because we know our craft inside and out, whether that's helicopters or intelligence or, in my case, engineering. But here's the thing they don't put in the promotion packet: knowing your job cold doesn't automatically mean you know how to lead the people doing that job. And if you're not careful, you can hide behind the technical expert label and never take ownership of the team's performance. You can tell yourself the problem is the team, not you.

I learned that the hard way about eighteen months ago, and it's what I want to talk about tonight.

The principle is from chapter two of Extreme Ownership by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin, and it's as blunt as it gets: No Bad Teams, Only Bad Leaders. The core idea is this — when a team is struggling, when performance is subpar, when morale is low, when the mission isn't getting done right, the first place you look is not at the team. You look at the leader. Because a team's performance is a direct reflection of its leader's standards, attitude, and example. If the team isn't performing, it's a leadership problem before it's a personnel problem. Always.

That's hard to hear. It's a lot easier to point at the guy who keeps showing up late, or the soldier who can't seem to get her equipment squared away, or the NCO who doesn't follow through — and say, see, that's the problem, right there. And sometimes those things are real issues. But the principle says you don't get to stop there. You have to ask: what am I doing, or not doing, as the leader that's allowing this to continue? What standard am I actually enforcing? What example am I setting? What have I failed to communicate, or train, or inspect, or correct?

It's ownership at the most uncomfortable level, because it means you can't pass the buck.

Before I get into the story, I need to say something about what this principle looks like through the lens of following Jesus Christ, because that's the foundation under everything else I do, including how I lead. Jesus is the perfect example of a leader who took full ownership — not just for his own actions, but for the failures and sins of everyone he leads. That's what the Atonement is. He didn't stand at a distance and say, well, you people really messed this up, good luck with that. He stepped in. He took it on himself. He owned it completely, even though he was the only one in the equation who did nothing wrong. 

That's the standard. I'm never going to lead like Christ leads, not perfectly, but that's what I'm aiming at. And one of the things that means in practice is that when the people I'm responsible for aren't where they need to be, my first move is to look inward, not outward. It means I don't get to write people off. It means I take responsibility, the same way Christ took responsibility for me when I didn't deserve it and couldn't earn it. If he can own my failures, I can own the failures of the team I've been given to lead. That's the heart of it.

Alright. The story.

So about eighteen months ago, my unit was tasked with a training exercise that involved setting up a forward operating site — nothing classified, nothing complicated in theory, just the kind of engineer work we're supposed to be able to do in our sleep. Establish a perimeter, set up some hardstand, run power, get comms up, make it functional for a battalion headquarters to operate out of for seventy-two hours. Standard stuff. And I was the officer in charge of the engineer detachment doing the work.

We had a week to prep and then three days to execute on site. My team was eight soldiers — a mix of junior enlisted, a couple of specialists, one sergeant who was pretty solid, and one junior NCO, a corporal, who I'll just say was not my favorite person to work with. This corporal had been in the unit about a year, and in that time he'd developed a reputation. He was lazy. He cut corners. He had an excuse for everything. Other soldiers complained about him. The NCOs above me had already talked to him more than once, and nothing seemed to stick. When I found out he was on my team for this exercise, my first thought was, great, now I have to babysit this guy on top of everything else.

I didn't say that out loud, but I thought it. And I'm pretty sure my attitude showed.

We hit the prep week and it was clear pretty fast that we were behind. Equipment wasn't staged right, the load plan for the trucks was sloppy, nobody seemed to know what the priority of work was once we got on site. I was frustrated. I was busy trying to coordinate with the battalion staff, making sure we had the right grid coordinates, the right materials, the right timeline — doing the Warrant Officer technical-expert thing — and every time I turned around, something on the team level was screwed up. And every time something was screwed up, it seemed like that corporal was somewhere in the middle of it.

By the time we rolled out to the site, I was already writing the whole team off in my head. I had decided this was just a bad draw. Weak team, weak NCO, and I was going to have to drag them across the finish line and then write a very honest after-action report about who the problem was.

Day one on site was a disaster. We were supposed to have the hardstand laid and the power runs started by end of day. We got maybe half of that done. Soldiers were standing around waiting for guidance. Equipment wasn't where it needed to be. The corporal and another soldier got into an argument about who was supposed to be doing what, and I had to step in and break it up. By nightfall, I was furious. I called the team together and gave them a very direct talking-to about how unacceptable this was, how we were behind schedule, how they needed to get it together and execute.

And then I went back to my tent and sat there in the dark, stewing. I was angry at them. I was angry at the corporal especially. I was angry at the situation.

But somewhere in that stewing, a thought crept in that I didn't want to hear. It was a question, and the question was this: what did you actually do to set them up for success?

I didn't want to answer that question. Because the answer was not much.

I had been so focused on the technical side — on coordinating with higher, on making sure the plans were right, on being the subject-matter expert — that I had barely led the team at all. I hadn't walked them through the priority of work in detail. I hadn't made sure everyone understood their specific roles. I hadn't inspected the prep work during the week; I'd just assumed the NCOs would handle it. I hadn't set a clear standard and then enforced it. And when I found out that corporal was on the team, I had already written him off in my mind. I didn't give him clear guidance, I didn't invest any time in him, I just avoided him and expected him to fail.

And guess what? He did. They all did. Because I set them up to fail.

That was the No Bad Teams, Only Bad Leaders moment. Sitting in that tent, I had to own it. This wasn't a bad team. This was bad leadership. My leadership.

The next morning, I got up early and I changed my approach. I called the sergeant and the corporal aside before we kicked off and I told them straight: yesterday was on me. I didn't give you what you needed to succeed. Today's going to be different. Then I walked the entire team through the day's priority of work, step by step. I made sure every single soldier knew exactly what their job was, who they were working with, and what the standard was. I put the corporal in charge of one specific task — setting up the generator tie-ins — and I gave him clear instructions and then I told him I trusted him to own it. I didn't hover, but I checked in. I inspected. I made myself available.

And I stayed with the team. I wasn't off in a planning tent somewhere, I was on the site, working alongside them, visible, engaged, setting the pace.

The difference was night and day. 

Day two, we caught up. Day three, we finished ahead of schedule. The team worked together, they moved with purpose, and yeah, that corporal I had written off? He actually did a solid job with the generators. Not perfect, but solid. And more than that, his attitude shifted. When he saw that I was invested, that I was leading and not just managing from a distance, he stepped up.

At the end of the exercise, the sergeant pulled me aside and said, "Sir, I don't know what you did, but that was a completely different team than day one."

What I did was lead. That's all. I took ownership.

Here's the thing, and this is what I want you to hear tonight, whether you're in the military or not: it is so easy to blame the team. It is so easy to point at the weakest link and say, that's the problem, if only we didn't have that guy, if only we had better people, then we'd be fine. And sometimes, yeah, there are people who genuinely aren't a fit, who need to be moved or removed. That's real. But nine times out of ten, before you get to that point, the issue is leadership. The issue is you.

If your team isn't performing, the first question is not what's wrong with them. It's what am I doing, or not doing, that's producing this result?

Are you setting a clear standard? Are you actually enforcing it, or are you letting things slide and then getting frustrated when performance drops? Are you communicating expectations clearly, or are you assuming people just know? Are you present, engaged, leading by example, or are you managing from a distance and then blaming them when they don't execute? Are you investing in the people who are struggling, or have you written them off?

And here's the other side of it: what's your attitude? Because your attitude as the leader sets the tone for the whole team. If you're checked out, they'll check out. If you're negative, they'll be negative. If you're making excuses, they'll make excuses. If you've decided in your head that this is a bad team, they'll sense it, and they'll live down to that expectation.

But if you take ownership, if you lead with standards and presence and investment, most of the time the team will rise.

Not always. Not every time. Leadership isn't magic. But most of the time, when you actually lead well, you'll be shocked at what the team can do.

I think about that corporal sometimes. If I had kept the attitude I started with, I would've written him off completely, and I would've missed what he was actually capable of when someone led him well. I would've blamed him for a failure that was mine. And I would've learned nothing.

No bad teams, only bad leaders. It's a hard principle, but it's true.

So here's what I want you to do. Think about a team you lead, or a group you're responsible for, whether that's soldiers or employees or a crew or your own family. Is there an area where they're not performing the way you want? Is there someone you've written off as the problem?

Before you do anything else, ask yourself: what am I modeling? What standard am I actually holding? What have I failed to communicate, or train, or inspect, or correct? What is my attitude communicating to them?

And then ask: what does taking ownership look like here? Not just accepting blame in some vague way, but actually changing how I lead. What do I need to do differently tomorrow?

That's the work. That's where ownership lives.
