You know how sometimes you catch yourself in the middle of a Sunday morning scramble, trying to get the kids fed and dressed and to church on time, and your wife reminds you about the lesson you volunteered to teach in Gospel Doctrine in about forty-five minutes that you haven't actually prepared yet, and you realize you never followed up with the family you're assigned to minister to this month like you said you would, and somewhere in all of that you're also mentally cataloging the three talks you need to give feedback on because you're helping coordinate something in the ward, and none of it is actually getting done because you're just bouncing between all of it in your head like a pinball?

Yeah. That was me two weeks ago.

And the truth I had to own that morning was this: I was failing at all of it precisely because I was trying to do all of it at once.

Tonight's principle is Prioritize and Execute. It's chapter seven in Extreme Ownership by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin, and the core idea is simple to say and brutally hard to do. When multiple problems hit you at once, your job as a leader is to step back, identify the single highest priority, execute on that until it's solved or stable, then move to the next one. Not try to juggle everything simultaneously. Not let the loudest problem dictate what you work on. Relentlessly prioritize, then execute, then reprioritize.

The reason this is hard is because our instinct when chaos hits is to try to do everything at once. We feel the pressure of all the problems at the same time, so we spread ourselves thin across all of them, which means nothing actually gets solved. We're reacting instead of leading. And in a firefight or a business crisis or a family emergency, that's how people get hurt, how missions fail, how everything falls apart.

The book talks about this in the context of combat, where a SEAL team might be taking fire from multiple directions, dealing with a casualty, trying to call for extract, all at the same time. If the leader tries to manage all of that simultaneously, the team dies. The leader has to say: the highest priority right now is suppressing that machine gun. Everything else waits. Once that's handled, we move the casualty. Then we call for extract. One thing at a time, in order of what will kill us first if we don't handle it.

That same principle applies when you're trying to follow Jesus Christ.

Because here's the thing. Discipleship is not a static checklist. It's not like you reach some level of faithfulness where everything is handled and you just coast. If you're actually trying to follow the Savior, you're constantly dealing with multiple pulls on your time, your energy, your heart. There's always another commandment to live better, another person to serve, another weakness to overcome, another calling to magnify. And if you're not careful, the sheer volume of it can paralyze you. You end up doing nothing well because you're trying to do everything at once.

I think about the Savior's example here. Jesus had every reason to be overwhelmed by competing demands. Everywhere He went, people wanted something from Him. Healing, teaching, miracles, answers. The disciples wanted His attention. The Pharisees wanted to trap Him. The crowds wanted to make Him king. His own family didn't always understand what He was doing. And underneath all of that, He was carrying the weight of an infinite Atonement that none of us can fully comprehend.

But when you read the Gospels, you don't see Jesus scattered. You don't see Him reacting to whoever yells loudest. You see a man who knew exactly what His mission was, and who prioritized ruthlessly in service of that mission. 

There's a moment in Mark chapter one where Jesus has been healing people all evening in Capernaum, and early the next morning He goes off alone to pray. The disciples come find Him and say, essentially, Everyone's looking for You, there are more people to heal, we need to get back there. And Jesus says, No. Let's go to the next towns. That's why I came, to preach the gospel. He doesn't let the urgent crowd dictate His priority. He knows what the highest priority is, and He executes on that.

Or think about Gethsemane. The highest priority in that moment, the thing that everything else in human history depended on, was Him staying in that garden and finishing what He came to do. Not escaping. Not calling down legions of angels. Not even comforting the disciples who kept falling asleep when He asked them to watch with Him. Just: stay. Submit. Drink the cup. The weight of every other priority, every other possible course of action, had to fall away so that one thing could be accomplished.

That's Prioritize and Execute at the most profound level. The Savior owned the mission, identified the highest priority, and executed, even when it cost Him everything.

For me, that Sunday morning two weeks ago, the chaos started the night before. I'd come home late from a Guard drill weekend, and I was fried. My wife had been solo with the kids all weekend, which meant she was fried too. I walked in the door and immediately got hit with everything I'd missed: our oldest had a rough game and wanted to talk about it, our daughter needed help with a project that was due Monday, the youngest had been asking for me all day. My wife needed me to be present, needed me to step back into the family rhythm I'd been absent from for two days. 

And I also knew I had that Gospel Doctrine lesson in the morning. I'd agreed to cover for a friend who was out of town, and I hadn't done any prep because the drill weekend had been more intense than I expected. I figured I'd throw something together Saturday night. Except Saturday night I was exhausted and I told myself I'd get up early Sunday and handle it then.

So Sunday morning comes. I'm up early, sitting at the kitchen table with my scriptures and a manual and a notepad, trying to put together something coherent, and that's when my wife comes out and reminds me about the ministering family we're assigned to, the ones going through a hard time right now, the ones I said I'd check in with this week and didn't. And then I remember I told another brother in the ward I'd give him feedback on some youth talks by Sunday. And the kids are starting to wake up and somebody needs breakfast and my wife is looking at me like, Are you really going to sit there with your nose in a manual right now when your family needs you?

And she's right. But also the lesson is in two hours and I've got nothing. And also that ministering family really does need a call. And also I did promise that feedback. And I'm sitting there trying to figure out how to do all of it at once, and what actually happens is I do none of it well.

I half-prepare a lesson while distractedly talking to my oldest about his game. I throw together some breakfast while mentally drafting what I'm going to say to the ministering family. I snap at my daughter when she asks me for help with her project because I'm trying to focus on three other things. I text the brother about the talks and say I'll get him the feedback later, which we both know means I'm not going to do it. I show up to church frazzled, teach a disjointed lesson I'm not proud of, sit through sacrament meeting barely present because I'm stewing about all the things I didn't do.

I failed that morning. Not because I didn't care. Not because I wasn't trying. I failed because I tried to do everything at once, which meant I led nothing.

Here's what I should have done. I should have stopped, taken a breath, and asked: what is the single highest priority right here, right now? 

And if I'm honest, the highest priority was not the lesson. It wasn't the ministering call. It wasn't the feedback on those talks. The highest priority was my family. My wife had just carried the whole load solo for two days. My kids needed their dad back. That was the most important thing in front of me, and I owed it to them to execute on that first.

The lesson? I should have called my friend Saturday and told him I couldn't cover it. Or I should have taught something simple and short and from the heart instead of trying to fake my way through a prep I didn't have time for. The ministering family? I should have sent a text Saturday night saying, I'm thinking of you, I'll call you Monday. That's not perfect, but it's honest and it keeps the relationship intact. The feedback? I should have told that brother up front, I can't get to this by Sunday, let's push it to next week.

None of those are ideal outcomes. But they're all better than what I actually did, which was fail at all of it simultaneously while pretending I had it under control.

Prioritize and Execute doesn't mean everything gets done perfectly. It means you own the reality that you can't do everything at once, so you identify what matters most, you execute on that, and then you move to the next thing. You lead the chaos instead of letting the chaos lead you.

The hard part, especially in discipleship, is figuring out what the highest priority actually is. Because a lot of the time, the loudest thing is not the most important thing. The urgent is not the same as the essential. 

There's a scripture in Doctrine and Covenants, section 10 verse 4, where the Lord is talking to Joseph Smith about some people who are trying to destroy the work, and He says, Do not run faster or labor more than you have strength. But it goes on, and this is the part that matters: see that all these things are done in wisdom and order, for it is not requisite that a man should run faster than he has strength. And again, it is expedient that he should be diligent, that thereby he might win the prize. Therefore all things must be done in order.

Wisdom and order. Not frantic scrambling. Not trying to do ten things because you're afraid of letting someone down. Wisdom to discern what actually matters most, and order to execute on that without getting pulled into the noise.

For me, that means I have to get ruthlessly honest with myself about what's actually the highest priority in any given moment, versus what just feels urgent because someone's upset or because I'm afraid of looking like I dropped the ball. A lot of times, the thing that feels most urgent is not the thing that will matter most a week from now, a month from now, a year from now.

That ministering family I was worried about? I did call them Monday. We talked, I listened, I offered what help I could. It was a good conversation. But if I'd tried to squeeze that call in Sunday morning while also prepping a lesson and also being present for my wife and kids, I would have done all of it badly and that conversation wouldn't have been worth anything. By waiting one day and giving it my full attention, I actually served them better.

The lesson I half-taught? I apologized to the class the next week. I told them I'd overcommitted and under-prepared, and that wasn't fair to them or to the material. A couple people came up afterward and said they appreciated the honesty. One guy said he'd been feeling the same way about a calling he'd taken on, trying to do too much and doing none of it well. We had a good conversation about what it actually means to serve versus what it means to just say yes to everything and then drown.

That's ownership. Not pretending I nailed it. Not making excuses. Just: I failed here, I own it, here's what I'm learning.

The thing I keep coming back to is that the Savior never asks us to do everything at once. He asks us to do the next right thing. One thing. The highest priority. Then the next one. 

There's a reason He taught us to pray, Give us this day our daily bread. Not, Give us a ten-year supply and a detailed plan for every possible contingency. Just: today. What do we need today. What's the priority today.

I think we overcomplicate discipleship sometimes. We look at the whole massive weight of everything we're supposed to be and do, everything we're not yet, every way we're falling short, and we try to fix all of it right now. We try to overcome every weakness, magnify every calling, perfect every relationship, serve every person, all at the same time. And then we burn out, or we give up, or we end up like me on that Sunday morning, failing at all of it because we're trying to do all of it.

But that's not how the Savior leads us. He meets us where we are, and He says, Here's the next step. Take it. Then we'll talk about the step after that.

Prioritize and Execute.

So here's what I'm asking myself now, and what I'm asking you to think about tonight. What's actually the highest priority in front of you right now in your discipleship? Not what's loudest. Not what makes you feel most guilty. Not what someone else thinks you should be focused on. What's the one thing that, if you executed on it well, would make the biggest difference?

Maybe it's repairing a relationship that's been broken for too long. Maybe it's actually keeping the Sabbath day holy instead of just checking the box. Maybe it's getting real about an addiction or a habit you've been pretending isn't a problem. Maybe it's spending time in the scriptures not because you're supposed to, but because you actually need to hear from God. Maybe it's serving one specific person who needs you right now, instead of scattering yourself across ten different service projects.

I don't know what it is for you. But I know this: you can't do everything at once. And the Savior doesn't expect you to. He expects you to prioritize, to own what matters most, and to execute on that. Then move to the next thing.

That's leadership. That's discipleship. That's Extreme Ownership.
