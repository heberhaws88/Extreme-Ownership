I was standing in the parking lot at the supply house this morning, keys in one hand, phone in the other, two missed calls from one of my journeymen already showing on the screen, when it hit me — I can't keep running my business like this.

I'd been on my way into the counter to grab materials for a service call, which I probably shouldn't have been doing anyway because I've got three other jobs running right now and two bids sitting on my desk that I haven't looked at yet. But I told myself I'd just swing by real quick, pick up what I needed, knock out this call, then get back to the office. And now here I am, phone ringing again — different guy this time — and I know exactly what's happening. They're calling because they need an answer. They need a decision. They need me to tell them what to do. And the reason they need me is because I've trained them to need me.

That's what I want to talk about tonight. Decentralized command.

This is chapter eight in the book Extreme Ownership, written by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin, and the principle is pretty straightforward on the surface — you can't control everything directly, you can't be everywhere at once, so you have to push decision-making authority down to the people who are closest to the actual problem. You train them, you give them the overall mission and the why behind it, and then you let them own their piece of it. You step back. You trust the structure you built.

Easy to say. Harder to do. Especially when you're the guy whose name is on the trucks.

Before I get into the story I want to tell tonight, I need to take a minute here, because no matter which lens we're looking through on any given night — business, Guard, family — my faith is the foundation under all of it. I follow Jesus Christ. I'm a member of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. And decentralized command, weirdly enough, shows up all through the way the Lord organizes His work.

Think about it. He doesn't run everything from the top down in some micromanaged hierarchy where nobody moves without direct instruction from headquarters. He calls people, He sets them apart, He gives them authority and stewardship over a specific area — a congregation, a youth group, a family, a calling — and then He expects them to act. To make decisions. To pray, to counsel together, to solve problems in their own stewardship without constantly running back up the chain for permission. He doesn't want robots. He wants disciples who can think, who understand the mission, who can hear the Spirit and make a call.

That doesn't mean He's absent. It doesn't mean there's no structure or no accountability. It means He trusts the people He's called to do the work He's called them to do. And when I try to hold onto every decision in my business, when I make my guys dependent on me for every judgment call, I'm not building that kind of strength. I'm building dependency. I'm building a bottleneck. And bottlenecks don't scale. They just break.

Alright. Let me tell you what's been going on.

About a year and a half ago, I hired a guy I'm going to call my lead journeyman. He'd been in the trade for a while, solid skills, good head on his shoulders, reliable. I brought him on because I was at that point where I couldn't keep doing every job myself and also run the business, so I needed someone who could run work without me standing over his shoulder. That was the theory, anyway.

In practice, what I did was I kept him on a pretty short leash. I'd send him out to a job, but I'd be calling him three times a day to check in. I'd swing by the site unannounced just to see how things were going. If he ran into something that wasn't on the plan, he'd call me, and I'd tell him exactly what to do. If a customer asked a question, he'd say he'd have to check with the boss. If he needed to make a call on whether to pull a permit for something borderline, he'd wait for me to weigh in. And I told myself this was good management. I told myself I was staying on top of things.

What I was actually doing was teaching him that he didn't have real authority. I was teaching him that his job was to execute, not to lead. And after a year and a half of that, I'd successfully created a really expensive extra set of hands who couldn't function without me in his ear.

The moment this all came to a head was about two months ago. I had four jobs running at the same time, which is more than I usually stack up, but we'd had a good stretch and I didn't want to turn work away. Three resi remodels and one small commercial tenant improvement. I was trying to float between all of them, make sure everything stayed on track, keep the customers happy, keep the inspectors happy, make sure materials showed up when they were supposed to. I also had an apprentice who was still pretty green, and I was trying to give him enough support that he didn't feel abandoned but not so much that I was doing his job for him.

It was too much. I knew it was too much. But I kept telling myself I could handle it if I just stayed organized, if I just worked a little harder, if I just got up a little earlier.

Then one of the resi jobs — a kitchen and panel upgrade — started going sideways. The homeowner was the kind of guy who wanted to be involved in every decision, which is fine, that's his right, but he also kept changing his mind about things. We'd frame something out, he'd look at it, decide he wanted it different. We'd move it, he'd decide he liked it the first way. My lead guy was on that job, and every time the homeowner had a new idea, my lead would call me. What do you want me to do? How do you want me to handle this? Do I charge him for the extra time?

Meanwhile, one of the other resi jobs hit a problem — we opened up a wall and found some old knob-and-tube that wasn't on the plan and wasn't in the scope of work. I'd told the crew on that job to call me if they found anything unexpected, so they did. They called me. And they stopped working while they waited for me to get back to them.

And the commercial job — small as it was — the general contractor was breathing down my neck about schedule because one of the other trades had fallen behind and now everybody was jammed up trying to finish at the same time.

I was getting calls from all three jobs, plus I'm trying to keep the apprentice moving forward on his work, plus I've got a supplier calling about a past-due invoice that I forgot to pay because I haven't had time to sit down and do paperwork in two weeks. I'm in my truck driving from one job to the next and I realize — I'm the problem here. I'm the bottleneck. Everything is waiting on me, and the reason everything is waiting on me is because I set it up that way.

That night I sat down with my wife and I just told her straight — I don't know how to let go. I don't know how to trust these guys to make calls without me, because what if they make the wrong call? What if they cost me money? What if they make the customer unhappy? What if they do something that fails an inspection and it comes back on my license? All of that is real risk. It's not imaginary. But what I was doing wasn't actually reducing that risk. It was just concentrating it all on me, and I was starting to crack under the weight.

She asked me a good question. She said, do you trust your lead guy?

And I said, yeah, I do. He's solid. He knows his stuff.

She said, then why are you treating him like he doesn't?

That landed.

So here's what I did. I'm still doing it, actually, because this isn't something you fix overnight. I sat down with my lead journeyman and I told him, look, I've been doing this wrong. I hired you to run work, but I haven't actually let you run work. I've been managing you like you're an apprentice, and that's on me. So here's what's changing. When you're on a job, you're in charge of that job. You make the calls. You talk to the customer. If something comes up that's not in the scope, you figure out what it's going to take to handle it, you give the customer a price, and you get their approval. You don't need to call me unless it's something major — and I mean major, like we found structural damage or there's a safety issue or the whole plan needs to change. Day-to-day stuff, judgment calls, how to route a pipe or where to put a box or whether to pull a permit on something minor — that's your call now. You own it.

He looked at me like he wasn't sure if I was serious.

I said, I'm serious. But here's the thing. For this to work, you need to understand the mission. Not just the task, the mission. The mission is we do good work, we do it safely, we do it on time, we stay within budget unless there's a real good reason not to, and we leave the customer happy. Those are the boundaries. Inside those boundaries, you make the call.

Then I told him — if you make a call and it doesn't work out, we'll talk about it. We'll figure out what happened and how to do it different next time. But I'm not going to hang you out to dry for making a decision you had the authority to make. That's part of this. You own the job, I own the outcome. If it goes bad, it's on me. That's what extreme ownership means. But you don't get to hide behind "I was just following orders" anymore, because I'm not giving you orders. I'm giving you a mission.

I had similar conversations, in different forms, with the other guys. The apprentice, I'm still checking in more often, because he's still learning, but I made it clear to him — I want you to try to solve the problem first before you call me. I want you to think it through, look at the code book, talk to the other guys if they're around, and make your best guess. Then call me and tell me what you think the answer is, and we'll talk through it. I'm not asking you to do it alone, but I am asking you to think.

It's been two months. Has it been perfect? No. Three weeks ago my lead made a call on a change order that I probably would have priced a little higher, so we left a little money on the table. But you know what? The customer was happy, the job got done, and my lead learned something about how to estimate on the fly. I didn't yell at him. We just talked through it. Next time he'll know.

Last week the apprentice wired up a three-way switch wrong and didn't catch it until the homeowner tested it and it didn't work right. He had to go back and fix it. He was mortified. But he fixed it, and he won't make that mistake again, and the homeowner was actually pretty gracious about it because my guy owned it, apologized, and made it right. That's leadership. That's what I want him to learn.

And here's the thing I didn't expect. My lead journeyman is better at some of this stuff than I am. He's better with certain kinds of customers. He's more patient. He explains things in a way that makes sense to people who don't know anything about electrical work. I would have kept him in a box where I never got to see that. I would have kept him as an expensive tool instead of letting him become a leader.

The business is running better now. I'm not going to pretend it's all smooth sailing, because it's not. I've still got stress, I've still got jobs that go long, I've still got cash-flow problems some months. But I'm not the bottleneck anymore. I can actually sit down and do a bid without my phone ringing six times. I can take a day off without the whole operation grinding to a halt. And the guys are growing. They're stepping up. They're starting to act like they own their piece of the work, because I finally gave them permission to.

Decentralized command doesn't mean you're not in charge. It doesn't mean you abandon people or throw them in the deep end with no training. It means you train them well, you give them clear boundaries and a clear mission, and then you trust them to execute within that framework. You stay available. You check in. You course-correct when you need to. But you don't micromanage. You don't hold onto every decision. You build leaders, not dependents.

If you're in a spot where you're the bottleneck — whether that's in your business, in your Guard unit, in your home, wherever — ask yourself this. Am I holding onto decisions that someone closer to the problem could and should make? And do the people under my charge understand the mission well enough to make good calls without me in the room?

If the answer to that second question is no, that's not their fault. That's on you. You haven't trained them. You haven't communicated the mission clearly enough. You haven't built the structure that lets them succeed.

And if the answer is yes, they do understand it, but you still won't let go — then you need to own that. You need to ask yourself what you're afraid of, and whether holding on tight is actually protecting anything, or whether it's just keeping everyone small.

Here's what I want you to do. Pick one thing you're holding onto that you don't need to be. One decision, one responsibility, one area where you're the go-to person and it's slowing everything down. And tomorrow, give it to someone else. Tell them what the mission is. Tell them what the boundaries are. Tell them you trust them to make the call. And then step back. Let them own it.

It's going to feel uncomfortable. It's going to feel risky. Do it anyway.
