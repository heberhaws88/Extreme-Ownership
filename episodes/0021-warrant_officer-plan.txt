I got to the armory last Thursday at five thirty in the morning, coffee still hot in my hand, ready to finalize the convoy brief for a bridge-inspection movement we were running out to a site about ninety miles west. The plan looked solid on paper. Routes marked, convoy intervals set, equipment manifests complete. I'd spent most of Wednesday night putting it together after work, after the kids were in bed, sitting at the kitchen table with my laptop and a stack of field manuals I don't look at as often as I probably should.

I walked into the bay and found my senior NCO, a staff sergeant who's been doing this longer than I've been in uniform, standing there with his arms crossed and a look on his face that told me exactly what kind of morning this was about to be.

He said, "Sir, we've got a problem."

Turns out two of the vehicles I'd planned to use didn't have current dispatch paperwork. One of the trailers I'd assumed would be available was already committed to another unit's training event. And the route I'd picked, the one that shaved fifteen minutes off the trip, ran through a stretch of road that had been under construction for the last month — something literally everyone in the company knew except me, because I hadn't asked.

We made it work. We always make it work. But we rolled out an hour late, with a cobbled-together convoy that didn't match the brief I'd written, and I spent the entire drive knowing that every soldier in that convoy was watching a Warrant Officer learn a lesson he should've already known.

Tonight's principle is Plan. And the core idea is this: good planning doesn't mean sitting alone in your office the night before and producing a perfect product that you hand down from on high. Good planning means taking the time to analyze the mission, to identify every likely problem before it shows up, and to build a plan with input from the people who are actually going to execute it. It means asking the questions you don't want to ask because they might make you look like you don't have it all figured out. It means accepting that the guy three ranks below you might know something critical that you don't, and that your job as a leader is to create the space for him to tell you.

Jocko Willink and Leif Babin lay this out in chapter nine of Extreme Ownership, and they're not talking about paperwork for paperwork's sake. They're talking about the difference between a plan that survives contact with reality and a plan that falls apart the second something unexpected happens — which, in the military and everywhere else, is always.

Here's the thing I've had to learn the hard way, over and over: I like to think I'm competent. I am competent, in certain narrow technical lanes. I can read a set of engineering drawings. I can troubleshoot a power distribution system. I know how to calculate load-bearing requirements for a temporary bridge. That's why I'm a Warrant Officer. That's the whole point of the rank — you're a subject-matter expert in your field, and the Army needs people who can go deep on the technical side without getting pulled into the broader command responsibilities that come with being a commissioned officer.

But competence in one area does not mean you've got the whole picture. And when you're leading soldiers, even in a technical role, even as a Warrant who's supposed to be the quiet expert in the corner, you are still leading. And leadership requires more than being right about the thing you're an expert in. It requires humility. It requires admitting that you don't know everything. It requires actually planning, not just assuming.

Now before I go any further into that convoy story, let me tie this back to where it belongs. Because I follow Jesus Christ, and I'm a member of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, and that means something when we're talking about planning. It means something when we're talking about humility.

There's a verse in Proverbs, chapter fifteen, verse twenty-two: "Without counsel purposes are disappointed, but in the multitude of counsellors they are established." That's King James English for: if you try to do it all yourself, you're going to fail, but if you actually seek wisdom from the people around you, your plans will hold up.

That's not just good advice for military operations. That's a principle of discipleship. Jesus didn't operate alone. He called twelve apostles. He sent them out in pairs. He asked questions. He listened. Even the Son of God, who knew all things, modeled a way of leading that involved other people. And if He did that, then I, a Warrant Officer who barely understands half of what the logistics officer is talking about in the weekly synch meeting, definitely need to do that.

Pride is the enemy of good planning. Pride tells you that asking for input makes you look weak. Pride tells you that you should already know the answer. Pride tells you that the plan you came up with on your own is going to be better than the plan you build with a team, because you're the expert, you're the one in charge, you're the officer.

And pride gets soldiers hurt. Pride gets missions failed. Pride gets you standing in front of your commander explaining why the thing you said would take four hours took nine, and why you didn't know about the obstacles that everyone else saw coming.

Following Christ means killing that pride over and over, every single day. It means praying for the humility to ask the questions. It means recognizing that God put other people in your life for a reason, and that reason includes the fact that they know things you don't. Planning, real planning, is an act of humility. And humility is the soil that discipleship grows in.

Alright. Back to that convoy.

So we're an hour late rolling out, and I'm sitting in the lead vehicle going over in my head all the things I should have done differently. And the list is long. I should have walked the motor pool two days prior and physically verified which vehicles were available. I should have pulled my senior NCO into the planning process from the beginning, not just handed him a completed product and expected him to execute. I should have asked the junior enlisted guys who actually drive these routes all the time if there was anything I needed to know. I should have built in more time. I should have done a rehearsal. I should have planned for the fact that something was going to go wrong, because something always goes wrong.

Instead, I did what I've done too many times: I treated planning like a solo event. I sat down with the information I had, I made the best decisions I could with that information, and I packaged it up into a neat five-paragraph order that looked good on paper. And then I got surprised, because the information I had wasn't complete. Because I didn't ask.

Here's what I've learned, slowly, painfully, over the last several years in uniform: planning is a team event. It has to be. The Warrant Officer is not the guy who has all the answers. The Warrant Officer is the guy who knows enough to ask the right questions and then listens to the people who actually have the answers.

When we got back from that bridge inspection, I sat down with my senior NCO and we went through the whole thing step by step. And I told him, flat-out, "I should have brought you in earlier. I should have asked what I was missing. I didn't, and that's on me."

And he nodded, and he said, "Yeah, sir. You should have."

That's the kind of honesty you get when you own your failures. And that honesty is worth more than any amount of pretending you had it under control.

So the next time we had a similar mission, I did it differently. I started the planning process three days out instead of the night before. I pulled in my senior NCO from the start and we built the convoy order together. I walked the motor pool with the mechanic and verified every single vehicle. I asked the junior soldiers who were going to be driving if they knew anything about the route that I didn't. And one of them, a specialist who's been with the unit for six years, mentioned that there was a weight-restricted bridge on the route I was planning to use, and that we'd need to either take a different route or use lighter vehicles.

I did not know that. It wasn't on the map I was looking at. But he knew it, because he'd driven that route a dozen times. And because I asked, we avoided what would have been a serious problem.

That mission went smooth. We rolled out on time, hit every checkpoint when we said we would, completed the inspection, and got everyone back safe. And it went smooth not because I'm a better planner than I was three months prior, but because I finally accepted that planning is not a thing you do alone.

Now, I'm not going to pretend I've got this figured out. I still catch myself doing the lone-wolf thing more often than I'd like to admit. Just two weeks ago I tasked one of my junior Warrants with putting together a training package for some new equipment we'd just received, and I told him exactly how I wanted it structured and exactly what I wanted covered, and I didn't ask him if he had any ideas. And the training package he turned in was fine, but it wasn't great, and I realized afterward that I'd basically just told him to execute my plan instead of letting him build one. Which means I got my plan, with all its limitations, instead of getting his plan, which might have been better.

I had to go back to him and say, "Hey, I didn't give you room to actually plan this. I just gave you orders. Let's start over. What do you think we should do?"

And he had good ideas. Ideas I wouldn't have thought of. And the training we ended up running was better because I backed off and let him plan it.

This is hard for me. I think it's hard for a lot of technical people. We get good at a thing, and we start to believe that being good at the thing means we should also be the ones planning how the thing gets done. But leadership doesn't work that way. Leadership means you've got to let other people into the process, even when you're pretty sure you know the best way forward. Because you might be wrong. And even if you're not wrong, the plan that everyone helped build is going to get executed better than the plan you built alone, because people support what they help create.

Here's another angle on this: in the Guard, we're part-timers. We're citizen-soldiers. Most of us have full-time jobs. I run my electrical business during the week, and I put on the uniform one weekend a month and a couple weeks a year. Which means the time I have to plan is limited, and the time the soldiers under me have to plan is limited, and if I don't start early and bring people in, we end up in a situation where nobody really understands the plan and we're all just reacting in real-time.

That's not leadership. That's chaos with rank structure.

Good planning takes time. It takes more time than you think it's going to take. And it takes the willingness to sit down with people and say, "Alright, here's the mission. Here's what I'm thinking. What am I missing? What could go wrong? What do you think we should do?"

And then you've got to actually listen. You've got to be willing to change your plan based on what you hear. You've got to be willing to admit that the private who's been in the unit longer than you have might know something you don't. That's not weakness. That's wisdom.

One more thing before I wrap this up: part of planning is contingency planning. It's not enough to have a plan for how things are supposed to go. You've got to have a plan for what happens when things don't go the way they're supposed to go. Because they won't. The vehicle's going to break down. The weather's going to turn. The site you're supposed to inspect is going to be inaccessible. The equipment you need isn't going to show up. Something is going to go wrong.

And if you didn't plan for that, if you didn't sit down with your team and say, "Alright, what are the three most likely things that could go sideways here, and what are we going to do when they do?" — then you're going to be making it up on the spot. And making it up on the spot is how people get hurt.

So here's what I'm asking you to think about tonight, whether you're in uniform or not: Did you actually plan for this week, or are you just reacting to whatever comes at you? And who should have had a voice in that plan who didn't?

Maybe you're planning a project at work and you've been doing it alone because you're the supervisor and you're supposed to have the answers. Stop. Bring your team in. Ask them what they think. Ask them what could go wrong.

Maybe you're planning something at home, some big decision or some change, and you've been working it out in your own head without actually talking to your wife about it. Stop. Sit down with her. Build the plan together.

Maybe you're planning your week and you haven't thought past Monday morning. Stop. Take thirty minutes. Look at the whole week. Identify the likely problems. Build in margin. Ask the people around you what you're missing.

Planning is not about having all the answers. Planning is about asking the right questions, bringing the right people into the conversation, and building something that can survive reality. And reality is brutal. Reality does not care how good your plan looked on paper. Reality only cares whether your plan accounted for the things that were actually going to happen.

So plan. Actually plan. Don't just write an order and hope for the best. Sit down. Bring people in. Ask the hard questions. Identify the contingencies. Build a plan that your whole team understands and has helped shape. And then, when something goes wrong anyway, because it will, you'll have a team that knows how to adapt because they were part of the planning process from the start.

That's it. That's tonight. Own the planning. Own the outcome. And bring your people into the process. I'll see you tomorrow.
