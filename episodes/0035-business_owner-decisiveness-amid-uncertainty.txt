You can't know everything before you decide. And if you wait until you think you do, you've already made a decision — you've decided to let the moment choose for you instead of choosing it yourself.

That's what I'm wrestling with tonight. Decisiveness amid uncertainty. It's chapter eleven in Extreme Ownership by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin, and the core idea is this: leaders almost never have complete information. You can wait, gather a little more data, run one more scenario, sleep on it one more night — but at some point waiting becomes the decision, and it's usually the wrong one. A good leader gathers what's reasonably available, weighs it fast, decides, and then adjusts as the reality on the ground changes. Perfect clarity isn't coming. Move anyway.

Tonight I'm coming at this as a business owner, as an electrician who runs his own shop. And I'll be straight with you — this principle has cost me money, time, and credibility more times than I want to count, because I've failed it more often than I've lived it. Indecision doesn't feel like a choice when you're in it. It feels responsible. It feels careful. But it bleeds you.

Before I get into the story I want to tell tonight, I need to anchor this where it always has to anchor for me — in following Jesus Christ. Because decisiveness isn't just a business tactic or a leadership technique. It's built into the call to discipleship. Think about the way the Savior called His first Apostles. He didn't give them a white paper on the ministry plan. He didn't wait until they had their lives sorted out or their questions answered. He walked past them working their nets and said, "Follow me." And they did. Immediately. That word shows up over and over in the Gospels — immediately. They left the nets. Immediately. They followed. That doesn't mean recklessness. It means they decided in the presence of uncertainty because the call was clear enough and waiting wasn't going to make it clearer.

As a member of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, I believe God still calls us that way. Not usually with a voice out of heaven, but with a prompting, a feeling, a question that won't go away, a door that opens just enough to see the next step but not the whole staircase. And if I wait for the whole staircase to be lit up before I move, I'm not being wise. I'm being disobedient. Faith is acting on what you know even when you don't know everything. It's deciding in the presence of uncertainty because you trust the One who called you.

That's the foundation. Now let me tell you where I failed this and what it cost.

A while back — maybe a year and a half ago — I got a call from a potential client, a guy who was developing a small commercial property, three retail bays and some office space above. He needed the whole electrical system roughed in, service brought in from the street, panels, the works. It was a bigger job than I usually bid. Most of my work is residential service calls, some remodels, the occasional new house. This was a step up. Not huge, but bigger. And the money was real.

I went out, walked the site, took measurements, asked questions. He wanted a bid within the week. I told him I'd get it to him. I came back to the shop and started running numbers. Material costs, labor hours, how many guys I'd need, how long it would take. But I kept hesitating. I didn't have enough experience with commercial work to know if my numbers were right. I could be way over and lose the job. I could be way under and lose my shirt. I told myself I needed to gather more information. I called a supplier to confirm pricing on some switchgear. I sketched out three different crew configurations. I looked at my schedule and tried to figure out when I could even start. Every time I sat down to finalize the bid, I found another variable I wasn't sure about.

A week went by. I didn't send the bid. The client called, asked if I was still interested. I told him yes, absolutely, just finalizing some details. He said fine, but he had two other electricians bidding and he needed to move soon. I said I understood. I told myself I'd finish it that night. I didn't. I spent another two days tweaking numbers, second-guessing my labor hours, wondering if I should bring in a subcontractor for the panel work or just do it in-house. I finally sent him a bid — ten days after I walked the site, three days after he told me he needed to move.

He went with someone else. He was polite about it. Said he appreciated my time, but he'd already accepted another bid. I asked if it was a pricing issue, and he said no, actually my number was competitive. He just couldn't wait any longer. He had a schedule to keep.

I lost that job because I didn't decide. I had enough information to bid it responsibly after that first site visit. I didn't need perfect certainty. I needed a number that reflected a reasonable estimate of the work based on what I knew, with a margin built in for what I didn't. But I waited. I told myself I was being careful. What I actually was, was scared. Scared of being wrong. Scared of looking foolish. Scared of losing money on a job I didn't fully understand. And that fear dressed itself up as prudence, and I let it.

Here's the thing — indecision didn't protect me. It cost me. I didn't avoid risk by waiting. I guaranteed a loss. The client moved on. My crew sat on smaller jobs that week when they could've been starting something bigger. And I burned credibility with a guy who could've been a repeat client or a referral source if I'd just delivered what I said I'd deliver when I said I'd deliver it.

The principle says you decide with the information you have, and then you adjust. You don't wait for certainty. You move, and you correct as you go. If I'd sent that bid on time and won the job, maybe my labor estimate would've been off. Maybe I would've had to eat a few hours. Maybe I would've hit a snag I didn't see coming and had to problem-solve on the fly. Fine. That's the job. That's reality. You learn more from doing the work than you ever will from sitting in the office trying to model every possible outcome in your head. Decisiveness doesn't mean you get it perfect. It means you commit to a course of action and then own what happens next, including the adjustments.

I've gotten a little better at this since then, but it's still a fight. Just a couple of months ago I had a decision in front of me about hiring. I've got two apprentices and a journeyman who works for me pretty regularly, but we've been busy enough that I've had to turn down work or push jobs out further than I want. I knew I needed another body, another guy I could trust to run simple service calls or handle rough-in work without me standing over his shoulder. But hiring is risk. It's payroll. It's workers' comp. It's another human being depending on me to keep the work coming. And I didn't know if the current pace would hold. What if it was just a seasonal bump? What if I brought someone on and then had to lay him off three months later?

I sat on it. I kept telling myself I'd decide after I saw how the next few weeks shook out. But of course, the next few weeks were also busy, and I still didn't hire anyone. I just kept scrambling, kept working six-day weeks, kept telling clients I could get to them in two and a half weeks when I knew they wanted it done yesterday. I was waiting for certainty about the future workload, which is insane, because that certainty doesn't exist. You can't know in May what December is going to look like. You just can't.

What finally pushed me off the fence wasn't perfect information. It was my wife. She asked me one night why I seemed stressed all the time, and I told her it was the workload. She asked if I was turning down jobs, and I said yeah, some. She said, "So you have more work than you can handle, and you're stressed about whether to hire someone to help you handle it?" And when she said it like that, it sounded as dumb as it was. I hired a guy two weeks later. Young kid, green, but he's got a good attitude and he's willing to learn. It's been two months now and I haven't regretted it once. I should've done it eight weeks earlier.

Here's what I'm learning, slowly and badly: the cost of indecision is almost always higher than the cost of a wrong decision, because at least a wrong decision teaches you something and lets you move. Indecision just bleeds time, opportunity, and momentum. And in a small business, you don't have much of any of those to spare.

The tricky part is that indecision feels virtuous. It feels like you're being responsible, like you're not rushing, like you're thinking it through. And sometimes that's true. There are decisions that deserve a hard pause, a second look, a night to sleep on. But most of the time, if I'm honest, when I'm stalling it's not because I need more information. It's because I'm afraid of being wrong. And that's not wisdom. That's cowardice wearing a mask.

Extreme Ownership means you own the outcome of your decisions, but it also means you own the outcome of your indecision. If I don't bid a job on time and I lose it, that's on me. If I don't hire when I need to and my crew burns out or clients go elsewhere, that's on me. I don't get to hide behind "I was waiting for more clarity." There wasn't more clarity coming. I was just afraid to move.

So what does decisiveness actually look like in practice? For me, it's meant building a few forcing functions into how I operate. One is putting time limits on decisions. If a client asks for a bid, I tell them I'll have it to them in three days, and then I get it to them in three days. I don't let myself sit on it. I gather what I can in that window, I run the numbers, I make my best call, and I send it. If I'm wrong, I'm wrong, but at least I'm in the game. Another is naming my own stall tactics out loud. If I catch myself saying "I just need to check one more thing," I stop and ask myself: is this actually going to change my decision, or am I just afraid to pull the trigger? Most of the time it's the second one. So I decide.

The other thing I've learned is that decisiveness doesn't mean you're locked in forever. Decide, then adjust. You're not carving it in stone. You're setting a direction and moving. If the information changes, you change. If you learn something new on the job, you adapt. But you can't adapt if you never start.

Let me bring this around. If you run a business, if you lead a crew, if you're responsible for anything that moves and changes and demands calls you can't fully model in advance — ask yourself: what decision am I stalling on right now? What am I waiting for perfect clarity on that's never going to be perfectly clear? Is it hiring, or firing? Is it a bid you're afraid to send because you're not sure your numbers are right? Is it pulling the trigger on a piece of equipment, or a new service offering, or a hard conversation with a client or an employee? What's the thing you keep putting off, telling yourself you'll decide when you know more?

Here's the question that cuts through it for me: what is my indecision already costing? Because it's costing something. It might be costing opportunity. It might be costing trust. It might be costing your peace of mind or your crew's morale or your family's stability. Indecision is not a neutral position. It's a choice, and it has a price.

Tonight, or tomorrow morning, name the decision. Write it down if you have to. And then set a deadline. Not someday. Not when you feel ready. A date. This week. And when that date comes, decide with what you have. Gather what's reasonable to gather, weigh it fast, and move. If you're wrong, you'll find out soon enough, and you'll adjust. But you'll be moving. You'll be leading. And that's what your people need from you. Not perfect foresight. Just a leader who decides and then owns what comes next.
