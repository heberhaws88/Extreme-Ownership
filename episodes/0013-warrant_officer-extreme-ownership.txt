I got tapped last month to run a pretty simple project on the military side. Pretty simple on paper, anyway. We had a training exercise coming up, three-day drill weekend, and my job as the engineer Warrant Officer was to get a couple temporary bridges set up at the site ahead of time so the infantry guys could rehearse river crossings. Timeline was tight but manageable. I had a solid sergeant, a guy who's been in the unit longer than me, and a crew of younger soldiers, mostly privates and specialists, all capable. I gave the sergeant the mission, walked him through the site plan, told him what I needed done and when I needed it done by. Confident. Delegated. Moved on to the next thing on my plate.

Day one of the exercise rolls around. I show up at the site expecting two bridges ready to go. What I find instead is one bridge half-assembled, parts scattered everywhere, and the other bridge not even started. The sergeant's standing there looking frustrated, the privates are milling around looking lost, and the infantry commander whose guys are supposed to be training on this thing is giving me a look that says everything I need to know about how this just made his day harder.

My first instinct, and I mean my very first thought in that moment, was to be angry at the sergeant. I gave him clear guidance. I gave him the resources. I gave him the timeline. He dropped the ball. That's the story I started telling myself in real time, standing there looking at that mess.

That instinct is what tonight's about.

The principle is called Extreme Ownership, and it's the first chapter of the book by that same title, written by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin, two Navy SEAL officers. The idea is ruthlessly simple and ruthlessly hard to live. When you're the leader, you own everything in your world. Every outcome, good or bad. Every failure. Every gap. You don't pass blame down to the people under you. You don't pass it sideways to your peers. You don't pass it up to your boss or to circumstances outside your control. You own it. All of it. Because if you're the leader, it is your fault. Not in some theoretical, philosophical sense. In the most practical, operational sense. If something didn't get done, if something went wrong, the first question isn't who screwed up. The first question is what did I as the leader fail to do that allowed this to happen.

That's not natural. It's not the way we're wired. When something goes south, we look for reasons. We look for who to blame. And if we're honest, we usually start by looking anywhere but the mirror.

Before I go any further into that bridge story, I need to say something about ownership and following Jesus Christ, because this principle lives right at the center of what it means to be a disciple.

Jesus owned what wasn't His fault. That's the gospel in one sentence. He took responsibility for sin He didn't commit, for brokenness He didn't cause, for a debt He didn't owe. And He did it willingly. Nobody forced Him. He could have walked away. He could have pointed at all of us and said, you made this mess, you fix it. Instead, He owned it. All of it. He stepped into the gap and said, I'll carry this.

When I stand in front of my soldiers or my kids or my wife or my God and try to deflect blame, try to explain why something isn't really my fault, I'm doing the opposite of what Christ did. I'm doing what Adam did in the garden. The Lord asks, what happened here, and Adam says, well, the woman You gave me, she gave me the fruit. It's her fault. Actually, if we're being honest, it's kind of Your fault for giving her to me in the first place. That's the first recorded excuse in human history, and it's pathetic, and I do the exact same thing more often than I want to admit.

Extreme Ownership is a leadership principle, but it's also a discipleship principle. To follow Jesus means to own my stuff. My sin. My failures. My gaps. No excuses. No blaming someone else. Just ownership. Repentance starts there. You can't repent of something you won't own.

So back to the bridge site.

I'm standing there looking at this disaster, and my brain is running the calculation. The sergeant screwed up. That's objectively true. I can make a case for that. I can go to my commander and say, sir, I delegated this to the NCO, he didn't execute, lesson learned, I'll handle it myself next time. That explanation covers me. It's not even a lie. The sergeant did drop the ball.

But here's the thing. Extreme Ownership doesn't care about technically true explanations. It cares about one question: as the leader, what did I fail to do?

And the answer, once I stopped being defensive long enough to see it, was pretty obvious.

I didn't supervise. I gave the sergeant the mission and walked away. I assumed he understood the details the same way I understood them. I assumed he'd break it down for the privates and specialists in a way that made sense. I assumed he'd check their work. I assumed he'd figure out the sequence of assembly on his own even though these bridges are a little tricky if you haven't done them before. I assumed a lot. I didn't check in. I didn't ask how it was going. I didn't show up the day before to see the progress. I just fired and forgot, like a smart missile, except this missile didn't have the guidance system I thought it did.

The sergeant didn't fail. I failed to lead him.

That's what Extreme Ownership forces you to see. If I had checked in halfway through, I would've caught the confusion early. If I had asked the sergeant to brief me back on the plan, I would've realized he wasn't clear on the sequence. If I had assigned one of the more experienced specialists to walk the privates through the assembly instead of assuming the sergeant would think of that himself, we'd have had two bridges ready to go.

Every single point of failure traces back to something I didn't do as the leader.

Here's the other piece of this. I'm a Warrant Officer. My whole job is to be the technical expert. I'm the engineer. I know how these bridges work. I know how to assemble them. I know the tolerances and the load ratings and the time it takes. The Army made me a Warrant Officer specifically because I have that expertise. And there's a trap in that. When you're the technical expert, it's really easy to think that your job is just to know the right answer and tell people what to do. You brief the mission, you give the task, and then you go back to your desk and work on the next technical problem. That's what I did. I acted like a subject matter expert, not a leader.

But I wear the same uniform as every other officer. I have the same responsibility. And that responsibility is not just to know the right answer. It's to make sure the mission gets done. Which means I have to lead, not just advise. I have to train, supervise, inspect, correct, and follow through. The sergeant works for me. If he doesn't understand the task, that's on me. If the privates don't know what they're doing, that's on me. If the timeline slips, that's on me.

All of it. On me.

So what did I do standing there at the bridge site with the infantry commander waiting and my guys looking at me? I owned it. Out loud. I told the infantry commander, sir, this is my fault, I didn't supervise the work, we're going to get this fixed, and I'll have it ready in four hours. I told the sergeant, this isn't on you, I should've checked in, let's figure out what we need to do right now to get this done. And then I rolled up my sleeves, got in the dirt with the privates, and we built the bridges. All hands. I didn't yell. I didn't make anyone feel stupid. I just led.

We got it done. The infantry guys got their training. It wasn't pretty, and we lost time we shouldn't have lost, but the mission got accomplished.

Here's what happened after. That sergeant came to me the next drill and said, sir, I need to tell you, I didn't plan that well, I should've mapped it out better before we started. And I said, I appreciate that, but I'm the officer, I should've been there to help you plan it, let's talk about what we both learned. That conversation wouldn't have happened if I'd blamed him. If I'd thrown him under the bus, he would've gotten defensive, shut down, and learned nothing except that I'm not someone he can trust. Instead, because I owned it first, he felt safe enough to own his part. That's what Extreme Ownership does. It creates a culture where people can admit mistakes, learn, and get better, because the leader's already set the example by owning his own.

And here's the other thing. My commander never heard an excuse from me. When he asked how it went, I told him the truth. I said, sir, the bridges were late, that's on me, I didn't supervise the work, it's handled now, here's what I'm going to do differently next time. He didn't chew me out. He didn't lose confidence in me. He respected the ownership. Because leaders respect leaders who own their stuff. What they don't respect is excuses.

I've been thinking about this a lot since then, because it applies to every part of my life, not just the military side.

At home, when my kids don't do what I've asked them to do, my first instinct is to be frustrated with them. But if I'm honest, half the time the problem is I didn't explain it clearly, or I didn't check to make sure they understood, or I didn't follow up to see if they actually did it. That's on me. I'm the dad. I'm the leader in that home. If my kids are failing at something, the first question isn't what's wrong with them. It's what did I fail to teach or reinforce or model.

At the business, when one of my apprentices makes a mistake on a job, I can blame him, or I can ask whether I trained him well enough, whether I gave him clear direction, whether I checked his work. Most of the time, if I'm honest, there's something I could've done better as his boss.

Even at church. I serve in a calling. If something I'm responsible for doesn't get done, I can blame the people I was working with, or I can own the fact that I'm the one who was supposed to coordinate it, communicate it, follow through on it.

Extreme Ownership doesn't mean I'm responsible for everything that happens in the universe. It means I'm responsible for everything in my world, everything in my sphere of leadership. My soldiers. My kids. My employees. My team. If they fail, I own it. Because I'm the leader. That's the job.

And the hard part, the part that makes this principle so brutal and so necessary, is that owning it means I have to change. If I blame someone else, I don't have to do anything differently. It's their problem. But if I own it, then I have to look at what I did wrong and fix it. I have to get better. I have to lead better. That's uncomfortable. It's humbling. It requires me to admit I don't have it all figured out.

But that's where growth happens. That's where I actually become a better leader. Not by being right. By owning where I was wrong.

Here's what I want you to do. Think about one thing in your life right now that didn't go the way you wanted it to. Maybe it's at work. Maybe it's at home. Maybe it's a relationship. Maybe it's a project. Something that didn't turn out right. And I want you to ask yourself, honestly, what was my part in that? Not what was their part. What was mine. What did I fail to do? What did I fail to see? Where did I not lead the way I should have?

And then own it. Out loud, if you can. To the person it affects. To your boss. To your spouse. To your kid. To yourself. No excuses. No blame. Just ownership.

It's going to feel vulnerable. It's supposed to. That's the cost of leadership. But it's also the path to trust, to growth, to actually fixing the problem instead of just explaining it away.

I'm still learning this. I still catch myself wanting to blame the sergeant, the apprentice, the kid, the circumstance. But every time I choose ownership instead, something shifts. The problem gets smaller. The relationship gets stronger. I get better.

That's Extreme Ownership.
