I got called into the battalion headquarters building on a Wednesday night last month, just as I was wrapping up some paperwork in the engineer shop. The S-3 needed to see me. That's the operations officer, for anyone not tracking military staff titles. I walked in and he had that look on his face — the one that says the plan just changed and somebody's problem is about to become my problem.

He told me we had a bridge-gap crossing exercise scheduled for the following drill weekend, which I already knew, but what I didn't know was that the site we'd planned to use was now off-limits. Environmental concern, some paperwork snafu, doesn't matter. The site was gone. He needed me to scout a new location, submit a plan, coordinate with the landowner and the county, and have everything approved and ready to execute in nine days. Oh, and by the way, the battalion commander was briefing the brigade on this exercise Monday morning, so he needed my recommendation by Sunday night at the latest.

I walked out of that office with about thirty percent of the information I actually wanted. I didn't know if the alternate sites I had in mind would even be accessible this time of year. I didn't know if the landowner would pick up the phone. I didn't know if the county would require permits we couldn't get in time. I had a lot of unknowns and not a lot of hours to resolve them.

And that's where we're camped out tonight. Decisiveness amid uncertainty. Chapter eleven of Extreme Ownership, by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin. The core idea is this: leaders almost never have perfect information. If you wait until every question is answered and every variable is nailed down, you've already made a decision — the decision to do nothing, or to do it too late. A good leader gathers what information is actually available, makes the call, and then adjusts on the move as reality fills in the gaps.

Waiting for certainty feels safe. It feels responsible. But in most situations, it's neither. It's paralysis dressed up as prudence.

Before I get into the specifics of how that played out with the bridge site, I want to spend a minute on why this principle matters beyond just getting the mission done, because for me decisiveness isn't just a leadership competency — it's connected to faith.

Following Jesus Christ means walking forward when the path isn't fully lit. There's a verse in Ether, in the Book of Mormon, where the Lord tells the brother of Jared that we don't receive a witness until after the trial of our faith. In other words, you don't get all the answers up front. You step forward in faith, you act on what light you have, and more light comes as you move.

I think a lot of us — and I'm absolutely including myself here — treat our relationship with God like we're waiting for a complete operations order before we're willing to obey. We want every contingency addressed, every question answered, every risk mitigated, and then, maybe, we'll move. But that's not how it works. The Lord gives us commandments, He gives us principles, He gives us the Holy Ghost to guide us step by step — and then He expects us to decide and to act. He doesn't typically hand us the whole map. He hands us a direction and says go.

Indecision in our discipleship is still a decision. If I know I need to have a hard conversation with someone I've wronged, and I keep waiting for the perfect time or the perfect words, I've decided not to repent yet. If I know the Lord has called me to serve in a specific way, and I keep waiting until I feel fully prepared or fully certain, I've decided not to serve yet. Uncertainty is not an excuse for inaction. It's the environment in which faith is actually exercised.

And that same principle applies when I'm standing in the engineer bay with a problem to solve and a clock ticking.

So, back to the bridge site. I left that meeting with the S-3 and I had a choice. I could spend the next three days gathering every possible piece of information, running every possible option to ground, trying to de-risk the decision to the point where I was absolutely sure I was making the right call — and by the time I had that certainty, it would be too late to actually execute. Or I could gather what I could reasonably get in the time available, make the best call I could make with that information, brief it, and then be ready to adjust if something changed.

I went back to my desk and pulled up the map. We had two alternate sites that had been on the list when we originally planned this exercise. One was a county park with a dry creekbed that would work for a simulated gap crossing. The other was a private property, a farm with a drainage ditch that ran through it, owned by a guy who'd let us use his land before for training. The county park was easier to access, no permission needed beyond the county's standing agreement with the Guard. The farm was a better training site, more realistic terrain, but it required coordinating with the landowner and I hadn't talked to him in over a year.

I didn't have time to fully scout both sites, coordinate both options, and then pick one. I had to pick one and run with it.

I called the landowner. Got his voicemail. Left a message. Then I sat there staring at my phone for about ten minutes, which in hindsight was ten minutes of indecision that I didn't have to spend. I was waiting to hear back from him before I committed to a course of action, but waiting wasn't actually gathering information — it was just stalling.

So I made the call. I was going with the farm. It was the better site, I had a decent relationship with the guy, and even if he said no, I had enough time to pivot to the county park as a backup. But I wasn't going to sit in limbo hoping for his call. I started working the problem as if the farm was the site. I pulled the grid coordinates, I sketched out where we'd stage equipment, I started drafting the risk assessment and the coordination checklist.

The landowner called me back that night. He was good with it, same terms as last time. Great. One variable resolved. But I didn't have certainty when I started working the problem — I had a reasonable assumption and a backup plan.

The next day I drove out to the site. I needed eyes on it. The map said it would work, but maps don't tell you if the access road is washed out or if there's a new fence line that blocks your maneuver space. I got out there and it was mostly good. The ditch was dry, the ground was firm enough, the access road was rough but passable. But there was a new issue: the power company had run a new line across part of the area I'd planned to use for vehicle staging. I didn't know if that was a safety issue or not. I didn't know if we'd need clearance from the power company or if we just needed to keep equipment a certain distance away.

I could have stopped right there. I could have said, well, I don't have enough information, I need to call the power company, I need to get their specs, I need to wait for their answer. And by the time I had all that, I'd be briefing the battalion commander on Sunday night with half a plan.

Instead, I adjusted. I moved the staging area. I put it on the other side of the access road, away from the power lines, which actually made more sense tactically anyway. I made a note to confirm with the power company that we were clear of their easement, but I didn't let that unknown stop me from finishing the plan.

By Saturday I had a complete concept of operations sketched out. I knew where the bridge would go, I knew where the vehicles would stage, I knew how we'd control traffic, I knew what the safety brief would cover. I ran it past one of the senior NCOs in the platoon, a guy with more bridge time than I have, and he poked a couple holes in it — good holes, things I'd missed. I adjusted again.

Sunday night I sent the plan to the S-3. He forwarded it to the battalion commander. Monday morning the commander briefed it to brigade. We were good to go.

Now here's the thing. The plan I briefed on Sunday was not the plan I would have written if I'd had three weeks and perfect information. It wasn't flawless. There were things I'd have done differently if I'd had more time. But it was a solid plan, it was executable, and it was on time. And more importantly, because I'd made decisions along the way instead of waiting for certainty, I'd built in enough flexibility that when we actually ran the exercise the following weekend and something did go wrong — a piece of equipment that was supposed to be there wasn't, because another unit had tasked it out and nobody had updated the status — we were able to adjust on the fly and still accomplish the training.

If I'd waited for perfect information, we wouldn't have had a plan at all.

Here's what I'm learning about decisiveness, and I'm still learning it, I haven't mastered this: indecision costs more than most wrong decisions. A wrong decision that's made in good faith, with the information available, can be corrected. You adjust, you adapt, you move forward. But indecision just burns time and creates paralysis, and by the time you finally make a choice, you've often lost the opportunity to execute it well.

And the tricky part for me, especially as a Warrant Officer, is that my whole job is supposed to be technical expertise. I'm the engineer. I'm supposed to know the answer. And there's this tension between being the technical expert who gives sound advice and being a leader who has to make calls even when the technical picture isn't complete.

I've seen Warrant Officers — and I've been this guy myself — who won't commit to a course of action until they've analyzed it to death, because they're afraid of being wrong, afraid of giving bad technical advice. And I get it. If I tell the commander that a bridge will hold a certain load and I'm wrong, people can get hurt. But there's a difference between being reckless and being decisive. Being decisive doesn't mean guessing. It means making the best call you can with what you know, clearly communicating what you're certain of and what you're not, and being ready to adjust.

There was another situation a few months back where I got caught in this exact trap. We were planning a demolition range, and I was working up the safety template for the explosives we'd be using. There was a question about the safe distance for a particular charge size, and the manual I was referencing had two different tables that gave two different distances. One was more conservative, one was less. I spent two days trying to figure out which one applied to our specific scenario, digging through references, sending emails to people who might know.

And finally the NCO I was working with, a guy who's been doing demo for fifteen years, said to me, "Sir, just use the bigger number and let's move on." And he was right. I didn't need to resolve the ambiguity perfectly. I needed to make a call that was safe and defensible and let us get back to planning the actual training. I picked the more conservative distance, documented why, and we moved forward.

That's decisiveness. It's not recklessness. It's not ignoring the technical piece. It's gathering what you can, applying sound judgment, and making the call.

Here's where I think this applies to most of us, whether you're in uniform or not. You've got a decision in front of you that you've been stalling on. Maybe it's a personnel decision — somebody on your team who isn't working out, and you keep waiting for one more conversation, one more chance, one more data point before you make the call. Maybe it's a financial decision, a business decision, a family decision. Maybe it's something in your walk with God — a prompting you've felt that you keep putting off because you're not sure, you're not ready, you want more clarity.

And I'm not saying rush into things. I'm not saying don't gather information or don't seek counsel. But I am saying that at some point, you have to decide. And that point is usually sooner than you think.

So here's the question I want you to sit with tonight, the one I'm sitting with myself: what decision have you been waiting on, and what are you actually waiting for? Are you waiting for information that's legitimately coming and will actually change your decision? Or are you waiting for a level of certainty that doesn't exist, and in the meantime the cost of indecision is piling up?

If it's the latter, and I think it is for most of us more often than we'd like to admit, then the move is this: gather what you can reasonably gather in the time you actually have. Seek counsel if you need it, but don't use that as a stalling tactic. Make the best call you can make with what you know. Communicate it clearly. And then be ready to adjust as you get more information.

You will not have perfect clarity. That's not the standard. The standard is faithful, timely action with the light you've been given.
