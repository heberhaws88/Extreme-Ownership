I was sitting in a sacrament meeting a few months ago, and the speaker was talking about the Atonement, and he made this point that didn't land the way he probably meant it to. He said something like, "We just need to let Jesus do His work — our job is to get out of the way and let Him lead." And I thought about that for a minute, and I realized — no, that's not actually how it works. That's not what the Savior asks of us. He doesn't ask us to just stand back and wait. He asks us to follow. To act. To bring what we have to the table and let Him magnify it. And that means we have a role. We have responsibility. And a big part of that responsibility is staying in communication with Him, making sure we understand what He's actually asking, not what we think He's asking. Because there's a difference.

Tonight I want to talk about chapter ten of Extreme Ownership, Leading Up and Down the Chain of Command. The core idea is this: Leadership isn't just about managing the people under you. It's also about managing up — making sure the people above you have the information and context they need to make good decisions, and making sure you actually understand their guidance instead of just assuming you've got it figured out. If something's unclear, you ask. If your boss or your commander or your leader doesn't have the full picture, you give it to them. You don't just sit there and complain that they don't get it. You take ownership of that communication gap. You lead up.

And tonight I want to look at that through the lens of discipleship. What does it look like to lead up and down the chain of command when Jesus Christ is the one at the top of that chain?

Let me start with the up part, because I think that's where most of us get it wrong. We think we're supposed to just obey, just execute, just do what we're told. And sure, obedience matters. But the Savior doesn't ask for blind obedience. He asks for informed, intentional discipleship. He asks questions. He invites questions. Think about the brother of Jared in the Book of Mormon. The Lord tells him to build barges to cross the ocean. The brother of Jared builds them, but then he's got a problem: the barges are dark inside, and they need air, and they need light. So he goes back to the Lord and says, essentially, "I've built what You asked me to build, but I've got these problems, and I don't know how to solve them — what do You want me to do?" And the Lord doesn't rebuke him for asking. He works with him. He lets him propose a solution. And when the brother of Jared brings those stones, molten out of rock, and asks the Lord to touch them and make them shine, the Lord does it. That's leading up. That's taking ownership of the gap between the guidance you've been given and the reality you're facing, and closing that gap by communicating.

I think we skip that step a lot. I know I do. I get a prompting, or I hear counsel from a church leader, or I read a scripture, and I think I know what it means. And I just run with it. I don't stop to say, "Father, is this what You meant? Is this actually what You're asking, or is this what I want to do and I'm just slapping Your name on it?" I assume. And assumptions are where communication breaks down.

A few months ago I was praying about a decision in my business. I won't get into all the details, but I was looking at whether to take on a particular project — it was a big job, good money, but it was going to stretch my crew thin and it was going to mean a lot of nights and weekends away from my family. And I prayed about it. And I felt… something. Not a clear answer, just this kind of uneasy feeling. But I told myself it was just nerves. I told myself the Lord wouldn't give me an opportunity like this if He didn't want me to take it. So I took it. And about two weeks in, it became very clear that I had made the wrong call. The job was a mess. The client kept changing specs. One of my guys got hurt — nothing serious, thank God, but it rattled all of us. And I was so stretched I wasn't sleeping, wasn't present at home, wasn't able to be the dad or the husband I needed to be. And one night I was sitting in my truck after a sixteen-hour day, and I just said out loud, "Why didn't You stop me?"

And the answer that came back, not audibly but clear as anything, was: "I tried. You didn't ask."

That stopped me cold. Because He was right. I hadn't asked. I'd felt that unease and I'd explained it away instead of going back and saying, "Father, I need clarity here. What are You actually asking me to do? Because I don't want to assume." I hadn't led up. I'd just run with what I thought was the answer and then blamed Him when it fell apart.

Leading up the chain of command, when the one at the top of that chain is the Lord, means you take ownership of making sure you understand His will. You ask. You clarify. You bring Him the full picture of what you're facing, and you don't just assume He already knows so you don't need to say it. Of course He knows. But He wants you in the conversation. He wants you engaged. He wants you to bring your agency and your effort and your questions to the table, because that's how you grow. That's how you become more like Him.

There's a verse in Doctrine and Covenants, section nine, where the Lord is teaching Oliver Cowdery about translation, and He says, "You have not understood; you have supposed that I would give it unto you, when you took no thought save it was to ask me. But, behold, I say unto you, that you must study it out in your mind; then you must ask me if it be right." Study it out. Do the work. Engage your mind and your effort. And then bring it to Him and ask if you're on the right track. That's leading up. That's taking ownership of the communication. Not just passively waiting for Him to tell you every step, but actively engaging with Him, bringing what you've got, asking for confirmation or correction.

Now let me flip it. Leading down the chain of command in discipleship. What does that look like? Because if you're a disciple of Christ, you're not just following Him — you're also leading others to Him. You're a parent, a home teacher, a ministering brother or sister, a friend, a coworker. People are watching you. And one of the ways you lead them is by making sure they have what they need. You don't hoard information. You don't assume they'll figure it out. You give context. You share what you've learned. You make the path clearer for the people coming behind you.

The Savior did this constantly. He didn't just tell the apostles what to do — He explained why. He gave them context. He washed their feet and then He said, "Do you understand what I've done for you?" He taught them the principle behind the action. And when they didn't understand, He taught again. He didn't get frustrated and say, "You should've gotten it the first time." He took ownership of making sure they had what they needed.

I've got three kids. My oldest is twelve. And I've been trying, probably failing more than succeeding, to lead him not just by telling him what to do, but by making sure he understands why. A few weeks ago he was dragging his feet on his homework. Just completely avoiding it. And my first instinct was to just lay down the law: get it done, no discussion. But I stopped and I thought, he's not being defiant, he's being avoidant, and there's a difference. So I sat down with him and I asked him what was going on. Turns out he didn't understand the assignment. He'd been staring at it for an hour and he was so embarrassed that he didn't get it that he just shut down. He thought he should've been able to figure it out on his own.

And I thought, man, I do the same thing. How many times have I avoided something because I was embarrassed I didn't already know how to do it? How many times have I not asked for help because I thought I should've had it figured out by now?

So I told him, "Hey, not knowing something isn't a failure. Not asking when you don't know — that's the failure." And we worked through the assignment together. And I realized, that's leading down. That's taking ownership of making sure he has the context and the tools he needs, not just barking orders and expecting him to comply.

In the church we talk a lot about ministering. And I think sometimes we make it too complicated. Ministering is just leading down. It's making sure the people in your care, the people the Lord has put in your path, have what they need. Information. Support. A listening ear. It's not assuming they're fine just because they haven't said otherwise. It's checking in. It's saying, "Hey, how are you actually doing? What can I do to help?" And then actually doing it.

I've got a guy I minister to, he's in his seventies, lives alone, his wife passed a few years ago. And for a long time I would just text him once a month, make sure he was still breathing, check the box. I wasn't really engaged. And then one day I actually stopped by his house, and we got to talking, and he mentioned he was having trouble with his furnace. It wasn't heating right. And I said, "Why didn't you call somebody?" And he said, "I didn't want to bother anyone." And I thought, he doesn't know that he's not a bother. He doesn't know that I would've come over in a heartbeat if he'd just asked. So I went and looked at his furnace, and it was a simple fix, took me twenty minutes. And I told him, "Next time something breaks, you call me. Don't wait. Don't assume you're bothering me. That's what I'm here for."

That's leading down. That's taking ownership of making sure he knows he's not alone, that he's got support, that he doesn't have to just quietly struggle because he doesn't want to be a burden.

The Savior did the same thing in Gethsemane. And this is the part that just wrecks me every time I think about it. He's in the garden. He knows what's coming. And He asks Peter and James and John to stay awake with Him. He doesn't need them to fix anything. He's not asking them to take the cup from Him. He's just asking them to be there. To watch. To stay engaged. And they fall asleep. Three times. And He doesn't rebuke them harshly. He just says, "Could you not watch with me one hour?" There's sorrow in that question. Not anger. Sorrow. Because He wanted them there. He wanted them engaged. And they didn't understand what He needed from them.

I think about that when I think about leading down. Are the people under me, the people I'm supposed to be leading, do they know what I need from them? Have I made it clear? Or have I just assumed they should know, and then gotten frustrated when they didn't deliver?

In my business, I've got a crew of five guys. And there was a stretch last year where I was constantly irritated because they weren't staying on top of things the way I thought they should. Tools weren't getting cleaned and put away at the end of the day. Supplies weren't getting restocked. Little things, but they added up. And I was stewing about it, getting more and more frustrated, until one day one of my guys, one of the younger apprentices, he just looked at me and said, "Boss, I don't think we know what you want. You're upset, but we don't know what we're supposed to be doing differently."

And he was right. I hadn't told them. I'd just assumed they should know. I'd assumed it was obvious. But it wasn't. So I called a crew meeting, and I laid out exactly what my expectations were. I explained why it mattered — not just "because I said so," but here's how it affects the next job, here's how it affects our reputation, here's how it affects whether we're profitable or not. I gave them context. I led down. And you know what? It got better. Not perfect, but better. Because they knew what I needed and why.

Leading up and down the chain of command in discipleship means you take ownership of communication in both directions. You make sure the Lord has your full honesty and your real questions, not just the sanitized version of yourself you think He wants to see. And you make sure the people you're leading, your family, the people you minister to, whoever, you make sure they have what they need from you. You don't hoard information. You don't assume they'll just figure it out. You engage.

Here's the thing I keep coming back to: the Savior could have led from a distance. He could have just issued commands and expected obedience. But He didn't. He came down. He walked with us. He washed feet. He asked questions. He gave context. He made Himself available. And when He went to the Father, He didn't just submit blindly — He brought His whole self. He said, "If it be possible, let this cup pass from me." He was honest about what He was facing. And then He said, "Nevertheless, not my will, but thine, be done." That's leading up. That's engaging fully and then trusting fully.

So here's what I'm taking into tomorrow, and here's what I'm asking you to think about: Where have you been assuming instead of asking? Is there something you think the Lord is asking of you, but you haven't actually stopped to confirm it? Have you brought it to Him, studied it out, and asked if you're on the right track? Or are you just running with what you think is right and hoping it works out?

And on the other side: Who are you leading, and do they actually know what you need from them? Your kids, your spouse, the people you minister to, the people who look to you — have you given them the context and the clarity they need, or are you just frustrated that they're not reading your mind?

Take one of those questions into tomorrow. Pick one. Leading up or leading down. And do something about it. Have the conversation you've been avoiding. Ask the question you've been too proud or too scared to ask. Give the context you've been assuming didn't need to be said.

Because leadership, real leadership, the kind the Savior modeled, it's not about being the smartest or the strongest or the one with all the answers. It's about taking ownership of the space between where you are and where you need to be, and closing that gap through honest, intentional communication. Up and down the chain.
