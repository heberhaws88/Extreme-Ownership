I spent three hours Sunday afternoon writing up this week's schedule. Jobs assigned, crews matched to the work, materials ordered, permit pickups penciled in. Monday morning I handed out the assignments, sent everyone out, felt like I had my act together for once. By Wednesday noon the whole thing was in the dumpster. One job ran over because the panel we quoted wasn't actually big enough for the load calculation once we got into it. Another job we couldn't even start because the homeowner's contractor hadn't framed the garage like he said he would. I had an apprentice call in sick, which meant pulling a guy off one crew to cover another, which put that first crew behind. And I spent most of Tuesday chasing down a supplier who shorted us on a material order instead of doing the site visit I was supposed to do. By Thursday I was just reacting, putting out fires, moving people around like chess pieces, hoping we'd land somewhere close to on schedule by Friday. We didn't.

That's not planning. That's just writing things down and hoping.

Tonight's principle from Extreme Ownership is Plan. The idea is straightforward but harder than it sounds. A real plan isn't a wish list or a schedule you hope works out. It's the product of actually analyzing the mission, identifying the most likely problems before they happen, and building contingencies so when something goes sideways you're not scrambling. And just as important, a good plan is something the whole team understands and has helped shape. It's not you handing down orders from on high. It's you leading a planning process that gets everyone's brain in the game so they know not just what to do, but why, and what to do when the original what doesn't work.

Jocko Willink and Leif Babin lay this out in chapter nine. They're talking about combat operations, but the core idea applies whether you're clearing a building in Ramadi or running a three-man crew rewiring a commercial tenant space. You've got to think it through. You've got to war-game it. You've got to ask what can go wrong and have an answer ready. And you've got to make sure your people aren't just taking orders, they're actually part of the plan, so when you're not standing over their shoulder they can make good calls because they understand the mission.

I'll be honest, that's where I fall short most often. I plan in my head, or I plan on paper but I don't loop the crew in on the why, or I plan for the job as I want it to be instead of the job as it probably will be. And then I end up frustrated when reality doesn't cooperate and my guys don't read my mind.

Before I get into the main story tonight, I want to take a minute here on the faith side of this, because planning is actually a deeply Christian posture even though it doesn't always feel that way. There's this tension in Scripture. On one hand, James warns us not to boast about tomorrow because we don't even know what tomorrow will bring. We're a mist that appears for a little while and then vanishes. On the other hand, Proverbs is full of verses about the diligent man, the wise man who plans ahead, the fool who doesn't consider the future. Jesus Himself tells a parable about a guy who starts building a tower without counting the cost first and can't finish it, and everyone mocks him. So which is it? Do we plan or do we just trust God and wing it?

The answer is both, and the tension is the point. Planning isn't about controlling outcomes. It's about stewarding what God has put in front of you. It's about honoring the people who depend on you by thinking ahead instead of assuming it'll all just work out. My crew, my clients, my family, they're not served by me shrugging and saying, "Well, God will provide," when what I really mean is I didn't want to do the hard work of thinking it through. God gives us brains. He gives us experience. He puts us in positions of responsibility. Planning is using those gifts. But at the same time, every plan I make, I hold loosely, because I am not in control of outcomes. I can do everything right and still have the job go wrong. That's where trust comes in. I plan like it depends on me, I execute like it depends on me, and I trust God with the results. That's the balance. Extreme Ownership doesn't mean I think I'm sovereign. It means I take responsibility for what is mine to own, and then I trust God with what isn't.

Alright. Let me tell you about a job that went off the rails because my planning was garbage, and what I learned from it.

This was about a year and a half ago. I landed a remodel project, good-sized house, the clients wanted to gut the kitchen, add a bathroom upstairs, upgrade the panel, rewire a bunch of old knob-and-tube in the walls while we were at it. It was the kind of job that would keep a two-man crew busy for a solid three weeks, maybe four. Good money. I bid it, we agreed on a price, I scheduled my best journeyman and one of my apprentices to handle it. I figured I'd check in every few days, but mostly they could run it. I had other jobs to manage.

Here's what I did for planning. I walked the house with the client, took some notes, sketched out roughly what needed to happen, and gave my guys a start date. That's it. I didn't sit down with them and walk through the scope. I didn't talk through the order of operations. I didn't ask my journeyman, who'd been doing this for fifteen years, if he saw any potential problems I'd missed. I didn't identify what materials we'd need when, so half the time they were waiting on me to bring stuff or make a supply run. I didn't clarify with the client what decisions needed to be made before we could move forward on certain parts of the job, so we'd get to a point and have to stop and wait for her to pick out a fixture or tell us where she wanted an outlet. I didn't build in any buffer for the inevitable stuff you find when you open up old walls. I just said, "Here's the job, you guys know what you're doing, go."

And they did know what they were doing. That's the only reason it didn't fall apart faster than it did. But knowing what you're doing and having a plan are not the same thing.

About a week in, I started getting calls. My journeyman needed to know if we were responsible for patching drywall or just the electrical. I didn't know, I hadn't clarified that in the contract. He needed a material I hadn't ordered yet. He ran into a junction box buried in a wall that wasn't on any drawing and had to figure out what it fed before he could move forward. The client called me upset because they'd cut a hole for a can light and she didn't realize it was going to be there, she thought it was going somewhere else. I hadn't walked her through the lighting plan in detail. My apprentice, good kid, eager, but green, made a call on some wire routing that wasn't wrong exactly but created more work than it needed to because no one had talked through with him how we were approaching the rough-in overall.

By week two we were behind. By week three the client was frustrated, my guys were frustrated, I was frustrated. The job that I'd bid for three to four weeks dragged into six. I ate the extra labor cost because I couldn't in good conscience bill them for my failure to plan. I still made some money on the job, but not much, and more importantly I burned trust with a client who could've referred me to other people but probably won't, and I put my crew in a position where they were trying to do good work without a clear picture of what good looked like.

That was on me. I didn't plan. I winged it.

Here's what I should have done. I should have scheduled a real planning meeting before we started. Get my journeyman in the room, maybe even the apprentice, walk through the whole scope together. Let them ask questions. Let them point out things I missed. My journeyman's been in more houses than I have, he sees things I don't see. If I'd brought him in on the planning, he might've said, "Hey, if we're opening up that wall anyway, we should plan to replace this other circuit while we're at it, save us coming back later." Or, "Do we know if they want any low-voltage while we're in there? We should ask now." I should have gone through the job phase by phase, identified what materials we'd need for each phase, and made sure we had them staged and ready. I should have built in time for the unknowns, because there are always unknowns in remodel work. I should have clarified scope with the client up front, in detail, so we all knew who was responsible for what and there were no surprises. I should have walked my apprentice through the overall approach so he wasn't just taking instructions task by task but actually understood how his piece fit into the whole.

And here's the kicker. If I'd done all that, if I'd actually planned, it wouldn't have just made the job go smoother. It would have made my guys better. Because they would've been thinking, not just doing. They would've understood the mission. And when something came up that I hadn't anticipated, which it will, they would've been able to make a good call because they understood the intent.

That's what Extreme Ownership means by planning. It's not about having a perfect schedule that accounts for everything. You can't account for everything. It's about doing the hard work of thinking it through, getting the right people involved in that thinking, and building enough shared understanding that when reality punches you in the mouth, your team can adapt without falling apart.

I'll tell you what changed after that job. I started doing pre-job meetings for anything bigger than a service call. We sit down, we walk through the scope, we talk about order of operations, we identify what we need and when, we talk about what could go wrong. I ask my guys, "What am I missing? What do you see that I don't see?" And I actually listen. Half the time they catch something I overlooked. I also started building contingency into my bids and my schedules. Not padding, but honest contingency. If I think a job will take three weeks, I tell the client four, because something will come up. That's not pessimism, that's realism. And it means when we finish in three and a half weeks, they're happy we're early instead of annoyed we're late.

I also started being more intentional about clarifying scope and expectations with clients up front. I've got a walkthrough checklist now that I go through with them before we start. Where do you want outlets, how many, what height? Where do you want switches? Do you want dimmers? What about USB outlets? What about low-voltage for internet or speakers or security? Do you have the fixtures picked out or do you need help with that? Who's doing drywall, who's doing paint, what's our access look like, where can we stage material? It feels like overkill sometimes, but I can't tell you how many problems it's headed off. Because if we have that conversation up front, we have a plan. If we don't, we're just guessing and hoping, and that's not fair to them or to my crew.

The other thing I learned is that planning isn't a one-time thing. You plan before the job, but you also re-plan as you go. Every morning my guys know to check in, even if it's just a thirty-second call. "Here's what we're doing today, here's what we'll need, here's what might slow us down." If something changes, we adjust the plan. We don't just push forward and hope it works out. That's the difference between a plan and a wish.

I still screw this up, by the way. I still have weeks like the one I described at the start, where I think I've planned and really I've just made a list. I still sometimes skip the pre-job meeting because I'm busy and I think, "Ah, this one's straightforward, they've got it." And then it's not straightforward, and I'm annoyed at myself because I knew better. Extreme Ownership isn't about being perfect. It's about owning it when you're not. So when I skip the planning and the job goes sideways, I don't blame the crew, I don't blame the client, I don't blame bad luck. I own it. I didn't plan. That's on me. And then I do better next time.

Here's what I want you to chew on tonight. Think about something you're responsible for, whether that's a job, a project, a role you're in, whatever. Did you actually plan for it? Or did you just react to it? And if you did plan, who else should have had a voice in that plan who didn't? Because one of the sneakiest ways we fail at planning is we do it alone. We think it's our job to have all the answers, so we don't bring other people in. And we miss things. We miss what they see, we miss what they know, and we miss the chance to get them actually invested in the mission instead of just following orders.

So here's the action. Tomorrow, pick one thing on your plate this week and actually plan for it. Don't just write down what you're going to do. Think it through. What's the mission? What could go wrong? What do you need? Who needs to be involved? And then bring those people in. Have the conversation. Ask the questions. Build the plan together. See what happens.

I'm not saying it'll go perfectly. Plans never survive contact with reality unchanged. But you'll be way better off with a plan that has to flex than you will be just winging it and hoping for the best.
