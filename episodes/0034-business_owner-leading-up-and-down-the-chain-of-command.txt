I'm standing in a customer's garage at four thirty on a Friday afternoon, and my phone rings. It's the general contractor I've been working with on a strip-mall remodel across town. Before he even says hello, I can hear it in his voice — something's wrong.

He doesn't waste time. He says the inspector red-tagged the panel we installed yesterday. Says the bonding's wrong, says we need to tear it out and redo it before Monday or the whole project falls behind schedule and he starts losing money. He's not yelling, but he's close.

And here's the thing — I wasn't on that job yesterday. I sent one of my guys, a journeyman who's been with me for three years, solid worker, knows his stuff. I trusted him to handle it. But standing here in this garage, I realize I never actually told the general contractor that I wouldn't be there myself. I just assumed he knew I can't be everywhere at once anymore. And I definitely didn't check in with my guy before he started to make sure we were both crystal clear on what the inspector would be looking for on a commercial panel in that jurisdiction, which has some quirks.

So now I've got an angry contractor, a panicked journeyman who's going to have to work all weekend, and a mess that's going to cost me money and reputation. And the hard truth I'm sitting with right now is this — I failed to lead up and I failed to lead down, both at the same time.

That's what we're talking about tonight. Chapter ten of Extreme Ownership by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin — leading up and down the chain of command. The core idea is simple but easy to miss: leadership is not just about telling the people who work for you what to do. It's also about managing up, about making sure the people above you — your clients, your general contractors, whoever's writing the checks or setting the expectations — have the information and context they need to make good decisions. And it's about asking instead of assuming when you're not sure what's expected, even if asking makes you feel dumb or like you should already know.

You lead down by giving clear guidance. You lead up by keeping the people above you informed and by pulling clarity out of them when it's missing. Most of us are decent at one or the other. Doing both at the same time, on the same project, consistently — that's where it gets hard.

Before I get into what happened with that panel and how I had to own it, I want to spend a minute on what this principle has to do with following Jesus Christ, because that's the foundation under everything else for me.

Jesus taught us to be faithful stewards. A steward is someone who manages something that belongs to someone else — and that's what all of us are, whether we're running a business, leading a platoon, or raising kids. Everything we have, all of it, is the Lord's. We're just managing it for a season. And stewardship requires two things that map directly onto this principle: it requires us to serve the people we're responsible for, and it requires us to give an honest account to the one we answer to.

In the parable of the talents, the servants who invested what they were given didn't just bury it and hope for the best. They worked with it, they took risks, they managed down. But when the master came back, they also gave a clear, honest report — they managed up. The servant who buried his talent and then made excuses when the master returned, he failed at both. He didn't lead his work, and he didn't communicate truthfully with the one he answered to.

When I think about leading up in the context of faith, I think about prayer. Real prayer, not the grocery-list kind. Prayer where I'm actually reporting to the Lord about where I am, what I'm struggling with, what I need help seeing clearly. Prayer where I'm asking instead of assuming I know what he wants from me in a given situation. And I think about leading down — serving my family, my employees, the people the Lord has put in my path — with the same clarity and care I'd want if I were in their shoes.

Following Christ means I don't get to just manage the easy parts. I don't get to only lead the people below me and ignore the harder work of keeping the people above me informed, or asking the uncomfortable questions when I don't understand what's expected. Stewardship is a chain of command, and I'm somewhere in the middle of it, accountable both ways.

Alright. Back to that panel.

So here's how it actually went down. I've got a small electrical contracting business — just me and usually three or four guys, depending on the season. For the first few years, I was on every job, every day. I was the one bending pipe, pulling wire, talking to inspectors. But the business grew, and at some point I had to start trusting other people to run jobs without me, or I was never going to scale past being a one-man show with helpers.

This particular job was a tenant improvement, basically gutting and rewiring a couple of retail spaces in a strip mall. The general contractor's a guy I've worked with before, good relationship, he throws me work pretty regularly. He called me for the bid, we walked the space together, I gave him a number, he accepted it. In my head, this was my job. I was the one he hired.

But when it came time to actually do the work, I had two other projects that week that needed me more — one was a tricky service upgrade where the homeowner kept changing his mind about what he wanted, and the other was a troubleshooting call that turned into a whole-house rewire once we opened the walls and saw the mess behind them. So I sent my journeyman to the strip mall with an apprentice, gave him the plans, told him to handle the rough-in and the panel set, and I'd check in later.

I didn't tell the general contractor that I wasn't going to be there. It didn't even occur to me that I needed to. In my mind, my guy's a licensed journeyman, he's competent, sending him is the same as me being there. But in the general contractor's mind, he hired me. He expected me. And when something went wrong and he showed up to check progress and saw someone else running the job, that was the first crack.

The second crack was that I didn't actually sit down with my journeyman before he started and go through the specs in detail. I handed him the plan set, I said get the rough-in done and set the panel, and I assumed he'd read the notes about bonding and grounding, which are a little different in that city's commercial code than they are in residential. He's done commercial work before. I figured he knew.

He didn't ask. I didn't check. We both assumed.

So he sets the panel the way he's always set panels, clean work, good work, but not exactly to the spec the inspector wanted. Inspector comes through, sees it, red-tags it. Now my guy calls me, and he's stressed because he knows this is bad and he's not sure what he missed, and I'm trying to walk him through it over the phone, and then the general contractor calls me separately, frustrated because his schedule just got blown up and he doesn't understand why I wasn't there in the first place.

And here's where I have to own both sides of it.

Leading down: I did not give my journeyman the guidance he needed. I handed him a job and assumed he'd extract all the critical details from the plan set on his own. I didn't walk him through the commercial bonding requirements for that jurisdiction. I didn't make sure he knew to call me before he landed the panel if he had any questions. I didn't set him up to win. I set him up to do his best and hope it was enough. That's not leadership. That's delegation without support.

Leading up: I did not manage my relationship with the general contractor. I didn't tell him I wouldn't be on-site. I didn't explain that I was sending a qualified journeyman and that the work would be to the same standard. I didn't give him a chance to say, hey, actually, I need you there personally, or I need to know who's running this. I just assumed he'd be fine with it. I didn't ask. I didn't communicate. I left him in the dark, and when something went wrong, he felt blindsided, because he was.

If I'd led up properly, I would've called him earlier in the week and said, listen, I've got a couple of jobs that need me on-site right now, so I'm sending my lead journeyman to handle your rough-in. He's good, he's licensed, I trust him. If that's a problem for you, tell me now and I'll figure something else out. That conversation takes two minutes. It would've set the expectation. It would've given him the information he needed. And if he'd said, no, I really need you there, then I would've known, and I could've made a different call about how to cover my jobs that week.

And if I'd led down properly, I would've sat down with my guy the morning of, gone through the plan notes, highlighted the bonding detail, maybe even called the inspector's office to confirm what they'd be looking for. I would've told him, if anything seems weird or unclear, call me before you make the call, not after. I would've checked in halfway through the day, not just assumed it was going fine.

I did neither of those things. I let both relationships drift on assumption. And assumption is where leadership goes to die.

So what did I do? I ate it. I called the general contractor back, and I said, this is on me. I should've told you I wasn't going to be there. I should've made sure my guy had the details he needed. We're going to fix it, we're going to make it right, and it's not going to cost you anything or push your schedule any further back than Monday. I didn't make excuses. I didn't blame my journeyman. I didn't say, well, the plan notes were unclear, or the inspector's being unreasonable. I owned it.

Then I called my journeyman, and I said, I didn't set you up to succeed on this one. I should've walked you through the bonding requirements before you started. This is my fault, not yours. But here's what we're going to do — we're going to pull that panel tomorrow morning, we're going to redo the bonding exactly to spec, and we're going to get it re-inspected first thing Monday. I'll be there with you. We'll do it together. And going forward, anytime I send you on a commercial job, we're going to sit down first and go through the specs line by line, so this doesn't happen again.

He felt bad. I felt bad. But we fixed it. We pulled the panel Saturday morning, redid the bonding, I called the inspector personally and walked him through what we'd corrected, and we passed on Monday. The general contractor was still annoyed, but he respected that I owned it and fixed it fast, and he's kept calling me for work since then.

But here's the thing — I didn't just lose money on that job, which I did. I lost something more valuable. I lost a weekend. I lost my journeyman's confidence for a little while. I stressed out my wife, because I had to cancel plans we'd made so I could go fix a panel on a Saturday. And all of that could've been avoided if I'd just led up and down at the same time, instead of assuming both sides would be fine without me.

The hardest part of leading up, at least for me, is that it feels like showing weakness. It feels like I'm admitting I don't have it all together. When I call a general contractor and say, hey, I need to clarify what you're expecting here, or I won't be on this job myself, there's a part of me that worries he's going to think I'm disorganized, or that I don't know what I'm doing, or that I'm too small-time to handle his work. But the truth is the opposite. Professionals communicate. Professionals ask questions. Professionals keep the people they answer to informed. Amateurs assume and hope it works out.

And the hardest part of leading down is that it takes time I don't always feel like I have. It's faster to just say, go do the thing, and trust that it'll be fine. But fast and effective are not the same. Taking ten minutes to walk someone through the details, to make sure they know what good looks like, to tell them it's okay to call me if something's unclear — that's not wasted time. That's leadership. And it saves way more time than it costs when it prevents a callback or a redo or a red tag.

Here's what I'd ask you to think about, whether you run a business, manage a team, or just have people above and below you in any part of your life: Where are you assuming right now instead of asking? Where have you left someone in the dark — either someone who works for you or someone you answer to — because you didn't want to seem like you didn't have it together, or because you were busy, or because you just hoped it would work out?

Leading up means you keep the people above you informed, even when it's uncomfortable. It means you ask for clarification when the guidance is unclear, even if you feel like you should already know. It means you give them the information they need to make good decisions, instead of waiting for them to dig it out of you when something goes wrong.

Leading down means you give the people you're responsible for the clarity and support they need to succeed. It means you check in. It means you don't just delegate and disappear. It means you set them up to win, not just to try.

Both directions. Same time. That's the standard.

Tomorrow, pick one relationship where you're in the middle of the chain — someone above you, someone below you — and ask yourself if you're actually leading in both directions, or if you're coasting on assumption. Then do the hard thing. Have the conversation. Ask the question. Give the information. Own the gap.
