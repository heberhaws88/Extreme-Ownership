Good evening.

I've been thinking about the night the Savior washed His apostles' feet.

Most of the time when that story comes up, we talk about humility, about service, about being willing to do the work nobody else wants to do. And all of that's true. But there's something else there that I think we miss sometimes. Peter didn't want the Lord to wash his feet. He objected. And the Savior told him, essentially, if I don't do this for you, you can't be part of what we're doing together. You have no part with me.

That's not just about humility. That's about interdependence. That's about the fact that Peter couldn't do his job unless the Savior did His. The whole mission — the whole kingdom of God — required every person in the room to support the others, to make them successful, even when it looked like it wasn't their job.

Tonight we're talking about cover and move.

It's a term from combat, specifically from close quarters battle. When one element of a team is moving forward, another element is covering them — providing suppressive fire, watching their flanks, making sure they can advance without getting cut down. Then the roles reverse. The element that just moved sets up and covers while the first element moves. Nobody gets anywhere alone. The entire team succeeds together or they fail together, because each part of the team is responsible for the success of every other part.

Jocko Willink and Leif Babin, in their book Extreme Ownership, talk about this as one of the core laws of combat leadership. They make it clear — this isn't optional. It's not a nice-to-have when you're feeling generous. If you're running a team and you let one part operate in a silo, treating another part's mission as not their problem, you're setting the whole operation up to fail. The SEAL platoon doesn't win if one squad makes it to the objective and the other gets pinned down. They both make it or the mission's a bust.

Now, in the business world, in the family, in church service, we don't use those exact terms. But the principle is identical. If I'm running my electrical business and I treat the office admin's problems as her problem, not mine, because I'm out in the field and she's at a desk — I'm violating cover and move. If I'm at home and I think getting the kids to bed is my wife's lane so I'm off the hook when she's struggling — I'm violating cover and move. If I'm in the Guard and I treat another section's equipment shortfall as their issue to solve, not something that affects the whole unit's readiness — same thing.

Cover and move says every part of the team is responsible for the success of every other part. Not just my lane. Not just my job description. The whole thing.

And tonight I want to talk about what that looks like through the lens of following Jesus Christ, because I think the Savior demonstrated this principle more completely than any leader who's ever lived.

Take Gethsemane. The Atonement itself is the ultimate act of cover and move, if you're willing to look at it that way. The Savior didn't have to be there. He had no personal sin, no personal failure that required that suffering. But He was covering for us. He was taking on something that wasn't His so that we could complete the mission — so we could get home. He wasn't standing back saying, well, that's their problem to solve, I did my part by teaching them. He moved into the gap. He took ownership of the thing that would've stopped us cold.

And if you go back to the scriptures, you see this pattern over and over. In Mosiah, when Alma's people are in bondage, the Lord doesn't just free Alma and let everyone else figure it out. He strengthens them all. He covers them while they move. In Doctrine and Covenants section four it says if ye have desires to serve God ye are called to the work — and then it lists all these attributes you need, and then it says, remember faith, virtue, knowledge, temperance, patience, brotherly kindness, godliness, charity, humility, diligence. It's not a solo list. Those are team attributes. You can't build the kingdom alone. You're there to make other people successful in their callings, and they're there to make you successful in yours.

Paul, in First Corinthians twelve, talks about the body of Christ. He says the eye can't say to the hand, I don't need you. The head can't say to the feet, I don't need you. If one part suffers, all the parts suffer with it. If one part is honored, all the parts rejoice. That's cover and move in the church. That's the opposite of silo thinking. You don't get to coast in your calling while another part of the ward is struggling and say, well, that's the elders quorum president's problem, or that's the Relief Society's lane, I'm just here to pass the sacrament.

This is the part where I have to be honest about where I've failed at this.

A few months ago, my wife was drowning. She didn't use that word, but I could see it. Our daughter had been sick, the kind of sick where she's up half the night and cranky all day, and my wife was trying to keep the house running, keep the boys on track with school stuff, and also prepare a lesson for her calling. And I came home one evening, and I could just see it in her face. She was running on empty.

And here's what I did. I asked if she needed anything. She said no, she was fine. And I took that answer and I went downstairs and I worked on a bid for a commercial job I've got coming up, because I had my own pressures, my own deadlines, my own lane to stay in. I told myself I'd offered, she said no, so I was covered.

That's not cover and move. That's silo thinking. That's treating her struggle as her problem to solve, because my job is to provide and run the business and her job is to manage the home. Except we're not two separate elements operating independently. We're one team. Her success is my success. Her failure — or her getting pinned down by a sick kid and a calling talk and three boys who won't stay in bed — is my failure, because I didn't move into the gap and cover for her.

What I should have done is ignored the answer. Not in a bulldozing way, but in a reading-the-situation way. I should have said, hey, I'm putting the boys to bed tonight, you go work on your lesson or just sit down for twenty minutes. I should have done the dishes. I should have recognized that when one part of the team is under fire, the other part doesn't ask permission to help. You just move.

I didn't do that. And the next day she was even more fried, and I could see the resentment starting to build, and I finally woke up and realized I'd let her fight that battle alone when I was standing right there with the ability to help.

So I owned it. I told her I'd dropped the ball. I told her I saw she was underwater and I didn't step in, and that was on me. And then I asked her what she needed, and this time I didn't take no for an answer. I took the boys to my mom's for the afternoon so she could have some quiet. I handled bedtime for the next week. I picked up some of the household stuff that I'd been treating as her lane.

And here's the thing — it wasn't a grand gesture. It wasn't some big heroic move. It was just basic cover and move. It was recognizing that we win or lose together, and if she's losing, I'm losing, even if my part of the mission looks fine on paper.

The Savior didn't wait for us to ask for help in Gethsemane. He saw the mission, He saw what was required, and He moved into the gap. He covered us. And He asks us to do the same for each other.

In Galatians, Paul says bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfill the law of Christ. That's the law we're under. Not the law of stay in your lane. Not the law of I did my part, you do yours. The law of Christ is cover and move. It's take ownership of the success of the people around you, because you're all on the same team.

I see this in the Guard, too. We had a weekend drill a couple months back where another section was short on people. They had a vehicle deadline — maintenance that had to get done before the truck could go back in service — and they didn't have the bodies to finish it in the time they had. And my section, we'd finished our taskings for the day. We were technically good to go. We could've packed up, called it a day, headed home early.

But if that other section doesn't finish their vehicle, the whole unit's readiness takes a hit. Their failure becomes our failure when the battalion gets rated. So we stayed. We sent a couple of guys over to help them finish the work. It wasn't our truck. It wasn't our section's responsibility. But it was our unit's mission.

That's cover and move. That's what mission success looks like when you stop thinking in silos.

And here's where I think we lose this in church service sometimes. We get our calling, we get our lane, and we think our job is to execute our calling well and that's it. I'm the Sunday School teacher, so I show up and teach my lesson, and if the gospel doctrine class is struggling to get people to attend, well, that's not my problem. I'm the ward clerk, so I keep the records straight, and if the youth need more leaders, that's the youth leaders' job to figure out.

Except we're all building the same kingdom. We're all trying to get the same people back to the same Heavenly Father. If the youth program is failing, the whole ward is failing. If the new family that just moved in doesn't get fellowshipped because everyone assumed someone else would do it, we all failed. We don't get to stand at the judgment bar and say, well, I did my calling, the rest wasn't my responsibility.

The Savior didn't say that. He took responsibility for all of it. And He asks us to do the same.

I'm not saying you have to do everything. I'm not saying you ignore your own stewardship to fix everyone else's. That's not cover and move, that's chaos. Cover and move is coordinated. It's intentional. It's recognizing when another part of the team needs support and making sure they get it, even if that's not in your job description.

In my business, I've got a guy on my crew, one of my apprentices, who's good with customers. He's got that knack for explaining things in a way that doesn't make people feel dumb, and he's patient with questions. I've got another guy who's faster at rough-in work but gets awkward when the homeowner wants to talk. If I let them operate in silos — if I say, well, you're the rough-in guy so you just do rough-in, and you're the service-call guy so you just do service calls — I'm not using the team well. But if I recognize that they can cover and move for each other, that the rough-in guy can jump on a service call when we're slammed and the other guy can walk the customer through what we're doing while the first guy works, then the whole team wins. They're making each other successful.

That's what I'm trying to build in the business. And that's what I'm trying to build at home, even though I fail at it more often than I'd like to admit.

So here's the question I'm carrying into tomorrow, and the question I'm leaving you with tonight.

Who around me is struggling right now, in a way that I've been treating as not my problem?

Maybe it's someone in your family. Maybe it's someone in your ward. Maybe it's someone on your crew at work, or someone in your unit, or someone in your neighborhood. And maybe you've been thinking, well, that's their lane, that's their issue to solve. Or maybe you've been so focused on your own mission that you haven't even noticed they're pinned down.

Cover and move says their success is your success. Their failure is your failure. You're on the same team.

So look around. Find the gap. And move into it.

Not because you're trying to be a hero. Not because you're trying to rack up service points or impress anyone. But because that's what the Savior did for you. He saw you struggling under a weight you couldn't carry, and He moved into the gap. He covered you.

And He asks you to do the same for the people around you.

That's cover and move. That's the kingdom of God. That's how we all get home.
