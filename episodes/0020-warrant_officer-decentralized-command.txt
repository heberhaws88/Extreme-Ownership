Couple months back I was standing in the motor pool at zero dark thirty, watching one of my NCOs absolutely lose his mind trying to get six different moving pieces coordinated for a convoy op we were running later that day. He had soldiers prepping vehicles, another team staged on the equipment trailer, comms checks running late, and me — breathing down his neck asking him questions about every single decision he was making. 

I thought I was helping. I thought that's what a good officer does — stays engaged, stays on top of everything. What I was actually doing was making it impossible for him to lead. Every time he turned to give an order I was there asking him to justify it, second-guessing his call, stepping in to tweak something. And I didn't realize how badly I'd screwed this up until he finally turned to me, completely frustrated, and said, "Sir, do you want me to run this or do you want to run it yourself?" 

That hit me like a brick. Because the honest answer was: I wanted him to run it, but I didn't trust him to run it without me standing over his shoulder. And that's not leadership. That's control. And control doesn't scale.

Tonight we're talking about decentralized command.

The idea is simple but brutal in how it exposes your weaknesses as a leader. You cannot directly manage every detail of every operation. You will fail if you try. So you have to push decision-making authority down to the people closest to the problem — and then you have to train them well enough and trust them enough to make good calls when you're not in the room. Jocko Willink and Leif Babin lay this out in chapter eight of Extreme Ownership. They watched it work in combat in Ramadi, and they watched leaders fail catastrophically when they tried to micromanage everything themselves. The principle is this: leaders at every level have to own their piece of the mission, and the leader at the top has to let them.

That sounds great on paper. In practice it's terrifying. Because decentralized command means letting go. It means accepting that someone else might do it differently than you would. It means living with the risk that they might screw it up. And it means owning the outcome either way — because if you pushed authority down and didn't train them right, didn't give them clear commander's intent, didn't set them up to succeed, that's still on you.

So here's where this lands for me as someone trying to follow Jesus Christ — and this beat matters tonight even though we're coming at the principle through a Guard lens, because my faith sits underneath everything else I do. 

Jesus didn't micromanage the twelve guys He chose. He taught them, He corrected them, He gave them the mission — and then He sent them out. Two by two, into towns He wasn't even going to visit Himself, to preach and heal and cast out demons in His name. And some of them screwed it up. Some of them argued about who was the greatest. One of them betrayed Him. But He still entrusted them with the work. He still gave them authority. He still expected them to own it.

That's decentralized command in the most extreme sense. He built the kingdom by raising up leaders and letting them lead, not by controlling every detail Himself. And if I'm trying to follow Him, then I have to be willing to do the same thing — at home, in my business, and absolutely in the Guard. I have to trust the people God's put in my path, train them as well as I can, and then let them own their piece of the mission. Even when it's hard. Even when I think I could do it better myself.

Alright. Back to that morning in the motor pool.

So my NCO calls me out — politely, but he calls me out — and I realized I had a choice. I could pull rank, tell him to execute and quit questioning me, reassert control. Or I could own the fact that I was the problem.

I took about three seconds, looked at him, and said, "You're right. It's yours. I'll be in the TOC if you need me." And I walked away.

Hardest hundred meters I've walked in a long time.

Because here's the thing. I'm a Warrant Officer. My whole job is to be the technical expert. I'm the engineer. I know how this equipment works, I know how to plan a route, I know how to stage a convoy. I've done this more times than most of the soldiers on my team have been alive. So every instinct I have says: stay close, keep your hands on it, make sure it's done right. 

But that instinct is poison if I let it run unchecked. Because I'm not just a technical expert anymore. I'm a leader. And leaders build other leaders. If I'm the only one who knows how to run the op, then I haven't built a team — I've built a bunch of people who can't function without me. And the second I'm not there, the second I'm pulled into a different tasking or I'm dealing with higher headquarters or I'm down with an injury, the whole thing falls apart.

Decentralized command says: that's on me. If my people can't execute without me breathing down their necks, I haven't trained them. I haven't empowered them. I haven't given them enough context to make good decisions on their own.

So I went to the TOC. I sat down. I did not check in on the radio every five minutes. I let him run it.

And you know what? He did fine. Better than fine. He got the vehicles staged on time, got comms up, got the convoy out the door exactly when we needed to. There were a couple things he did differently than I would have done them — he swapped the order of two vehicles in the line based on a comms issue I wouldn't have prioritized the same way — but his call was defensible and it worked. 

The op went smooth. We rolled out, executed the mission, came back clean. 

And here's the part that really mattered: the next time we ran a similar tasking, I didn't have to be there at all. He ran it start to finish. And the soldiers under him started stepping up too, because they saw him owning it and they knew they were expected to do the same. Decentralized command cascading down the ranks, the way it's supposed to.

But I'm not going to pretend that was the end of the story, because it wasn't. A couple months later I screwed this up again in a completely different way.

We were running a project in a training exercise — building a temporary bridge across a gap, something we'd use for a couple days and then break down. My job as the engineer officer was to oversee the whole thing, make sure it met load specs, make sure it was safe, make sure we hit the timeline. But I also had two junior Warrants on the team, and this was a perfect chance to let them run the technical piece while I handled the command and coordination side.

I knew that. I even told myself I was going to do that. And then we hit the ground and I immediately started making every technical call myself.

One of the junior guys came to me with a question about how we were going to anchor the near side, and instead of asking him what he thought or walking him through the decision, I just told him the answer. Gave him the solution, moved on. Did the same thing an hour later when we had to adjust the far-side setup because of ground conditions. Did it again when we were picking crane positions. Every single time, I defaulted to giving the answer instead of coaching them to find it.

By the end of day one, those two guys had stopped bringing me questions. They were just waiting for me to tell them what to do. And I didn't even notice until my senior NCO pulled me aside that night and said, "Sir, you've got two Warrants on this project who aren't learning a damn thing, because you're doing all their thinking for them."

That one hurt worse than the motor pool, because I should have known better. I'd already learned this lesson. But I fell right back into it, because it's faster to just give the answer. It's easier to make the call yourself than to coach someone else through making it. And when you're on a timeline and the mission matters, the temptation to centralize everything back to yourself is enormous.

But that's exactly when decentralized command matters most. Because the mission isn't just this one bridge on this one training exercise. The mission is building a team that can execute when I'm not there. The mission is raising up leaders who can think and decide and own their piece of the problem. And if I'm not doing that, I'm failing — even if the bridge gets built on time.

So that night I sat down with those two Warrants and I told them the truth. I said, "I screwed this up. I've been making all the calls and that's wrong. Tomorrow you're running the technical piece. You make the decisions, you brief me on what you're doing and why, and I'll step in if something's unsafe or off-mission. But this is yours."

And it was ugly for a couple hours. They second-guessed themselves. They came to me with questions I wouldn't have even thought were questions, because they weren't used to owning the decision. But I bit my tongue and I asked them what they thought, and I made them work through it. And by the end of day two they were running it. Not perfectly. Not the way I would have done it. But they were thinking, they were leading, and they were learning.

That's what decentralized command actually costs. It's slower at first. It's messier. You have to watch someone struggle through a problem you could solve in thirty seconds. You have to let them make a call you wouldn't make, and then trust that either it'll work or they'll learn from it when it doesn't. And the whole time, you're still responsible. If it goes sideways, it's still on you — not because you made the call, but because you're the leader and you didn't set them up to make a good one.

Jocko and Leif talk about this in the book. Decentralized command only works if you've done the work up front. You have to make sure your people understand the mission. Not just their individual task, but the why behind it — the commander's intent, the endstate, the priority. You have to make sure they know the limits of their authority, so they're not guessing about what decisions they're empowered to make. And you have to make sure they know you've got their back, so they're not afraid to act.

If you don't do that work, decentralized command is just chaos. People making decisions without context, stepping on each other, working at cross purposes, and then blaming you when it falls apart. And they're right to blame you, because you failed to lead.

But if you do the work — if you train them, if you give them the context, if you trust them — then decentralized command is the only way anything big actually gets done. Because you can't be everywhere. You can't make every call. And if you try, you will burn out and your team will never grow.

I'm still learning this. I still catch myself wanting to step in and take over, especially when I can see a better way to do something. The technical expert part of me does not want to let go. But I'm learning that my job isn't to be the smartest guy in the room. My job is to build a room full of people who can think and lead and execute without me.

So here's what I'm carrying into tomorrow, and here's what I want you to think about too.

Ask yourself: am I holding onto decisions that someone closer to the problem could and should be making? 

Not should you delegate everything. Not should you disappear and let people flounder. But are there calls you're making, tasks you're controlling, problems you're solving that you're holding onto because it's easier or faster or because you don't trust the person who should own it?

And if the answer is yes, then the follow-up question is: have I actually set them up to make that call without me? Have I explained the mission? Have I given them the context? Have I trained them? Or am I just frustrated that they don't read my mind?

Decentralized command is not abdication. It's not throwing people in the deep end and walking away. It's deliberately building leaders at every level, and then trusting them to lead.

And if you're not doing that, you're not leading a team. You're just doing everything yourself with a bunch of people watching.

And then step back and let them execute. It's going to be uncomfortable. They might do it differently than you would. They might even screw it up. But if you don't let them own it, they'll never learn. And you'll never build the team you actually need.
