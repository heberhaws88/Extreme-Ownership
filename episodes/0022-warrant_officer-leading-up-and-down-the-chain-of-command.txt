I spent most of Friday afternoon in the motor pool trying to un-mess a situation that never should have gotten messy in the first place. One of the junior NCOs had managed to schedule a generator swap at the exact same time a different section needed the rollback, and nobody knew there was a conflict until both crews showed up expecting the same piece of equipment. My phone rang, I walked over, everybody's standing around looking annoyed, and I'm thinking — how did this get past everyone involved without a single person flagging it earlier?

The answer, which took me about ten minutes to piece together, was that nobody had actually confirmed anything with anybody. The junior NCO thought he had implicit approval because nobody told him no. The other section assumed their request took priority because they'd mentioned it in passing at a meeting two weeks ago. And I hadn't checked in with either group because I figured they'd come to me if there was a problem. Everybody assumed. Nobody led up, nobody led down. And we all paid for it in wasted time and a completely avoidable headache.

So that's where we're starting tonight, because the principle we're talking about is Leading Up and Down the Chain of Command. This is chapter ten in Extreme Ownership, and it's one of the ideas that sounds obvious until you actually try to live it. The core concept is this: leadership isn't just about managing the people under you. It's also about managing up — making sure the people above you, the ones you answer to, actually have the information and the context they need to make good decisions. And when you're unclear on what's expected, when the guidance is fuzzy or you're not sure what the intent is — you ask. You don't guess. You don't assume. You ask, you clarify, you close the loop. Leading up and down the chain means you own the communication in both directions, not just the direction that feels comfortable.

All right. Let's get into the story, because this is where the principle stops being theory and starts being the thing that saves you or sinks you.

A few months ago we had a training exercise — composite scenario, no real unit details, but the setup's pretty common in engineer units. We were tasked with doing a route clearance and setting up a tactical assessment for a mobility corridor. Basically, can we get vehicles and equipment through this stretch of road, what are the obstacles, what needs fixing, how long will it take. Not complicated in concept, but there's a lot of moving parts. You've got different sections doing different tasks, you've got a timeline that's tight but not impossible, and you've got a commander who's counting on the assessment to inform the next phase of the exercise.

I was the Warrant in charge of the technical side — making sure the engineers doing the actual work had what they needed and the assessment was accurate. The officer I answer to, a captain, was handling the higher-level coordination and briefing the battalion commander. Clear roles, clear responsibilities, should've been straightforward.

Except I made an assumption that bit me. Hard.

About halfway through the exercise, I realized we were behind schedule. Not catastrophically behind, but enough that I knew we weren't going to finish the full route assessment in the time we'd originally projected. And here's where I screwed it up — I didn't immediately loop the captain in. I told myself, we'll just push the guys a little harder, we'll make up time, no need to sound the alarm yet. I figured I'd handle it at my level, and if it became a bigger problem, then I'd escalate it. I didn't want to look like I couldn't manage my piece of the mission. I didn't want to create unnecessary stress for him when maybe we'd pull it off anyway. So I kept my head down and kept pushing.

What I didn't realize, because I hadn't asked and he hadn't told me — and that's on both of us, but it's definitely on me — was that the timeline wasn't flexible. The battalion commander had tied the next phase of the exercise to our assessment being done by a specific time, and there were other units waiting on our input. The captain knew that. I didn't, because I hadn't confirmed the priority and the constraints. I'd just assumed the timeline was a rough target, not a hard stop.

By the time I figured out we weren't going to make it, and I finally radioed the captain to give him a heads-up, it was too late to adjust anything meaningful. We missed the window. The next phase got delayed. The battalion commander wasn't thrilled. And the captain, to his credit, didn't throw me under the bus, but when we debriefed afterward he was blunt — why didn't you tell me sooner? And I didn't have a good answer, because the real answer was, I didn't want to admit I was behind, and I didn't think it mattered that much. Both of which were failures on my part.

That's the failure to lead up. I didn't keep the person above me informed. I didn't ask clarifying questions about the timeline and the stakes. I assumed I understood the commander's intent, and I assumed I had more latitude than I actually did. And because I failed to lead up, I put the captain in a position where he couldn't make good decisions, because he didn't have the information he needed until it was too late to matter.

But here's the other side of it — the leading down piece — because that same exercise had a moment where I got that part right, at least more right than I got the other part.

One of the junior NCOs on my team, a sergeant who's solid but still learning, was tasked with coordinating the equipment inspections before we moved out. Pretty standard job — make sure the gear's accounted for, make sure it's serviceable, make sure we're not rolling out with a deadlined vehicle or missing a critical piece of kit. And about an hour before we were supposed to step off, I walked by and saw him standing near the vehicles, looking at his clipboard, not really doing much. I asked him how the inspections were going. He said fine. I asked him if everything was accounted for. He said yeah, he thought so.

I stopped. I thought so. Not yes. Not definitely. I thought so.

So I asked him to walk me through it. And it turned out he'd done a visual check, which is better than nothing, but he hadn't actually confirmed with each section that all their equipment was present and functional. He'd assumed that if something was missing or broken, someone would've told him. Which — maybe they would have. Or maybe they wouldn't have, because people get busy, people assume someone else is tracking it, things slip through the cracks.

So I pulled him aside, not in a harsh way, but direct. I told him, when you're the one responsible for accountability, you can't assume people will come to you. You have to go to them. You have to close the loop. And then I had him go back and actually do it — confirm with each section, yes or no, do you have everything you need, is everything functional. It took him an extra twenty minutes. And guess what — one of the sections was missing a piece of equipment. Not mission-critical, but something we needed, and we had time to grab it because we caught it early. If we'd rolled out and discovered it missing halfway through, that's a problem. But because we caught it then, it was just a minor fix.

That's leading down. That's taking the time to make sure the people under you understand the standard, understand the why, and understand how to execute. It's not micromanaging. It's not doing their job for them. It's setting them up to succeed by making sure they have clarity, and correcting them when they don't, and doing it in a way that teaches instead of just punishes.

And here's the thing that ties both halves of this principle together — leading up and leading down are actually the same skill set. It's all about communication, clarity, and owning the fact that if there's a gap in understanding, it's your job to close it. Whether that's up the chain or down the chain doesn't matter. If you're in the middle — and most of us are in the middle somewhere, we've got people we answer to and people who answer to us — then you're responsible for both directions.

As a Warrant Officer, that's basically the job description. I'm a technical expert, which means the junior soldiers and NCOs look to me for guidance on the engineering piece. But I'm also an officer, which means I've got a responsibility to the command, to the mission, to making sure the people above me have what they need to lead effectively. I can't just be good at one and ignore the other. I have to be good at both, or I'm failing at the job.

And that motor pool mess I mentioned at the start — the conflicting schedules, the confusion, the wasted time — that happened because I failed to lead in both directions that week. I didn't make sure the junior NCO understood that he needed to confirm his plan with me before committing to it. And I didn't check in with the other section to make sure I knew what their timeline looked like. I assumed it would sort itself out, and it didn't, because assumptions are not a leadership strategy.

So when I finally got everybody together and we sorted out the schedule, I didn't just fix the immediate problem and move on. I took a few minutes and talked through what went wrong. I owned the fact that I should've been checking in with both groups earlier in the week. I told the junior NCO that next time, he needs to close the loop with me before he locks anything in. And I told the other section that if they've got a priority timeline, they need to make sure I know it's a priority, not just mention it in passing and hope I remember. Everybody walked away with a clearer picture of how we're going to communicate going forward. Not because I lectured them, but because I owned my part of the failure and made sure everybody understood the standard.

That's what leading up and down the chain looks like in practice. It's not glamorous. It's not a big dramatic moment. It's a series of small, intentional decisions to communicate clearly, to ask when you're not sure, to confirm instead of assume, to keep people informed instead of hoping they'll figure it out.

And if you're sitting there thinking, well, I'm not in the military, I don't have a chain of command — you're wrong. You do. Maybe it's not called a chain of command, but you've got people you answer to, and you've got people who depend on you, and the principle applies exactly the same way. If you're in a trade, you've got a foreman or a project manager or a customer, and you've got apprentices or helpers or subcontractors. If you're in an office, you've got a boss and you've got coworkers or reports. If you're a parent, you've got a spouse — hopefully — and you've got kids. In every one of those situations, you're leading up and down. You're managing relationships in both directions. And if you're only good at one direction, if you only focus on managing the people under you and you ignore the people above you, or if you only focus on pleasing the people above you and you neglect the people who depend on you — you're failing as a leader.

So here's the question you take into tomorrow. Think about the last week. Where did you assume instead of ask? Where did you fail to keep someone above you informed, and it caused a problem or could have caused a problem? And on the flip side, where did you fail to give someone under you the clarity or the guidance they needed, and they stumbled because of it?

Pick one. Just one. And figure out how you're going to close that loop. Maybe it's a conversation you need to have. Maybe it's a process you need to tighten up. Maybe it's just a habit you need to build, checking in more often, asking more questions, making sure people know what you need from them and what they can expect from you.

Lead up. Lead down. Own the communication. Don't assume. Don't hope it works out. Make it work out.
