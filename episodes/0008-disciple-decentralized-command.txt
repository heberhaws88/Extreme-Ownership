I've been thinking about control lately, and how hard it is to let go of it. Especially when you believe the mission matters. Especially when you're responsible for the outcome.

There's a moment that comes to mind — not from my own life, but from the Savior's. The night of the Last Supper. He's got maybe twelve, fourteen hours left before Gethsemane. He knows what's coming. He knows exactly what's coming. And in that moment, knowing He'll carry the weight of everything — past, present, future, every sin, every sorrow, every broken thing — He gets down on the floor and washes His disciples' feet.

He doesn't call a staff meeting to deliver last-minute marching orders. He doesn't micromanage the next seventy-two hours. He doesn't issue detailed contingencies for after He's gone. He washes their feet. He breaks bread with them. He says, this is how you'll know you're mine — that you love one another. Then He leaves them to figure out the rest.

I don't think that's resignation. I think that's Decentralized Command.

---

Tonight I want to talk about chapter eight of Extreme Ownership by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin. The principle is Decentralized Command. The core idea is this: no single leader can manage every decision, see every angle, control every variable. If you try, you become the bottleneck. You slow everything down, you miss what's happening on the ground, and you burn yourself out while the people under you stop thinking for themselves. Instead, you have to push decision-making authority down to the people closest to the problem. You give them the mission — the why, the boundaries, the intent — and then you trust them to execute. You train them to own their piece of the fight, and then you let them fight it.

That's harder than it sounds. Because it means accepting that they might do it differently than you would. It means accepting that they might make mistakes you could've prevented if you'd kept your hands on the wheel. It means living with the discomfort of not knowing every detail, not being in every room, not having final approval on every call.

It also means this: if you haven't trained them, if you haven't explained the mission clearly, if you haven't set boundaries and intent and given them the tools to succeed — and then they fail — that's on you. That's still your ownership. Decentralized Command isn't abdication. It's the opposite. It's taking responsibility for building a team that can function without you standing over their shoulder.

---

Let me slow down here and talk about what this looks like in following Jesus Christ. Because I think there's something profound in the way the Savior led, and it's not always what we expect from the guy we call Lord and King.

He didn't centralize everything. He didn't keep all the authority at the top. He called twelve apostles, sure — but then He sent out seventy more. He taught in parables so people had to wrestle with the meaning themselves. He healed people and then told them, go, show yourself to the priest, go tell no one, go back to your town — He put the next step on them. He didn't hover. He didn't micromanage their faith.

And then, at the end, He left. He didn't stay to run the church from a corner office in Jerusalem. He ascended. He sent the Holy Ghost. He left fallible, frightened, frequently-wrong human beings in charge of carrying His gospel to the ends of the earth. That's Decentralized Command. That's pushing the mission down to the people closest to the problem — which, in that case, was every single person who would ever need to hear the gospel.

He trusted them. Even knowing Peter would deny Him. Even knowing Thomas would doubt. Even knowing they'd scatter in Gethsemane. He trusted them anyway, because the mission was bigger than any one leader's ability to control it.

In the Doctrine and Covenants, section one-oh-seven, there's this beautiful structure laid out for how the priesthood is supposed to work. It's decentralized by design. You've got a First Presidency, sure. You've got apostles. But you've also got stake presidents and bishops and elders quorum presidents and Relief Society presidents and home teachers and ministering brothers and sisters, all of them holding keys or authority or stewardship over their own little piece of the kingdom. No one person runs it all. No one person can. The whole system is built on the idea that you push responsibility down, you train people in the doctrine and the principles, and then you trust them to lead where they are.

And when it works, it works beautifully. When it doesn't — when a bishop tries to make every decision himself, or when a Relief Society president won't delegate because she's afraid someone else will do it wrong, or when I, as a husband and a father, insist on controlling every little thing in my home because I don't trust my wife or my kids to handle it — the whole thing bogs down. People stop growing. They stop thinking. They stop owning their own discipleship.

The Savior didn't do that. He gave people space to choose. Space to act. Space to fail, even. That's agency. That's also Decentralized Command.

---

Let me tell you where I've been failing at this lately.

A while back my wife and I were talking about our kids' spiritual education. We've got three of them — two boys and a girl — and we're doing our best to raise them in the gospel. Family scripture study, family prayer, family home evening, the whole nine yards. But here's the thing: I had started to take all of it on myself. I was the one picking the scriptures. I was the one deciding what we'd talk about. I was the one steering every conversation, answering every question, making sure we hit the points I thought mattered. My wife would chime in, sure, but I'd already set the agenda. The kids would listen, or not listen, depending on the night. But it was my show. I was running it.

And one night my wife said, quietly, after the kids had gone to bed — she said, do you think maybe we could let them lead sometimes?

I didn't get defensive. I wanted to. But I didn't. I just said, what do you mean?

She said, I mean, what if we let our oldest pick the scripture and tell us what he sees in it? What if we let our daughter pray without you correcting her phrasing? What if we let them own some of this, instead of you handing it all to them pre-packaged?

And I sat there, and I realized: I'd been centralizing everything. I'd been holding all the decision-making, all the teaching, all the spiritual leadership in our home, in my own hands. And I'd told myself I was doing it because it mattered, because I was responsible, because if I didn't do it right the kids might not get it.

But really, I think I was afraid. Afraid they'd pick a boring scripture. Afraid they'd miss the point. Afraid it wouldn't go the way I thought it should. Afraid, honestly, that if I wasn't in control it wouldn't count — like their discipleship only mattered if I was the one shaping it.

That's not leadership. That's not fatherhood, not the kind the Savior modeled. That's me making myself the bottleneck in my own family's spiritual growth.

So we changed it. Not all at once, but we started. We started letting the kids take turns choosing the scripture. We started letting them ask the questions instead of me always having the answers teed up. We started giving them space to wrestle with the doctrine themselves, to say I don't know, to get it wrong and figure it out.

And you know what happened? Some nights it was awkward. Some nights my oldest picked a verse I wouldn't have picked, and we ended up talking about something I hadn't planned for. Some nights my daughter's prayer was shorter than I would've made it, or my youngest got distracted halfway through and we had to start over.

But some nights — more nights than I expected — they said things I never would've thought to say. They made connections I hadn't seen. They owned it. It became theirs, not just mine.

I'm still learning this. I still catch myself jumping in, taking over, steering the ship when I should be letting them steer. But I'm trying. Because if I don't teach them to own their own faith now, while they're still home, while I'm still here to course-correct when it matters — then what happens when they leave? What happens when I'm not in the room? If I've centralized everything, if I've made them dependent on me to tell them what to think and what to believe, then the first time they're on their own and the questions get hard, they won't have any muscle memory for working it out themselves. They'll flounder. Or worse, they'll walk away, because they never learned that this was theirs to own in the first place.

Decentralized Command in a family means training your kids to think, to pray, to study, to struggle with the gospel themselves. It means trusting that the Holy Ghost can teach them things you can't. It means accepting that their path with Jesus Christ might look different from yours, and that's okay — because it's their path, not yours.

That's hard. Especially when you love them. Especially when you know how much the gospel has meant to you, and you want so badly for them to have that too. But you can't force it. You can't control it. You can only teach them the mission, set the example, give them the tools — and then trust them to choose.

---

Here's the thing about Decentralized Command that I don't think gets talked about enough: it doesn't mean you check out. It doesn't mean you hand off responsibility and walk away. You're still the leader. You're still accountable. You're still responsible for making sure the people under you understand the mission — the why, the boundaries, the intent. You're still responsible for training them, for checking in, for course-correcting when they drift off mission.

What it means is this: you lead by clarity, not by control. You lead by trust, not by micromanagement. You lead by making sure everyone knows what we're trying to accomplish and why it matters — and then you let them figure out how.

The Savior did this constantly. He'd teach a principle, He'd show them the way, and then He'd send them out. Go and do thou likewise. He didn't follow them around correcting every step. He trusted that the Spirit would guide them, that the doctrine would hold, that the mission was clear enough that they could execute it without Him hovering.

That takes confidence. Not in them, necessarily — they're going to mess up, just like we all do — but in the mission itself. In the doctrine. In the fact that the gospel is true whether you manage every detail of it or not.

And it takes humility. Because it means admitting you're not the only one who can do this. It means admitting that other people might have insight you don't, might see angles you miss, might come up with solutions you wouldn't have thought of. It means trusting that God can work through them just as much as He works through you — maybe more, because they're closer to the problem than you are.

I think about the Savior in Gethsemane. Totally alone. Shouldering a weight no one else could carry. Bleeding from every pore. And even then — even in that moment — He didn't try to do the apostles' part for them. He didn't try to make Peter's choices for him, or spare John the grief, or force Judas into a different path. He carried what was His to carry. He let them carry what was theirs.

That's the hard part of Decentralized Command in a spiritual sense. You can teach. You can testify. You can love. You can set the example. But you cannot make someone else's choices for them. You cannot carry their faith for them. You can't centralize their agency. At some point, you have to trust them to own it — and trust God to be with them when they do.

Where in my life am I holding onto control because I'm afraid of what happens if I let go? Where am I making myself the bottleneck — at home, at church, wherever I lead — because I don't trust the people around me to own their piece of the mission?

And then, harder question: have I actually trained them? Have I made the mission clear? Have I given them the doctrine, the tools, the why? Or have I just expected them to figure it out, and then gotten frustrated when they didn't?

Because Decentralized Command only works if you do the hard work up front. You have to teach. You have to clarify. You have to make sure everyone knows what we're trying to accomplish and why it matters. And then — then — you have to let go. You have to trust that they'll step up. You have to trust that God is in this too, working through them, refining them, teaching them things you can't.

It's scary. I know it's scary. It's scary for me. But I think it's the only way any of us grow.

The Savior trusted broken, fallible people to build His kingdom. He still does. That includes you. That includes me. That includes everyone we're leading.

Maybe it's time we trust them too.
