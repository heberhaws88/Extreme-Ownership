You know what the worst job site conversation is? It's the one where your best journeyman pulls you aside, looks you dead in the eye, and says "Hey, can we talk about how that bid went?" And your stomach drops, because you already know. You bid it too low. You promised the client a timeline you had no business promising. And you did it because you wanted to win the job, because you wanted to prove you could land the big commercial account, because your ego told you that you were good enough to make the numbers work even when the numbers didn't actually work.

That's where we're starting tonight.

I'm talking about ego. Specifically, the principle Jocko Willink and Leif Babin lay out in chapter four of Extreme Ownership: Check the Ego. The core idea is this — your pride, your need to be right, your fear of looking incompetent, all of that clouds your judgment. It keeps you from seeing conditions as they actually are. It makes you defensive when you should be listening. It turns small mistakes into disasters because you can't admit the small mistake early enough to fix it. Humility isn't just a nice quality for a leader to have. It's operationally necessary. You can't make good decisions if your ego is running the show.

And before we go any further into the business side of this tonight, I need to tie this back to the foundation, because checking your ego isn't just a leadership tactic for me — it's a discipleship requirement.

Jesus Christ was pretty clear about this. He didn't say "Blessed are the confident self-promoters." He said "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth." He didn't build his kingdom by asserting his rights and demanding everyone recognize his superiority. He washed feet. He served. He submitted to the Father's will even when it meant the cross. Pride is the original sin — it's what got Lucifer cast out, it's what got Adam and Eve kicked out of the garden, and it's what keeps us from becoming the people God is trying to make us into.

In the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, we talk a lot about being teachable, having a broken heart and a contrite spirit. That's ego-death language. It means you can't hold so tightly to your own version of things that you're unteachable. The scriptures are full of warnings about pride — King Benjamin teaches that the natural man is an enemy to God unless he yields to the Holy Spirit, becomes humble, submissive, meek. Paul tells the Philippians to let the same mind be in them that was in Christ, who didn't grasp at equality with God but emptied himself and took the form of a servant.

So when I talk about checking the ego on a job site or in a bid meeting, I'm not just talking about good business practice. I'm talking about the same spiritual discipline that's supposed to shape every other part of my life. If I can't handle being wrong about a wire size, how am I supposed to handle being wrong about something that actually matters? If I can't take correction from a client or an inspector without getting defensive, what does that say about how I'm receiving correction from the Lord?

Alright. Let's get into the story.

This happened about a year and a half ago. I'd been running my own shop for a few years at that point, and we were doing okay. Mostly residential service work, some light commercial, a few new-construction jobs here and there. Steady. But I wanted to grow. I wanted to land the kind of project that would put us on the map, that would prove we could run with the bigger outfits in town.

So when a mid-sized commercial remodel came up for bid — a medical office buildout, full electrical, data, fire alarm, the works — I jumped at it. I spent two full evenings putting that bid together. I walked the site twice. I ran the numbers. And then I ran them again, tighter, because the first pass came in higher than I thought the client would accept, and I wanted to win this thing.

I submitted the bid. We got the job. I was pumped.

Three weeks in, I knew we were in trouble.

The scope creep started almost immediately. The architect's plans were incomplete in a couple of areas, so we had to make calls on the fly. The client kept adding small requests — an extra circuit here, a relocated panel there, nothing that sounded like a big deal in the moment, but it all added up. And my lead guy on the job, one of my journeymen who'd been with me since the beginning, started asking questions. "Hey, are we tracking change orders for this?" "Did we account for the fire alarm programming time in the original bid?" "This panel location the client wants is going to add a full day of conduit work — are we billing for that?"

And I kept brushing him off. I told him we were fine, that I'd built in contingency, that we needed to keep the client happy because this could lead to more work. Which was all technically true, but it wasn't the whole truth. The whole truth was that I didn't want to admit I'd underbid the job. I didn't want to go back to the client and say "Hey, remember that price I gave you? Turns out I was wrong." I didn't want my guys to know I'd screwed up the bid. I didn't want to look like I didn't know what I was doing.

So I just kept pushing. We ate the extra costs. We worked longer days to try to make up the time. I pulled myself off other jobs to be on site more, which meant I was neglecting my other clients and my other crews, which created problems downstream. My cash flow got tight because I'd planned on draws at certain milestones and we were burning through material faster than I'd projected. I snapped at my wife one night when she asked how the job was going, because I didn't want to talk about it, didn't want to admit out loud that I was in over my head.

And my journeyman kept trying to help. He wasn't being insubordinate, he wasn't undermining me — he was doing exactly what a good team member is supposed to do, which is bring problems to the surface before they become disasters. But I couldn't hear it, because my ego was in the way. Every time he raised a concern, I heard it as criticism. Every time he suggested we needed to document a change or talk to the client about additional costs, I heard "You messed up the bid and now we're paying for it."

The job finally wrapped. We finished it, got paid, the client was happy with the work. And when I ran the final numbers, we'd lost money. Not a catastrophic amount, but enough to sting. Enough that it wiped out the profit from two other jobs that month.

I sat in my truck in the parking lot after the final walkthrough, and I just felt tired. Tired and stupid and angry at myself, because I knew exactly what had happened. I'd let my pride drive the bus. I'd wanted the win so badly that I'd ignored the warning signs. I'd bid the job not based on what it would actually cost, but based on what I wanted to believe it would cost. And when reality started shoving back, I'd dug in instead of adjusting.

That night, I called my journeyman. I told him I owed him an apology. I told him he'd been right, that I should have listened, that I'd let my ego keep me from seeing what was right in front of me. And then I asked him to help me figure out how to make sure it didn't happen again.

We spent an hour on the phone. He walked me through every point where he'd tried to flag something and I'd shut him down. It wasn't comfortable. But it was necessary.

Here's what I learned, and what I'm still learning.

First, ego makes you allergic to input. When you're running on pride, feedback feels like an attack. It doesn't matter if it's coming from a good place, doesn't matter if the person giving it is trying to help you succeed — if it threatens your self-image, your ego will reject it. And in a business, that's lethal. You need people around you who will tell you the truth, and you need to be able to hear it without getting defensive.

Second, ego makes you double down on bad decisions. Once I'd committed to that bid number, once I'd told myself I could make it work, I couldn't back off without admitting I'd been wrong. So I kept pouring time and money and energy into a losing proposition, because the alternative was eating crow. Pride makes you stubborn in exactly the wrong ways.

Third, and this is the one that still gets me, ego makes you isolated. I didn't talk to my wife about the job because I didn't want her to see me failing. I didn't bring it up with my guys because I didn't want to look weak. I didn't ask for help because I thought asking for help meant I wasn't capable. And so I carried the whole weight of it by myself, which made everything harder and meant I didn't have access to the people who could have actually helped me figure it out sooner.

Checking the ego doesn't mean you stop being confident. It doesn't mean you become a pushover or stop trusting your own judgment. It means you hold your judgment lightly enough that you can adjust it when new information comes in. It means you care more about getting it right than about being right. It means you build a team culture where people can speak up without getting their heads bitten off, because you've made it clear that you value the mission more than you value protecting your pride.

Since that job, I've made some changes. I have a rule now: if one of my guys raises a concern about a bid or a timeline or a scope issue, I don't respond in the moment. I say "Let me look at that," and I actually look at it. I run the numbers, I check the plans, I sit with it for a few hours before I decide whether they're right or I'm right. Half the time, they're right. The other half, I at least have a better answer for why I'm making the call I'm making, and they know I took them seriously.

I also started tracking my bids differently. I keep a spreadsheet now where I log my estimated costs versus actual costs on every job, and I review it monthly. It's humbling, seeing how often I'm off, but it makes me better. I'm learning where my blind spots are, where I tend to be optimistic in ways that don't match reality. That only works if I'm willing to look at the data honestly, which only works if my ego isn't running the show.

And I've gotten better, slowly, at admitting when I'm wrong. Not perfect at it, not even close, but better. A couple months ago I bid a service panel upgrade, and halfway through the job I realized I'd missed a whole section of code updates that were going to be required. It was going to add cost and time. And instead of just eating it and hoping the client wouldn't notice, I called him, explained the situation, and gave him the option to adjust the scope or pay for the additional work. He appreciated the honesty, and we worked it out. Would I have made that call two years ago, before the medical office job? I don't think so. My pride would've told me to just absorb it and not let the client see that I'd missed something.

Here's the thing, and this is where it ties back to faith for me. Pride tells you that you have to have it all figured out, that you have to be the smartest guy in the room, that admitting weakness or uncertainty is failure. But the gospel tells you the opposite. It tells you that you're broken, that you need a Savior, that the whole point is to recognize your dependence on God and on the people he puts around you. It tells you that strength comes through acknowledging weakness, that power is made perfect in weakness.

When I'm trying to run my business on ego, I'm trying to be self-sufficient, and that's a lie. I'm not self-sufficient. I need my guys, I need my clients to give me honest feedback, I need my wife to keep me grounded, I need the Lord to guide me when I don't know what I'm doing. And the more I can let go of the need to protect my image and just focus on doing the work well and serving the people I'm supposed to serve, the better the business runs and the more I actually grow as a person.

So here's what I want you to take into tomorrow. Ask yourself this: Where has my pride made me defensive instead of teachable this week? Is there a conversation you've been avoiding because it would mean admitting you were wrong about something? Is there feedback you've been dismissing because it stings, even though part of you knows it's probably true? Is there a decision you're resisting not because it's the wrong call, but because changing course would mean your ego takes a hit?

Sit with that. Don't brush past it. And if you find something, if there's a place where your pride has you dug in, ask the Lord for the humility to let it go. Ask him to help you care more about getting it right than about being right. And then go have the hard conversation, make the adjustment, own the mistake, whatever it is you need to do.

Checking the ego is a daily discipline. It's not something you do once and then you're good. It's something you have to choose, over and over, every time your pride tries to take the wheel. But every time you choose humility over ego, you get a little better at it. You build a little more trust with the people around you. You make a few fewer stupid mistakes. And you become a little more like the leader, and the disciple, you're supposed to be.
