I was sitting in the chapel last Sunday, right after the sacrament, and the speaker said something that stopped me cold. She wasn't talking to me specifically, didn't know me, probably didn't even notice I was there — but I felt like she'd just reached across the room and tapped me on the chest. She said, "The Lord can't teach a man who already knows everything." Simple. Clean. And it landed because I'd spent the last three days being exactly that man.

Tonight's principle is Check the Ego. Chapter four of Extreme Ownership by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin. The core idea: ego is the enemy of effective leadership. When your pride is in the driver's seat, you can't see what's actually happening around you. You stop accepting feedback. You stop learning. You become unteachable. And when you become unteachable, you become dangerous — to your team, to your mission, to yourself. Humility and objectivity have to win over pride if you want to make good decisions. If you want to lead well. If you want to grow.

And tonight I'm looking at this through the lens of discipleship — following Jesus Christ — because that's where the principle cuts deepest for me. This is the ground I fail on most consistently, and it's the ground where checking my ego matters most.

Let me say the faith piece up front, because it's the foundation for everything else I'm going to talk about tonight. Jesus Christ is the perfect example of leadership without ego. Think about that. The Son of God, who had every right to demand recognition, every right to stand on His authority, every reason to let His pride dictate His actions — and instead He washed feet. He knelt in the dirt with a basin and a towel and washed the feet of men who were about to abandon Him, one of whom was about to betray Him. He took on Himself accountability for every sin, every failure, every broken thing in all of human history — none of it His, all of it ours — and He did it willingly. In Gethsemane He didn't say, "This isn't fair, I didn't do anything wrong, why should I have to suffer for what they did?" He said, "Not my will, but thine, be done." That's ownership without ego. That's humility at a level I can't even fully wrap my mind around.

And the scriptural record is full of this same pattern. In Ether, chapter twelve, Moroni writes to the Lord and says, essentially, "I'm worried my words aren't good enough. I'm worried people will mock our weakness in writing." And the Lord's response is, "I give unto men weakness that they may be humble." Not — I give you weakness so you'll feel bad about yourself. But so you'll be humble. So you'll stay teachable. So your ego won't get in the way of what I'm trying to do through you.

Or think about Moses, one of the greatest prophets, described in Numbers as "very meek, above all the men which were upon the face of the earth." Meek. Humble. Teachable. Not weak — Moses was bold, he confronted Pharaoh, he led a nation through a desert for forty years. But his ego didn't run the show. That's the pattern.

So here's where it gets personal for me, and where I have to own my own failure this week.

There's a decision I made about six months ago in my walk with the Lord that I was convinced was right. I'd prayed about it. I'd felt peace about it. I moved forward. And it involved how I was structuring my time — specifically how much time I was giving to church service, to my calling, versus time with my family, versus time in the business. I felt like I'd struck the right balance. I felt like the Lord had confirmed it. And for a while, things seemed fine.

But over the last couple of months, my wife had started gently — and I mean gently, she's not harsh about these things — bringing up the fact that I seemed stretched. That I was saying yes to too many things. That the boys and our daughter were noticing I wasn't as present. And every time she brought it up, I had an answer. I had a reason. I'd say, "Well, this is temporary, once we get through this project it'll settle down," or "This is service, this is what we're supposed to be doing," or "I prayed about this, I know it's right."

And I believed that. I wasn't lying to her. I really believed I was doing the right thing. But here's the problem: I'd stopped praying about it. I'd prayed six months ago, gotten an answer, and then put it in a box labeled "settled" and moved on. I wasn't checking back in. I wasn't asking the Lord if the situation had changed, if my capacity had changed, if what He needed from me now was different than what He needed from me then. I'd stopped being teachable on this specific issue because my ego was wrapped up in being the guy who heard from the Lord and followed through. I didn't want to admit that maybe I'd misread something, or maybe I'd been right initially but circumstances had shifted and I hadn't adjusted.

So when my wife would bring it up, I'd get defensive. Not loud, not mean — I'm not proud of this, but I'd get that edge in my voice where you can hear someone's walls going up. I'd deflect. I'd justify. And the whole time, underneath, there was this little voice I was ignoring that was saying, "You're not actually listening. You're protecting your position instead of seeking truth."

Last Sunday, sitting in that chapel, hearing that line — "The Lord can't teach a man who already knows everything" — I felt it. Like a punch to the gut. Because that's exactly what I'd been doing. I'd decided I already knew the answer, and so I'd closed myself off to correction. My ego — my need to be right, my need to not admit I might've missed something — had made me unteachable. And if I'm unteachable, the Spirit can't guide me. If the Spirit can't guide me, I'm just a guy stumbling around in the dark, convinced I can see.

I went home that afternoon and I told my wife I needed to talk. And I said, "I think I've been wrong. I don't think I've been listening to you, and I don't think I've been listening to the Lord." And that was hard. It was hard because I still believed, at some level, that I'd been doing the right thing. But belief isn't the same as truth. And pride was keeping me from finding out which one I was actually standing on.

So I prayed. Not the kind of prayer where you're asking the Lord to confirm what you already think. The kind where you actually open your hands and say, "Show me what I'm missing. Show me where I'm wrong." And what came back wasn't dramatic. It wasn't a rebuke. It was just — clarity. A quiet sense that yes, six months ago that decision was right. But circumstances had changed. My capacity had shifted. What the Lord needed from me now was different. And I'd been so locked into being right that I couldn't see it.

Here's the principle: ego makes you blind. It doesn't matter if you started out right. It doesn't matter if you prayed and got confirmation. If you stop checking, if you stop staying open to correction, if you let pride convince you that you've got it all figured out, you will drift off course. And the longer you let your ego run the show, the further you'll drift before you even realize it.

In the Doctrine and Covenants, section one hundred and thirty-six, it says, "Be thou humble; and the Lord thy God shall lead thee by the hand, and give thee answer to thy prayers." Lead thee by the hand. Not — give you the map once and then let you go. Lead you, step by step, as you stay humble enough to keep following. Humility isn't a one-time posture. It's a constant practice. Checking your ego is something you have to do over and over, because ego creeps back in the moment you stop watching for it.

This is true in leadership anywhere. In the Guard, if I get so locked into my assessment of a situation that I stop listening to the soldiers on the ground who are actually seeing what's happening, I make bad calls. In my business, if I get so proud of my process that I stop hearing feedback from my guys or from clients, I lose quality and I lose trust. But in discipleship, the stakes are higher. Because this isn't just about mission success or business reputation. This is about my relationship with God. This is about whether I'm actually following Him or just following my own ideas about Him.

The Savior's example is the standard. He had no ego. He had perfect knowledge, perfect authority, perfect power — and He used it all in perfect submission to the Father. That's the pattern. That's what I'm aiming for, even though I fall short constantly. And when I do fall short, when my pride does cloud my judgment, the way forward is the same every time: own it. Admit it. Stop defending. Stop justifying. Check the ego. Get humble. Get teachable again.

Here's where I landed this week. I sat down with my wife and we went through my calendar. We looked at what I'd committed to. We prayed together. And we made some changes. I stepped back from one responsibility in my calling — not abandoning it, but passing it to someone else who's capable and willing. I blocked out time that's just for my family, non-negotiable, no exceptions unless there's a genuine emergency. And I committed to check back in with the Lord weekly on this, not just assume I've got it figured out.

Was that easy? No. Did part of me feel like I was admitting failure? Yes. But that's the ego talking. The truth is, adjusting course when you realize you're off isn't failure. It's wisdom. It's humility. It's leadership. Failure is staying on the wrong path because you're too proud to admit you took a wrong turn.

So here's what I want you to carry into tomorrow. Ask yourself: where has my pride made me defensive instead of teachable this week? Is there an area in your life — in your discipleship, in your leadership, in your family, in your work — where you've stopped checking because you've decided you already know the answer? Is there feedback you've been dismissing? Is there a decision you're clinging to, not because you're sure it's still right, but because admitting you were wrong would sting?

Check the ego. Get humble. Go to the Lord and actually ask Him to show you where you're off. Not to confirm what you think. To correct what you've missed. And if He shows you something, own it. Adjust. Don't defend. Don't justify. Just own it and move forward.

The Lord can't teach a man who already knows everything. But He will lead by the hand the man who's humble enough to admit he doesn't.
