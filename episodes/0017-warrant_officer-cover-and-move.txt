I got home from drill Sunday night, dropped my gear bag in the garage, and my wife asked how the weekend went. I said fine. She said you look tired. I said yeah, well, it was a long weekend. And that was about it for the debrief.

Here's what I didn't say. Saturday afternoon we had a vertical construction project we were trying to wrap before the snow hit — panel forms going up for a retaining wall, simple enough work but we were behind. My engineer platoon was supposed to have it done by fifteen hundred so the horizontal guys could start prepping the approach by sixteen hundred. We didn't finish until almost seventeen hundred. The horizontal team stood around for an hour waiting on us, then had to scramble to get anything done before we lost the light. The whole timeline slid. The officer running the exercise — a captain, good guy, sharp — didn't say much at the hot wash, but I could see it. We let the other platoon down. 

And here's the thing. I knew we were running behind at thirteen hundred. I knew it at fourteen hundred. I kept thinking we'd catch up, kept thinking my guys would find a rhythm, kept my head down in my own lane. It didn't occur to me until too late that I should have walked over to the lieutenant running horizontal and said hey, we're not going to make fifteen hundred, what do you need from us to keep your plan from falling apart. I didn't cover. I just moved — or tried to — and assumed everyone else would figure it out.

That's tonight's principle. Cover and Move. It comes from chapter five of Extreme Ownership by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin, and the core idea is this. Teams win through mutual support, not solo heroics. Every part of the team is responsible for the success of every other part, because in the end they all fail or succeed together. It doesn't matter if you executed your piece perfectly if the guy next to you got overrun because you were too focused on your own objective to notice he needed help. You don't get credit for your lane if the mission falls apart. You're all in it together.

It's a SEAL team concept originally, but it works the same way in an engineer unit, in an electrical crew, in a marriage, in a house with three kids who all need different things at the same time. The principle is the same. You can't win alone. And if you act like you can, you're going to lose — and you're going to take everyone else down with you.

Now before I get into the rest of tonight, I want to spend a minute here, because this principle connects directly to something Jesus taught, and it's not something you can skip over if you actually believe He's the Son of God and that His words matter.

He said the second great commandment is to love your neighbor as yourself. Not as much as is convenient. Not when you have margin. As yourself. That means their success, their struggle, their burden — it's yours. You don't get to stand back and say well, I did my part, not my problem if they're drowning. That's not love. That's self-preservation dressed up as professionalism.

And He also said that if you want to be great in His kingdom, you become the servant of all. You don't climb over people. You don't let them fail while you succeed. You go low. You cover. You help them win, even when it costs you something, even when it's not technically your job.

Cover and Move is what love looks like in a team. It's what discipleship looks like when you're not in a chapel. It's the practical, boots-on-the-ground expression of the truth that we're all part of one body, and if one part suffers, the whole thing suffers. Paul said that in first Corinthians. He wasn't talking theory. He was talking about how you actually function as the people of God in a broken world.

So when I stand there on a drill weekend watching another platoon wait on me because I didn't communicate, didn't coordinate, didn't think past my own task list — that's not just a leadership failure. That's a failure to love my neighbor. It's a failure to see that his mission is my mission, because we're on the same team, and the team only wins if we all win.

Alright. Back to that Saturday.

So here's the setup. We're doing a joint training exercise, two engineer platoons working a simulated FOB expansion. My platoon, vertical construction — we're putting up structural support, barriers, fighting positions, that kind of work. The other platoon, horizontal — they're grading, drainage, road surface prep. In a real deployment these two elements have to work in sequence. Vertical gets the hardstand and barriers up, horizontal makes sure you've got trafficable routes and proper runoff so the whole site doesn't turn into a mud pit the first time it rains. You need both. And you need them coordinated.

The captain running the exercise made that real clear in the op order Friday night. He said this is not two separate projects, this is one construction op with two phases, and you will succeed or fail as one team. Everybody tracking? We all said yes sir.

Saturday morning my guys rolled out early, started setting the panel forms for the retaining wall. It's not complicated work but it's slow if you don't have a rhythm, and we had a couple of newer enlisted who were still learning the setup. I'm a Warrant, I'm the technical expert, so I'm walking them through it, making sure the forms are plumb, making sure the bracing is solid, making sure we're not going to have a blowout when we pour. I'm in my element. I'm doing what I'm good at.

And I'm completely siloed.

Around thirteen hundred one of my sergeants comes over and says sir, we're not going to hit fifteen hundred. I look at the progress, I do the math, and yeah, he's right. But instead of stopping and asking what that means for the next phase, I just say alright, let's tighten it up, we'll move faster. He says roger and we keep working.

Fourteen hundred rolls around. We're still behind. I'm starting to get frustrated with the pace, starting to think my guys aren't pushing hard enough, starting to micromanage. One of my corporals asks if we should let horizontal know we're running late and I say no, we'll catch up, just stay focused.

That right there. That's where I lost it.

Because here's what I was thinking. I was thinking my job is to get this retaining wall up. That's my piece. That's what I'm responsible for. If I can pull that off, I've done my job. What happens after that is someone else's problem. The horizontal platoon has their own leadership, their own NCOs, their own timeline. They'll figure it out.

That's silo mentality. That's the opposite of Cover and Move.

What I should have been thinking is this. My job is to make sure this construction op succeeds. That means my platoon's work and the other platoon's work have to come together. If I don't finish on time, they can't start on time. If they can't start on time, they can't finish on time. If they don't finish, the mission fails. Which means if I don't finish, the mission fails. And if I know I'm not going to finish, and I don't tell them, I'm setting them up to fail — which means I'm setting the whole team up to fail.

I didn't see it that way in the moment. I saw my lane. I stayed in my lane. I thought that was the professional thing to do.

We finally wrapped the retaining wall at sixteen fifty. Almost seventeen hundred. I walked over to where the horizontal platoon was staged and their lieutenant — younger guy, good attitude, prior enlisted — he's just standing there with his guys, waiting. They've been waiting for over an hour. He doesn't say anything snarky, he just says alright we're up, and they start hustling to get their equipment in place. But you can see it. They're behind before they even start. And it's my fault.

At the hot wash that night the captain goes through the timeline, notes that we ran long on phase one, notes that phase two didn't complete because they ran out of daylight. He doesn't throw anyone under the bus. He just says we didn't execute as one team, we executed as two separate elements, and that's why the mission didn't succeed. He's not wrong.

Afterward I pulled the horizontal lieutenant aside and I said hey, I should have come to you at thirteen hundred and told you we were behind. That's on me. He shrugged and said yeah, would have helped. Then he said, you know what would have helped even more? If you'd asked if we had anyone who could cross over and help you finish faster. We had two guys who know vertical, we were just sitting there prepping equipment. We could have sent them over.

I didn't even think of that. Didn't occur to me. Because I was so locked into my guys, my platoon, my task, I didn't see the possibility that the other team could help me — or that I should ask.

That's the thing about Cover and Move. It goes both ways. It's not just about you helping the other guy. It's about being humble enough to ask for help when you need it, to admit you're behind, to let someone else step into your lane and cover you so the team can keep moving. Pride kills that. Silo mentality kills that. The idea that asking for help is weakness, or that coordination is someone else's job — that kills it.

I didn't own that on Saturday. I should have.

Sunday morning before we rolled out I caught the captain for a minute and I said sir, I didn't communicate with horizontal when I should have, I didn't think past my own phase, and that's why the op fell apart. He nodded and said yeah. Appreciate you saying it. Then he said, you know why Warrant Officers exist? I said to be technical experts. He said yeah. And also to make sure the technical work supports the mission, not just the task. You can build a perfect retaining wall that doesn't matter if the mission fails. Don't lose sight of that.

He's right.

Here's the thing about being a Warrant Officer. You're in this weird middle space. You're an officer, so you're expected to lead, to think strategically, to care about the bigger picture. But you're also the technical expert, the guy who knows how to actually do the thing, so you're in the weeds more than a lieutenant or a captain usually is. That's good — you need that technical depth. But it's also a trap, because it's easy to let the technical work become the whole mission in your head. You get so focused on doing your piece right that you forget your piece is only one piece.

And if you let that happen, you stop covering. You just move. And the team falls apart.

I've seen it in my business too. I'll have one crew running a commercial job and another crew on a residential service call, and if I'm not careful I'll optimize for one and forget the other one exists. I'll send my best guy to the big job because that's where the money is, and I'll leave the small job short-handed, and then the small job drags or we make a mistake and the customer gets frustrated. I didn't cover. I moved. I prioritized one part of the team and left the other part to fend for themselves.

Doesn't work. You can't build a company that way. You can't build a platoon that way. You can't build a family that way.

My wife and I, we've had seasons where I'm slammed with work and she's drowning with the kids, and I come home and I'm still thinking about the job I just left, still mentally in my lane, and I don't even see that she needs me to cover. She needs me to step in, take the bedtime routine, deal with the meltdown, give her ten minutes to breathe. That's Cover and Move in a marriage. That's what it looks like to actually be on the same team instead of two people living parallel lives under the same roof.

I'm not always good at it. I get tunnel vision. I get task-focused. I forget that her success is my success, that if she's overwhelmed and I'm not helping, we're both losing.

Same thing with my kids. My oldest, he's getting to the age where he's starting to push back, test boundaries, try to figure out who he is. My daughter, she's quieter, she doesn't demand attention the same way. It's easy for me to spend all my energy on the squeaky wheel and not notice that the quiet one needs me to cover too. She needs me to check in, to notice when she's struggling, to make space for her even when she's not asking for it. That's my job. That's what it means to be her dad.

I can't just focus on the loudest need and call it good. I have to see the whole team. I have to cover all of them.

Here's what I'm taking from that drill weekend. If I know something's not going to plan, if I know I'm behind or stuck or in trouble — my job is to communicate that immediately, not hope I can catch up. My job is to ask what the person next to me needs, not assume they'll figure it out. My job is to think past my task and ask how my task supports the mission, and if it doesn't, to adjust. And my job is to be humble enough to ask for help when I need it, because the mission matters more than my pride.

That's Cover and Move.
