I showed up to the motor pool Saturday morning and walked straight past the answer to my problem because admitting I needed it would have meant admitting I'd been wrong for the last three weeks.

We were prepping a bridging exercise — not a full deployment, just a training evolution, but the kind where if you screw it up people notice and the after-action review gets uncomfortable. I'm the Warrant Officer on the engineer side, which means I'm supposed to be the technical expert. My lane is knowing how the equipment works, how the bridge sections mate up, load ratings, site surveys, all of it. And I'd made a call early on about how we were going to stage the vehicles and the bridge components based on the terrain we'd be working. Made sense on paper. I'd done the math. I knew what I was doing.

Except one of the NCOs, a staff sergeant who's been in engineer units longer than I've been in the Guard, had mentioned two weeks prior that he thought we should stage differently. He didn't make a big deal out of it. Just said something like, hey sir, I've seen this kind of site before, might want to consider flipping the setup. And I thanked him, said I'd think about it, and then didn't think about it, because I'd already decided and I knew I was right.

Fast forward to Saturday and we're an hour into setup and it's a mess. Vehicles are in each other's way. We're having to reposition a five-ton twice because the approach angle is garbage. The whole thing is taking three times longer than it should and everyone's standing around watching instead of working, and the officer I answer to is watching from the edge of the site with his arms crossed, and I know exactly what he's thinking because I'd be thinking it too.

And that staff sergeant is right there, not saying a word, just doing his job, but I can feel it. He told me. He told me this would happen and I blew him off because my ego couldn't handle the idea that maybe I didn't have all the answers.

Tonight's principle is Check the Ego. It's chapter four in Jocko Willink and Leif Babin's book Extreme Ownership, and the core idea is this: your pride, your need to be right, your fear of looking incompetent — all of that clouds your judgment. Ego keeps you from seeing conditions as they actually are. It keeps you from listening to the people around you who might know something you don't. It makes you double down on bad calls instead of pivoting when new information comes in. And in a leadership role, your ego isn't just your problem. It becomes everyone's problem, because the whole team pays for decisions you made to protect your pride instead of accomplish the mission.

Now before I go any further, I need to say this, because it's true every night no matter what lens we're looking through: following Jesus Christ and checking your ego aren't two separate projects. They're the same work. Jesus spent his whole ministry teaching that the way up is down. That the first will be last. That the greatest among you is the servant. That if you want to save your life you have to lose it. Ego is the thing in you that wants to be seen, to be right, to protect your reputation at all costs. The gospel is the thing that says your reputation isn't the point — obedience is, service is, truth is. And here's the thing: you can't actually own your failures, which is what Extreme Ownership is all about, if your ego won't let you admit you failed in the first place. So checking the ego isn't just a good leadership tactic. It's a spiritual discipline. It's part of dying to yourself so Christ can live in you. And that's hard. I'm not going to pretend I've got that figured out. But I know that every time I've let my pride run the show, I've made worse decisions and hurt people I'm supposed to be leading.

Alright. Back to the motor pool.

So we're an hour in, everything's a disaster, and I've got a choice. I can keep pretending this is just bad luck or unforeseen circumstances, or I can admit that I ignored good counsel because my ego couldn't take it. And here's the thing — in that moment, admitting it feels worse than just grinding through the problem. Your brain wants to protect you. It'll find a dozen reasons why this isn't really your fault. The terrain survey wasn't detailed enough. We didn't get enough prep time. The NCO didn't push harder when he brought it up. Anything to keep you from saying the simplest true sentence: I was wrong.

But the mission doesn't care about your feelings. The mission cares about the bridge getting built. And the team knows. They always know. You're not fooling anyone when you pretend your bad call was actually unavoidable circumstance. You're just insulting their intelligence on top of wasting their time.

So I walked over to that staff sergeant and I said, you were right. We should have staged this the way you suggested. I didn't listen and now we're paying for it. What's the fastest way to unfuck this?

And you know what he did? He didn't gloat. He didn't say I told you so. He just nodded and said, alright sir, here's what I'm thinking, and we spent five minutes sketching a new plan in the dirt and another forty-five minutes repositioning everything, and then we actually got to work. The whole exercise still ran long. We didn't make up that lost hour. But we stopped the bleeding and we got the job done and the after-action review wasn't nearly as bad as it could have been.

Here's what I learned, or maybe what I relearned, because this isn't the first time ego's bitten me: being a Warrant Officer is a weird spot. You're an officer, which means people expect you to lead and make decisions and own the plan. But you're also a technical expert, which means people expect you to know your stuff cold. And when those two things collide, when someone questions your technical call, it's real easy to take it personal. It's easy to hear, I think you're wrong about this, as, I don't think you're competent. And if you've got any pride at all, and we all do, your instinct is to defend yourself. To prove you know what you're talking about. To shut down the question before it makes you look weak.

But here's the thing: nobody's good at everything. I'm a decent engineer. I know my way around bridge ops and demo and horizontal construction. But that staff sergeant has forgotten more about field staging than I've ever learned, because he's done it a hundred times and I've done it maybe a dozen. And if I'm so busy protecting my reputation as the technical expert that I can't listen to someone who actually has more experience in a specific slice of the job, then I'm not leading. I'm just performing. I'm playacting the role of the guy who knows everything instead of actually being the guy who gets the mission done.

And that's what ego does. It turns leadership into performance. You start making decisions based on how they'll make you look instead of what will actually work. You avoid admitting mistakes because you think it'll cost you respect, when really it's the covering up and the defensiveness that costs you respect. You stop asking questions because you think leaders are supposed to have all the answers, and so you cut yourself off from the information you actually need.

There's another piece to this that's specific to the Guard. We're part-timers. One weekend a month, two weeks a year, and maybe some extra orders here and there. Which means the guys in your unit might have way more full-time experience in adjacent fields than you do. That staff sergeant I ignored? He's a heavy equipment operator in his civilian job. He moves dirt and positions machinery for a living. And I'm an electrician. I know voltage drop and load calculations and wire sizing. I don't know site logistics the way he does. And my ego almost cost us the whole training day because I couldn't admit that.

The other thing ego does, and this one's insidious, is it keeps you from learning. When you're defensive, when you're protecting your image, you stop being curious. Someone offers you a piece of information and instead of thinking, huh, is that true, should I adjust, you're thinking, how do I dismiss this without looking bad. And you stop growing. You plateau right where you are because you're too proud to admit there's still stuff you don't know.

I think about this with my faith, too. How many times have I heard something in church, or read something in the scriptures, or had my wife or a friend gently point something out, and my first instinct was to justify myself instead of actually consider whether I need to change? Ego doesn't just mess up your tactical decisions. It messes up your relationship with God. Because if you can't admit you're wrong, you can't repent. And if you can't repent, you can't progress. The gospel only works if you're willing to say, I got this wrong, I need help, I need to change. And that takes humility. That takes checking your ego at the door every single time.

So here's what I'm working on, and maybe this lands for you too: I'm trying to notice the feeling. That little flare of defensiveness when someone questions a call I made. That tightness in my chest when I realize I screwed something up and now I'm going to have to own it in front of people. I'm trying to notice that feeling and recognize it for what it is — ego, trying to protect me from something that doesn't actually need protecting. Because here's the truth: people don't follow you because you're perfect. They follow you because you're honest. Because when you mess up you say so and you fix it. Because you care more about the mission and the team than you do about looking good.

And in a Guard unit, where everybody's got a civilian life and we're all volunteers and nobody's getting rich off this, the only thing that actually holds the team together is trust. And trust dies the second people realize you'd rather protect your reputation than tell the truth.

I still think about that Saturday morning sometimes. I think about how close I came to wasting the whole day because I was too proud to listen. And I think about the fact that if I'd just had the humility to take that staff sergeant seriously two weeks earlier, we could have avoided the whole problem. The mission would have gone smoother. The team would have spent less time frustrated. And I would have learned something instead of having to learn it the hard way.

So here's your question for tomorrow, and it's the one I'm asking myself: where is your ego making you defensive right now? Is there a decision you're hanging onto not because it's the best call, but because changing it would mean admitting you were wrong? Is there someone who's tried to tell you something and you brushed them off because it stung your pride? Is there a mistake you're covering instead of owning?

And here's the harder question: what's that costing? Not just you, but the people you're leading. Because ego doesn't just hurt you. It hurts everyone downstream.

Check the ego. Notice the defensiveness. Kill it before it kills the mission. And maybe, just maybe, listen to the staff sergeant who's been doing this longer than you have.
