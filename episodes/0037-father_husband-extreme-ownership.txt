And then I caught myself. Because that question, that instinct to look at somebody else as the reason things fell apart — that's the opposite of what I'm supposed to be doing. That's the opposite of extreme ownership.

So tonight we're talking about extreme ownership, chapter one of the book by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin. The core idea is this — the leader is responsible for everything in their world. Every outcome. Every failure. Every gap. No excuses, no blame pushed down or sideways. When something goes wrong, you don't point at your team or your circumstances or bad luck. You look in the mirror first. You own it.

And if that sounds simple, good. It's supposed to be simple. The problem is it's not easy. Especially at home. Especially when you're tired and the house is a wreck and the kids are fighting and your spouse is frustrated and every instinct in your body wants to say, well I wasn't even here, how is this my fault?

Before I go any further, I need to connect this to something deeper, because even on a night like tonight when we're talking about being a husband and a dad, faith is still the foundation under all of it. Following Jesus Christ means dying to self. It means putting others first. It means when He said take up your cross daily, He wasn't talking about a bumper sticker, He was talking about a daily choice to kill your pride and serve. And extreme ownership, at its core, is that same posture — it's the refusal to protect your ego at someone else's expense. It's saying I will absorb the blame, I will carry the weight, because that's what a leader does. That's what Christ did. He owned sin He didn't commit so that we could be made whole. I'm not comparing changing diapers to the atonement, but I am saying the same heart that bows before Jesus is the same heart that says to your wife, you're right, I should have been home earlier, or says to your kid, I'm sorry I lost my temper, that was on me.

That's not ownership. That's me abdicating.

Here's the thing about extreme ownership at home — it doesn't mean you do everything yourself. It doesn't mean your wife isn't responsible for her part or the kids aren't responsible for theirs. It means that when something falls apart, you don't start by pointing at them. You start by asking what you could have done differently. What you failed to communicate. What you failed to plan for. What you failed to lead.

And most of the time, if you're honest, you'll find something.

Except she wasn't being difficult. I was. I said I'd help her, but I didn't actually help her. I didn't stop what I was doing. I didn't give her my full attention. I didn't set her up for success. That's not ownership. That's me being a bad leader.

So I went and found her. I apologized. I told her I wasn't really helping before, but I was going to now, and I put the laptop away, and we built the thing together. Took maybe twenty minutes. And it was good. Not because I'm father of the year, but because I stopped blaming her for my failure.

Here's what I'm learning about extreme ownership at home — it's daily. It's not a one time decision. It's waking up every morning and deciding that when things go wrong today, I'm going to look at what I did or didn't do before I look at what anyone else did or didn't do.

It's asking myself, did I set my kids up for success today, or did I just bark orders and expect them to figure it out? Did I communicate with my wife, or did I assume she'd read my mind? Did I make time for what matters, or did I let the urgent crowd out the important and then blame everyone else for the chaos?

Most days, if I'm honest, I fail at this more than I succeed. I get defensive. I make excuses. I justify. I blame the kids for not listening when I didn't really teach them. I blame my wife for being upset when I didn't really lead well. I tell myself I'm too tired, I'm too busy, I've got too much on my plate, how am I supposed to do everything?

Extreme ownership is the antidote to that. It's the choice to say, no, I'm the leader here, and if something's broken, I own it. Even if it's not fair. Even if I'm tired. Even if other people dropped the ball too. I still own it.
