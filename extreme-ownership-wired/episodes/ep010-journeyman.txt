Alright. So that's the morning that leads us to this week's principle. And the principle is Believe.

Believe is one of those words that sounds soft until you understand what it's actually asking you to do. In the context of Extreme Ownership, believe isn't about motivation posters or pep talks. It's about something very specific and very demanding: before you can lead anyone else into a plan, a task, a change, or a direction — you have to genuinely understand it well enough to believe in it yourself. And if you don't believe in it yet, you have to do the work to get there before you open your mouth to your crew.

The idea in the book is framed from the leadership side — meaning, senior leaders have a responsibility to explain the why behind decisions so the people executing can actually buy in. That's real and it matters. But the version of this principle that hits hardest for a Journeyman is the layer underneath that one. Because you are often in the middle. You're not the person who made the call, but you're the person who has to relay the call and lead the work. And the people around you — the apprentices, the helpers, the new guys — they're watching your face when you deliver the message. They're listening to your tone. They're taking their cues from you, not from Derek or the project manager or the GC.

So here's the trap. If you don't understand why a decision was made, and you don't do anything about that, one of two things happens. Either you relay it with zero conviction and everyone around you picks up on the fact that you're not sold on it — and now they're not sold on it either, and nobody's fully in. Or worse, you relay it with fake conviction, the kind that falls apart the second someone asks a follow-up question, and now you're standing there unable to explain the basic reason for what you're asking people to do. Neither of those situations ends well on a job site. Neither of those situations ends well anywhere.

The flip side — and this is the part that actually requires effort — is that you have to be willing to push upward when you genuinely don't understand the reason for something. Not in a confrontational way. Not in a "this is stupid" way. Just plainly: "Hey, before I brief my guys on this, can you help me understand what's driving this decision? I want to make sure I'm communicating it right." That's it. That's not a challenge. That's not resistance. That's a Journeyman doing the leadership work of actually caring about their crew's effectiveness.

And sometimes the answer you get will be totally logical, like outlets blocked by a built-in. And sometimes the answer will be something higher up the chain that you can't fully see from the ground floor. And in those cases, you don't have to agree with every decision — you just have to be honest with yourself about whether the people above you have context you don't have, whether they're generally making decisions that make sense over time, and whether this falls inside a pattern of reasonable leadership even if this specific call isn't your favorite. If the answer to those questions is yes, then you can walk back to your crew with actual conviction because your conviction is built on something real.

What you cannot do — what absolutely does not work — is what I did that Tuesday morning. Take a decision you don't understand, don't ask about it, decide silently that it's dumb, and then transmit that silent judgment to everyone around you through your tone and your body language and your throwaway explanation. Because you think you're just doing the work. But what you're actually doing is leading. You're just leading badly.

Okay. So what does this look like in practice for a Horsepower Electric Journeyman? Not theory, not vibes — actual behaviors you can run this week.

First thing: before you relay any instruction, change, or direction from above, give yourself thirty seconds to ask yourself whether you actually understand the reason behind it. Not whether you agree with it. Whether you understand it. Those are different questions. If you can answer "here's why we're doing this" in one clear sentence, you're ready. If you're standing there thinking "Derek said so," you're not ready. And that gap — that moment where you don't know the why — is not something to paper over. It's something to fix before you walk to your crew.

Second thing: get comfortable asking the question upward. The specific language matters here because the wrong phrasing sounds like pushback and the right phrasing sounds like preparation. Try this: "Before I pass this along, can you give me the context? I want to make sure my guys understand what we're trying to accomplish." That's it. It's not long. It's not emotional. It signals that you're taking the communication seriously. Most project managers, most GCs, most lead electricians will respect that immediately because it means you're thinking about execution, not just compliance. And the ones who don't respect it — well, now you have useful information about how communication flows on that job and you can adjust accordingly.

Third thing: when you brief your crew, lead with the why, not the what. This is the opposite of how most of us were trained, because on a job site the natural instinct is to get to the task as fast as possible. "We're moving outlets, interior walls, east wing, go." But if you spend twenty seconds before the task description saying, "The owner has a built-in going on that exterior wall and we'd have outlets completely blocked — so we're relocating them to the interior to keep everything accessible," now your crew has a picture in their head. The work means something. The measurement matters because it's serving a real outcome, not just satisfying a change order. You'd be amazed how much more accurate and careful people get when they understand what the work is for.

Fourth thing: watch for the "sure" tone in yourself. You know the one. It's the flat agreement that isn't really agreement. You say yes but everything else about you says no. And the reason this matters isn't just about morale — it's about errors. People who are going through the motions because a leader they respect signaled that the work wasn't worth caring about — those people make mistakes. Measurement errors. Wrong circuits. Missed locations. The connection between a leader's level of belief and the quality of work that follows is not abstract. It is direct. If you catch yourself delivering a "sure" when you mean "I don't understand this," stop. Go back up the chain. Get the answer. Then come back and brief for real.

There's one more piece of this that doesn't get talked about enough in the electrical trade specifically, and it's this: on high-end residential work, the GC's reputation and your company's reputation are both running through every single decision you make at rough-in. These aren't track homes where you can go back and move things later without it being a whole thing. The homeowners are paying a premium, the design is deliberate, and the changes that feel arbitrary almost always have a reason even when that reason isn't communicated well. Learning to extend some default trust to the process — not blind trust, not unquestioning compliance, but default trust with active curiosity — is one of the things that separates a Journeyman who gets handed responsibility from one who gets handed the same tasks over and over. When you show up understanding the why, your crew works better and the people above you notice. Both of those things matter.

Alright, let's head over to the Wells Cutting Edge side of the yard. Same week, different boots in the mud, same principle about to cause the same kind of quiet damage in a completely different way.

Picture this. It's late spring. Perfect planting window. Wells Cutting Edge is on a big install — high-end residential, the kind where the design package came from a landscape architect, not a home improvement store brochure. We're talking custom paver patio, pergola footings, full irrigation zone build-out, and a planting plan that the homeowner has been emotionally attached to for approximately eight months based on the number of laminated photos she handed the crew lead on day one.

I'm the Journeyman on this job. My name for today is Marcus, though that's not my real name, it's just easier than saying "the Journeyman." Marcus is running the irrigation and planting crew while another team is handling the hardscape paver work around the corner. Good split. We each have our lane.

About midway through the second week of the install, the crew lead — I'll call him Phil — Phil comes over and tells me that the landscape architect has updated the planting beds on the south side of the property. The original plan had a row of ornamental grasses along the fence line as a screening element. Standard stuff, looks great, we've planted them a hundred times. The new plan swaps them out for a low-growing flowering shrub instead — different height, different water needs, different spacing.

Phil says the architect made the change based on feedback from the HOA's design review. Something about sight lines and fence height restrictions. Phil is relaying this in the same tone Derek used on me in the electrical story — fast, breezy, "not a big deal," and gone before I can ask a follow-up question. He's got three other fires burning on this job and I'm apparently the fire that's already out.

Now here is where Marcus makes the same mistake I made, except Marcus makes it in a slightly different flavor. Marcus doesn't do the quiet eye-roll thing. Marcus does the competent-professional-who-adapts thing, which on the surface looks better but has the same rotten core. Marcus goes, "Got it, we'll adjust," and then goes directly to his crew and says, "Change of plans, we're pulling the ornamental grass order and installing flowering shrubs instead. Spacing is different, water needs are different, check the updated plan." Sounds fine, right? He even briefed the specifics. He even mentioned the spacing. But he never said why the change happened. And more importantly, Marcus himself had no actual idea why the change happened, because he never asked. He just trusted that Phil probably knew what he was doing and moved on.

Here's where the texture of this mistake shows up differently in landscaping than it does in electrical. In electrical, the error tends to be quick and measurable — wrong box location, wrong circuit, something you can point to on an inspection. In landscaping, the error can be slow and invisible until it isn't. And what happened on the Wells Cutting Edge job was this: Marcus's most experienced hand on the planting crew — guy who has been doing residential installs for six years, really knows his plants — this guy starts second-guessing the change while he's digging. He's thinking the same thing Marcus is half-thinking in the back of his head, which is that the ornamental grasses were the right plant for that application and whoever made this call doesn't totally know what they're doing. And so he spaces the new shrubs based on how he would have done it, not based on the updated plan, because in his head the updated plan is suspect and his experience is more trustworthy than a spec sheet from an architect who, in his view, made a bad call.

You see where this is going. The spacing ends up wrong. Not drastically wrong, not "we have to dig everything up" wrong — but wrong enough that by the end of the season those shrubs are going to be crowding each other, and by the following year the homeowner is going to be looking at a fence line that looks messy instead of designed, and Wells Cutting Edge is going to get a call that is not a happy call.

And when Phil eventually looks at the spacing on a site walk and flags it, the experienced hand says, "I went off my experience, I didn't think the spec was right for the conditions." And Phil looks at Marcus. And Marcus has nothing to say because Marcus never gave his crew a reason to trust the updated plan. He just handed them a task with no foundation under it. And the best guy on the crew, a guy who genuinely cares about doing good work, used his experience in the absence of a reason — and it went sideways.

That HOA restriction, by the way, was completely real and completely reasonable. The ornamental grasses at full height would have violated the sight-line clearance rule along that fence line. The shrub was selected specifically because it tops out below the threshold. The landscape architect wasn't guessing — she was solving a problem that would have caused a permit issue down the road. If Marcus had known that, and if he'd told his crew that, the experienced hand would have installed it to spec because the spec would have made sense to him. His expertise would have been in service of the plan instead of in conflict with it.

Alright. So what does Believe look like in practice for a Wells Cutting Edge Journeyman? Let's get specific.

First: same thirty-second rule as the electric crew. Before you relay a plan change, an updated spec, or a decision that came from above — ask yourself if you can explain the reason in one clear sentence. "We're using flowering shrubs instead of ornamental grasses because the grasses would violate the HOA sight-line restriction at the fence line." One sentence. If you can say that, you're ready to brief your crew. If all you can say is "Phil said to change it," you need to go back to Phil. The landscaping world specifically has a lot of changes that come through the chain without explanation — material substitutions, plant swaps, grading adjustments, irrigation zone splits — and the default culture is often just to adapt and keep moving. That's not wrong. The speed is valuable. But the speed cannot come at the cost of your crew understanding what the work is for.

Second: in landscaping more than almost any other trade, your crew includes people with real expertise and real opinions. That six-year installer knows a lot. The irrigation tech who's been doing this since before you were on the crew — she knows things the landscape architect doesn't know. That expertise is an asset and you absolutely want it available. But expertise without context is dangerous. It fills the vacuum where the reason for a decision should be. And the only way to keep expertise pointed at the problem instead of at the plan is to make sure the plan makes sense. When people understand why they're doing what they're doing, their experience becomes a tool for execution. When they don't, their experience becomes a competing authority.

Third: on this side of the trade, there's a timing element to belief that is specific to planting and installation sequencing. Once something goes in the ground, the window to fix it cheaply is very short. Grading errors, plant spacing errors, irrigation head placement that doesn't match the zone map — these things have compounding consequences in a way that a wiring mistake in rough-in does not, because you can usually re-run wire a lot easier than you can re-grade a yard or replant an established bed. This means that for a Wells Cutting Edge Journeyman, the moment to ask the question is always before the material goes in. Before the hole gets dug. Before the plant gets staged at the bed. If you're standing there with a shrub in your hand and your crew lead said to put it here but you don't understand why here and not six inches that way — that is the last clean moment to ask. Use it. The question you don't ask before the install is a conversation Phil has with the homeowner later, and nobody wants that conversation.

Fourth: the specific phrase that tends to work really well in the landscape trade for getting the why out of a crew lead or a project manager is some version of, "Help me understand the driver here so I can brief the crew right." Not "why are we doing this" — that can sound like resistance. "Help me understand the driver so I can brief the crew right" — that sounds like preparation. It signals that you're thinking about execution quality, not just questioning the decision. And it frames the ask in terms of your crew, which is the right frame because that's the actual reason you're asking.

One more thing about Believe in a landscaping context specifically: this is a trade where the homeowner is physically present in a way that electrical homeowners often aren't. Landscaping happens outside. Homeowners walk through it. They show up on Saturday mornings with coffee and questions. They point at things. And when a homeowner points at something and says, "Why is it like this?" — your crew is going to answer. They may not intend to answer, but they will. The installer who is quietly skeptical of the plan will give a subtly different answer than the installer who understands the plan and believes in it. Homeowners are perceptive. They are paying for a magazine-ready yard and they notice hesitation. When your crew understands what they're building and why, they talk about it with appropriate confidence. When they don't, they talk about it with the vague discomfort of people who are following instructions they don't fully trust. That difference shows up in ways you cannot fully control once it's out there.

So now we're back to the same Tuesday morning, two different job sites, two different crews, two different trades — and the exact same mistake wearing completely different work boots.

A Horsepower Electric Journeyman passes along a scope change he doesn't understand, signals to his crew that it doesn't make sense, and watches precision drop in real time. A Wells Cutting Edge Journeyman passes along a plan change he doesn't understand, gives his crew no reason to trust the updated spec, and watches the best guy on the crew fill the trust vacuum with his own judgment — not because he's being difficult, but because experienced people always find a way to make sense of their work, and if you don't give them sense, they'll make their own.

Both of those stories are about the same failure. The failure of the person in the middle. Not the person at the top who maybe could have communicated better. Not the apprentice who made the measurement error. Not the experienced hand who spaced the shrubs wrong. The person in the middle, who had a gap in their own understanding, didn't close it, and then sent that gap downstream where it multiplied.

Believe is not about agreeing with everything. It's not about pretending you have no reservations. It is about taking responsibility for your own understanding before you take responsibility for anyone else's. It is about being willing to go back up the chain and say, "I need to understand this better before I can lead it well." And it's about recognizing that your conviction — or your lack of it — is contagious, and your crew deserves to catch something worth catching.

Both crews, same deal. Electric and landscape, same deal. You're the middle of the chain. Act like it.

If you don't believe in the mission, neither will the people behind you.
