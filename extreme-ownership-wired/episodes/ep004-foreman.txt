So it's seven-fifteen on a Tuesday morning, and I'm standing in the kitchen of what is going to be — allegedly, eventually — a four-million-dollar custom home in the hills. The GC has already called me twice before I finished my coffee, which is never a good sign. The framers wrapped their rough-in two days early, which sounds like good news until you remember that "two days early" is just another way of saying "you have two days less than you planned." And I'm walking the space with my lead guy — I'll call him Donnie — and Donnie is explaining to me why the rough-in for the kitchen island isn't done yet.

Now Donnie is a good electrician. He has been on more custom residential jobs than most guys twice his age. He knows how to read a set of plans, he knows how to talk to a GC without starting a fistfight, and he has never once shown up without his tools. Donnie is a good electrician. But right now, Donnie is doing what I have come to recognize as the thing that good electricians do when something went wrong and they want me to understand that it is not, under any circumstances, their fault. He is pointing.

He is pointing at the plans. He is pointing at the island location. He is pointing at the spot on the subfloor where the island conduit should be stubbed up, and then he is pointing — with real feeling, with genuine conviction — at the other crew who had been in the space running low-voltage wire for the AV contractor. And he looks at me like, there it is. Mystery solved. Case closed. Not my problem.

And I remember standing there, looking at the uncompleted rough-in, thinking about the GC waiting on me to give him a timeline, thinking about the inspection that was now going to have to be pushed, thinking about the homeowner who had already emailed the GC three times asking why the electrical wasn't done yet — and I had this very human, very understandable instinct, which was: Donnie's got a point. The AV crew really did move through there. That really was a disruption. Maybe I bring this to the GC and I lay out the situation and I let him know that this wasn't really our fault and we just need a couple more days and —

And then I stopped. Because I'd read the chapter. And something in the chapter had stuck in my brain like a splinter that you can't quite get out. And the thing that was stuck was this: it doesn't matter. It genuinely does not matter whose fault it was in the moment. Because this job is mine. This crew is mine. This timeline is mine. And if the rough-in isn't done, the name that is attached to the rough-in not being done is my name.

That's the episode. That's the whole thing, really. Everything else we're going to talk about today is just that moment, unwrapped. Let me tell you what happened next, and then I'll actually explain the principle, and then we'll get into what you can do with it this week — both on the electrical side and over at the Wells Cutting Edge crew. Because this one hits just as hard whether you're pulling wire or setting pavers.

So. Back to Donnie. I'm standing there. I'm looking at the uncompleted rough-in. And instead of doing the thing where I carry Donnie's excuse upstairs to the GC like a little package, I said, "Okay. What do we need to get this wrapped today?" Not "what happened," not "whose fault is it," not "let's revisit Thursday." Today. What do we need.

And Donnie kind of blinked. Because he had come into this conversation ready to defend, and I had just taken the defense off the table. He said, "I mean, if I had another guy this morning I could get the island stubbed up by noon and finish the rest of the circuit runs by end of day." I said, "Done. I'm pulling Marco off the panel schedule and he's yours until noon." Donnie said, "And the AV crew can't come back in here until I clear it." And that was it. By noon, the island was done. By four o'clock, we were ready for inspection. I've fixed the staffing, we're on track for end of day, and I'll send you a photo when we're ready for inspection." Not, "well the AV crew came through and our staging got disrupted and technically if you look at the sequence of events —" None of that. My job. My crew. My call.

The GC, who is a hard man to impress, said, "Okay." And he walked away. And that was better than anything else I could have gotten out of that conversation.

Now let's talk about why that works. Because the Extreme Ownership principle — and this is what Jocko Willink and Leif Babin are really getting at in this chapter — is not about beating yourself up. It's not about taking the blame so that everyone else feels comfortable. It's not a performance. It's a mindset, and it's a practical tool, and they come from the same place.

Here's the idea. When a leader — and as a foreman, you are a leader, that is the job — when a leader owns everything in their world completely, it closes off all the exits. Not the exits for the crew, not the exits for the company. The exits for the excuses. And when the exits for excuses are closed, you are left with only one thing to look at, which is: what can I do right now to fix this?

The reason this is so hard is because it feels wrong. It feels unjust. If the AV crew really did disrupt Donnie's staging area, then saying "my fault" feels like lying. It feels like letting someone off the hook who should be on the hook. But here's what Willink and Babin understood from leading SEAL teams in combat — and what translates surprisingly cleanly to a four-million-dollar custom home in the hills — is that you don't own the outcome in order to assign blame. You own the outcome because you are the only one positioned to fix it. A SEAL platoon commander in Ramadi, Iraq isn't sitting around asking whose fault the ambush was. He's asking what he can do in the next thirty seconds. Ownership isn't about guilt. It's about control.

And there's a second layer to this that I think is even harder to see from the foreman seat. When you deflect — even legitimately, even when you're right — you are training your crew to deflect. You are telling everyone around you that "not my fault" is a valid response to a problem. And once that's the culture, once that's the default move when something goes wrong, you will spend more energy on your job site tracking down whose fault things are than actually fixing them. And the guy who built that culture, the guy who normalized deflection as a response to failure, is the foreman. Is you. Is me.

When you own it instead, you are doing something different. You're modeling. You are showing Donnie — and Marco, and whoever else is watching — that the move when something goes wrong is not to point, it's to problem-solve. And that compounds. That scales. After a while you start to see your guys doing it with each other. You start to see them coming to you with "here's what happened and here's how I'm fixing it" instead of "here's a list of reasons this isn't my problem." That transformation, that shift in crew culture, it starts with the foreman, and it starts in that moment when the easy move is to pass the blame upstairs and you don't.

There's one more piece I want to name before we get into the practical stuff, and it's this: ownership doesn't mean you ignore legitimate upstream problems. If the AV crew is consistently disrupting your staging and costing you time, you still raise that. But you raise it as a process problem with a proposed solution, not as an excuse for why your deliverable is late. "Hey, the current sequencing between electrical rough-in and AV rough-in is creating conflicts. Here's what I think the solution is." That is ownership. That is a leader talking. That is very different from "the AV guys came through and wrecked my Thursday and that's why we're behind." Even if both statements describe the same facts, they are not the same statement. One closes the loop. One opens a complaint.

Alright. Let's talk about what this actually looks like for a Horsepower Electric Foreman on a real job this week.

The first thing is the morning check-in, and most foremen do some version of this already, but I want you to think about what it's really for. When you walk the job in the morning — before the GC sees you, before the homeowner texts, before anything else — you are not just checking status. You are checking for problems that are going to become your problem whether you find them now or find them at three in the afternoon when it's too late to fix them. The framing on panel clearance. The outlet that got moved by the drywall crew and now doesn't match your rough-in. The wire run that got dinged by another trade. You find these things in the morning, you own them in the morning, and you have the whole day to solve them. You find them at three in the afternoon and all you can do is report them and apologize. The morning walk is your ownership tool. Use it like one.

The second thing is how you talk to the GC when something goes wrong. And I know, I know — there is a real temptation to front-load the context. To lead with the circumstances so that the GC understands the full picture before he judges the outcome. But here's what GCs hear when you do that. They hear: he's about to tell me something is late, and he's warming me up for it, and none of what he's saying is going to change the fact that something is late. And they stop listening. They wait for the part that matters. What I've learned to do is lead with the problem and the fix, then give the context if they ask. "We're going to need one more day on the kitchen rough-in. I've adjusted staffing and we'll be inspection-ready by end of day tomorrow. Here's why it happened and here's what I'm doing differently going forward." That order matters. Problem. Fix. Cause. Solution. Not: cause, cause, cause, problem, maybe fix. Lead with what you're going to do about it, not with why it happened.

Third — and this one is specifically about your relationship with your crew — when someone on your team makes a mistake, your first question out loud should not be "why did this happen" and it should definitely not be "who did this." Your first question out loud should be "how do we fix it." Because the moment you make "who did this" the first question, you have just told your entire crew that the priority when something goes wrong is self-protection. And they will protect themselves accordingly, which means they will hide things from you. Which means you will find out about problems later than you should, when they are bigger than they should be, and you will have less time to fix them. If you want your crew to bring problems to you early, you have to make it safe to bring problems to you early. And the way you make it safe is to visibly, consistently, make the first move "how do we fix it."

Fourth — and this one's about the inspection. You already know the inspection is the moment of truth. The inspector doesn't care about the AV crew. The inspector doesn't care about the scope change the GC dropped on you in week two. The inspector is looking at your work, and your work is going to pass or it isn't. The ownership move before every inspection is a self-inspection, and it's not a formality — it's you walking the panel, walking the circuits, walking the rough-in, looking for every single thing an inspector might flag, and either fixing it or having an answer for it before he walks in the door. Panel clearance. Breaker labeling. Box fill. Junction box covers. All of it. If the inspector finds something you already knew about, that's one thing. If the inspector finds something you could have caught and didn't, that's a different thing. The self-inspection is ownership in its most mechanical form. It says: this work belongs to me, and I have checked it.

Now let's head over to the Wells Cutting Edge side of the yard. Same principle, same perspective — a foreman looking at a mess and deciding what to do with it. Just different dirt.

It's a Thursday, mid-morning, and I'm at a job site that is — in theory — four days from a final walkthrough with one of those homeowners who has a Pinterest board with over six thousand pins and has shown it to me twice. The hardscape is in. The planting plan is done. The irrigation is supposed to be pressure-tested and we're supposed to be seeding the turf sections and cleaning up for the punch list. This is the home stretch. Everything is lined up.

Except. I'm getting a call from my crew lead — I'll call him Ray — and Ray is telling me that the paver section along the east terrace is showing a lip. A visible lip, the kind where one paver is sitting proud of its neighbor and you can see it from across the yard and you definitely cannot walk across it in heels without catching, which this particular homeowner absolutely will do. And Ray is telling me how it happened, which is that the pool contractor's crew came through three weeks ago to trench a conduit for their equipment pad, and when they backfilled, they didn't compact properly in one section, and over the last few weeks that section has settled, and that settlement has shifted the base under two rows of pavers, and here we are.

And Ray's not wrong about the sequence of events. The pool contractor's crew really did trench there. They really didn't compact correctly. I actually saw that backfill go in and I thought at the time that it looked a little light, and I made a mental note, and then I walked away and did not follow up, and that mental note turned into a memory of a thought I once had, and now it is Thursday and we are four days out and there is a lip in the east terrace.

That last part is the part that matters. I saw it. I didn't flag it. I moved on. And everything that happened after that — the paver installation over a compromised base, the weeks of settlement, the lip that is now visible from across the yard — all of that follows directly from the moment I saw the light backfill and decided it was someone else's problem to solve.

Now I could call the pool contractor. I could, technically, make a case that this is their issue because it was their trench and their backfill and their compaction failure. And maybe eventually there's a conversation to have about who pays for what. But here's what I know: that conversation, if I try to have it right now, four days before a final walkthrough, is going to take longer than four days. The homeowner does not care who compacted what. The homeowner has a final walkthrough in four days and she has a Pinterest board and she is going to stand on the east terrace and look down and see the lip, and the company name on the contract she signed is Wells Cutting Edge, and that is the name that is going to be in her head in that moment.

So I said to Ray, "What does it take to fix it?" And Ray said, "We pull those two rows, we re-compact the base, we relay, we re-polymeric-sand, and we need a full day — probably six hours of work with the right crew." I said, "What do you need from me?" He said, "I need another body and I need the time blocked out tomorrow." I said, "You've got it. I'll move Hector off the drainage punch list, he's yours tomorrow at seven." And then I called the GC and told him we had found a settling issue in the east terrace pavers, we had identified the cause, we were fixing it tomorrow, and the walkthrough timeline was still intact.

He asked what caused it. I told him the base in that section had settled after the pool contractor's trench work and that I should have flagged the compaction quality when I saw it and I didn't. He was quiet for a second. He said, "Okay, let me know when it's done." That was the conversation. No back and forth. No blame reassignment. No "well technically if you talk to the pool guys." Just: problem identified, fix in progress, timeline intact. Done.

And here's the thing about the east terrace. The fix was not that hard. Six hours, two guys, and we had a perfect terrace. The hard part was not the fix. The hard part was getting to the fix without spending a day and a half trying to make it someone else's problem first. That's where time goes on a job site. Not in the actual repair work. In the circling and the pointing and the phone calls and the "well technically" conversations. Ownership cuts all of that out. It goes straight to: what do we do, who does it, when does it happen.

Alright. Let's talk about what this looks like in practice for a Wells Cutting Edge Foreman. What does Extreme Ownership actually look like on a high-end landscape job this week.

First, and this is directly out of the east terrace story: when another trade does work that touches your scope, you don't check it for courtesy and move on. You flag it or you own what happens next. The pool contractor's backfill wasn't your job. But the pavers that went on top of it were your job. And the ground under those pavers was your job once you decided to set pavers on it. The ownership move, the moment I saw that light compaction, was to stop the process. To say to the pool contractor's crew, or to the GC, "I'm not setting pavers over that section until that base is properly compacted." One conversation. One slightly awkward moment. Versus: four days out from a final walkthrough, pulling and resetting two rows of pavers. The check-in on another trade's work is ownership in practice. It's saying: everything that happens on this job site in the footprint of my work is my responsibility, including the conditions I inherit.

Second: the pre-walkthrough self-audit. And I mean this one seriously, because on a landscape job there are so many things that can look almost right while not being quite right, and the homeowner on a high-end residential job has usually stared at design boards and Pinterest boards for long enough that she will find the almost-right things. The irrigation head that's spraying at eighty percent coverage instead of one hundred because the pressure is a little off. The plant that's in the right location on the plan but slightly off-center from the visual axis the designer intended. The polymeric sand that didn't fully cure in one joint because of the rain last week. The screening hedge that's two plants short of the HOA minimum height requirement. These things don't announce themselves. You have to go looking for them. And going looking for them before the homeowner does is ownership. Going looking for them the day after the homeowner finds them is damage control. Same walk, very different outcome, and the only difference is whether you did it before or after.

Third: the way you run the end-of-day check with your crew. On a big install, there are always small decisions that happen out in the field that your crew is making without you — a plant gets moved eighteen inches because of a root they hit, an irrigation head gets relocated because of a grade issue, a paver pattern gets shifted at the edge because the cut didn't land right. These things happen. They are going to happen. The question is whether you know about them at the end of the day when you can assess and decide, or whether you find out about them at the final walkthrough when the homeowner points at the gap and says, "That wasn't in the design." You want your crew to bring these things to you. And they will bring them to you if you have built a culture where bringing things to you is safer than hiding them. Which means when someone brings you a problem at end of day, your first response can't be "why didn't you call me immediately" or "who made that call." Your first response is: "What does it look like, and is it something we fix tonight or tomorrow?" Curiosity before judgment. Every time. It is the only way to stay informed on a complex install.

Fourth, and this is the one that's specific to the client relationship on high-end residential landscape work, which is a different animal from commercial work: when something goes wrong, the homeowner is going to find out. That is the baseline assumption. These homeowners are not in a corporate office somewhere looking at a construction report. They are driving by every day. They are standing at their kitchen window with a cup of coffee looking at your crew work. They see things. And if they find out about a problem by seeing it themselves and then asking about it, you are playing defense for the rest of the relationship. If they find out because you called them or the GC called them and said "here's what happened and here's what we're doing," you are still in charge of the narrative. You are the professional who identified the issue and fixed it. Ownership of the communication, not just the problem, is part of the job. When something goes sideways, the instinct is to wait until it's fixed to say anything, because then you can lead with the solution. But on a high-end residential landscape job, waiting can cost you. Sometimes the homeowner is going to walk the property before the fix is done. And if she sees the pulled pavers and doesn't know why, and she has to call the GC to find out what's happening, that is a different conversation than if you had called the GC that morning and said "we identified a settling issue, we're on it today." Control the information. Own the communication. Don't let the homeowner write the story.

So here's where we land, across both crews, on both sides of the yard.

The foreman is the accountability point. Not because everything is always the foreman's fault in some cosmic, literal sense. Obviously Donnie was behind on the rough-in. Obviously the pool contractor's crew didn't compact the backfill correctly. These things happened. The foreman didn't cause every problem in isolation. But the foreman is the one who determines what happens next. And the leader who says "my job, my fix, my call" gets to that next step faster than the leader who has to first figure out who to be angry at.

The thing that both stories have in common — the kitchen island that wasn't done and the east terrace that was settling — is that in both cases, the problem was actually fixable. It was always fixable. The risk was never "can we fix this." The risk was "how long do we spend not fixing this before we start." And every minute spent not fixing it is a minute where the GC is getting annoyed, the homeowner is getting nervous, the inspection is slipping, the walkthrough is in jeopardy. Ownership is how you close the gap between "problem exists" and "problem is being fixed." It's a tool that goes right at the thing that matters most.

And I'll leave you with this. No matter what trade you're in, no matter which crew you show up to tomorrow morning, the question that separates a leader from a complainer is not "whose fault is this." It's "what am I going to do about it."
