Here's Episode 1:

---

So there I am, standing in the middle of a half-framed master bedroom on a Tuesday morning, holding a rough-in box in one hand and my phone in the other, and my journeyman is looking at me like I just told him the sky was green. "Bro," he says, slow, the way people talk when they are choosing their words very carefully because the alternative is yelling, "where are the boxes for the walk-in closet?"

I look at my phone. I look at the plans. I look at the doorway leading to what is very clearly a walk-in closet. And I feel that particular stomach-drop that every apprentice knows, the one that starts somewhere around your ribs and lands somewhere in the general vicinity of your kneecaps, because I know exactly where those boxes are. They are in my van. In the milk crate I pulled them from this morning. Because I grabbed the master bedroom boxes, I grabbed the bathroom boxes, and I looked at the stack and I thought, yeah, that looks right, and I drove to the job.

That feeling right there? That is today's episode.

Welcome to Extreme Ownership: Wired. I'm your narrator, and for the next half hour or so, we are going to talk about one of the most important ideas in leadership, and we are going to do it the way we do things around here — through the kind of stories that make you laugh a little because you recognize yourself in them. We cover both crews, Horsepower Electric and Wells Cutting Edge, same principle, same perspective, two different trades, because whether you're pulling wire or installing pavers, the best leadership idea in the world doesn't mean anything until it lands in your actual day.

This is Episode 1, the first chapter, the foundation that everything else in this series is going to be built on. And appropriately, the principle is the one the book is named after: Extreme Ownership.

Let's go back to that bedroom.

My journeyman's name — for the purpose of this story — is Marco. Marco has been in the trade for eleven years. He is not a yeller. He is worse than a yeller. He is a very quiet, very calm, very disappointed person, and when he looks at you with that expression, you feel it in places you didn't know you had feelings. He exhales through his nose. Once. And then he says, "Okay. What happened?"

Now here is the thing about that moment. I had options. And I want to walk you through the options, because this is important, because I genuinely considered every single one of them in the approximately three seconds between his question and my answer.

Option one: the plans. We had gotten a revised set of plans from the GC two days before, and look, if you squint at the original set and the revised set, the closet dimensions changed. They changed by four inches. Four inches. And that means technically the box layout shifted, and if I had followed the original plans — which I may or may not have been referencing when I loaded up the morning before — that could be construed as a planning communication issue. I could point to the plans.

Option two: the supplier. My supplier had repacked our order at some point, and the boxes for different rooms had gotten mixed into the same bag, and I had to sort them out myself that morning. Which was annoying. Which I had mentioned to nobody, but which had happened. I could point to the confusion that created.

Option three: the framer. The framer had shifted the closet doorway about six inches from where it was marked on the slab, because framers do that, and it was a whole thing, and that had created some general uncertainty about where we were staging rough-in for that corner of the room. I could point to the framer.

I want to be honest with you. All three of those things actually happened. None of them are made up. And in the moment, standing there with Marco's quiet disappointment pointed at me like a flashlight, every single one of them felt like a pretty reasonable thing to say.

But here is what I said instead, and it wasn't because I was particularly mature or wise, it was because I had learned by then that Marco could hear a deflection coming from about a mile away and nothing made the conversation longer than trying one: "I didn't count my boxes before I loaded. I just grabbed what looked like enough."

Marco looks at me. He nods. And then he says, "Okay. So what are we doing right now?" Not in a punishing way. In a here's-the-problem, here's-the-next-question way. And we figured it out. I drove back to the shop, which was eighteen minutes away, I grabbed the closet boxes, I was back on site within forty minutes, and we made our rough-in schedule for that room by end of day. Did it cost us time? Yes. Did it cost us anything else? No. Because the moment I stopped trying to explain why it wasn't my fault, we could spend our energy on what to actually do about it.

Here is what I didn't understand then that I understand now. The plans thing, the supplier thing, the framer thing — those were all real. The plans had changed. The supplier had repacked the order. The framer had moved the door. All of that is true. And none of it loaded those boxes into my van. I did that. I chose to grab what looked right without verifying. And the moment I said "I didn't count my boxes," I wasn't erasing those other factors. I was just being honest about the one factor that I could control, which was also the only factor that was going to get us out of the jam.

That is actually the whole idea. That is Extreme Ownership. And I want to explain it properly before we get to the practical stuff, because it gets misunderstood all the time, and the misunderstanding is what makes people resist it.

Here is what Extreme Ownership is not. It is not self-flagellation. It is not standing in the middle of a jobsite going, "I'm terrible, I'm the worst, everything is my fault, I deserve whatever happens to me." That is not useful. That is theater. Nobody on a real crew has time for theater, and more importantly, the guys who do that version are usually doing it in a performative way that still keeps the conversation focused on them and their feelings rather than on the problem. Fake ownership is still a form of avoidance. It just wears a different costume.

Here is also what Extreme Ownership is not: a free pass for everyone around you to drop the ball. If the supplier repacks your order wrong every week and you just keep absorbing it and saying "my fault, my fault, my fault," you are not leading, you are just being a punching bag with good manners. Owning your role in a problem doesn't mean pretending other people don't have roles too. It means that you don't let their role become your excuse for your role.

What Extreme Ownership actually is, is this: as an apprentice on a jobsite, you are responsible for your piece of the job. Not in a legal or HR sense. In a practical, what-actually-gets-done sense. And when something goes wrong inside your piece — or because of your piece — your job is to be the first one to say "here is what I did or didn't do that contributed to this," and to be completely honest about it, without padding or softening, and then to pivot immediately to "here is what I'm going to do about it right now."

The reason this is powerful is actually pretty simple. When you take ownership, you stop waiting. You don't wait to see if someone else is going to notice. You don't wait to see if you can avoid the conversation. You don't wait to see if the problem resolves itself, which it almost never does on a construction site because problems on construction sites have a way of metastasizing with remarkable speed. You act. And acting fast is almost always what saves the day.

There is also something that happens to the people around you when you own your mistakes quickly and clearly. Marco didn't make me feel bad about the closet boxes because I told him immediately and directly what had happened. The conversation lasted about ninety seconds. If I had spent those ninety seconds pointing at the plans and the supplier and the framer, we would have been in that conversation for ten minutes, and at the end of it, Marco would have known exactly what I was doing, and the closet boxes would still not be on site, and I would have wasted everyone's morning including my own. Ownership is faster. That sounds counterintuitive because we think admitting a mistake is the painful part and avoiding it is the easy part. But the avoiding is where all the time actually goes.

One more thing before we get to the practical. This principle is called Extreme Ownership for a reason. The word extreme is doing real work there. It's not "ownership when it's obvious you were clearly at fault." It's ownership even when you have a pretty good case for why it wasn't your fault. It's ownership even when other people contributed to the problem. It's ownership even when the thing that went wrong was partly outside your control. Because there is almost always something inside your control — some choice you made, some thing you didn't verify, some moment where you assumed instead of confirmed — and that is the piece that belongs to you. Find that piece. Own that piece. The rest takes care of itself.

Now let's talk about what this actually looks like for a Horsepower Electric apprentice in a real week on a real job.

First thing, and this is the most basic version, is loading verification. I know this sounds small. I know. But the number of problems that start because an apprentice grabbed what looked like enough — boxes, wire, breakers, connectors, whatever — rather than counting against a list is genuinely staggering. Extreme Ownership means making a list every morning, every load, every time. Not in your head. On paper or in your phone. And checking it before the van leaves the shop or the yard. This is ownership in advance. You are taking responsibility for the day before anything can go wrong, rather than scrambling to own it after something already has.

The practical version is this: before you load, you ask yourself "what does this task actually require, and how do I know I have it?" If the answer is "I think I grabbed it" or "I'm pretty sure we have enough," you go check. You count. You verify. And if your journeyman or your foreman has a list or a pull sheet, you don't just glance at it, you read it, and you confirm out loud that what you've got matches it. Fifteen minutes of checking prevents four hours of problem-solving. That is not a metaphor. That is a literal time comparison from a real kind of situation.

Second thing: catch-and-report. On a high-end residential job, there is almost always a moment during rough-in where you notice something that doesn't look quite right. Maybe you're running wire and you get to the panel location and the framing is tighter than it should be for the clearance requirement. Maybe you pull up the lighting plan and you realize the circuit you're on doesn't match where the fixtures are going, and someone must have updated one document but not the other. Maybe you're setting boxes and the count in the plan is one short for what the room obviously needs.

An apprentice who doesn't own their work sees that moment and keeps going, because maybe it's not their problem, maybe someone else is handling it, maybe it'll work out. An apprentice who does own their work stops, and says something, and says it specifically. Not "hey, there's a thing over here that seems weird." That is the verbal equivalent of looking the other way. Specific means: "Marco, I'm at the panel location and the framing leaves me about six inches less clearance than the code requires. I haven't drilled yet. I wanted to check with you before I went further." That sentence takes twelve seconds. It gets the right person looking at the right problem at the moment when it can still be addressed without tearing anything out. That is ownership.

Third thing — and this is the one that takes the most guts — is the early admission. This is different from catch-and-report, because catch-and-report is about noticing something before it becomes a problem. The early admission is about noticing that you already made a mistake and saying so before you're asked. Before the inspection, before the journeyman does a walk, before the GC notices. You go find the right person and you say, with no drama, "Hey, I want to flag something. I set the outlet in the master bath at twelve inches and it should be twenty — I just caught it, and I want to figure out the fix before we close that wall."

Here is the honest truth about why apprentices don't do this. They are afraid that admitting the mistake will be worse than hiding it and hoping for the best. And sometimes — rarely, but sometimes — there is a foreman or a journeyman who makes that fear justified by responding to honesty with anger. If that's your situation, I'm not saying it's easy. I'm saying that even in that situation, the early admission almost always results in a smaller problem than the late discovery. Drywall is expensive. Callbacks are expensive. Inspection failures have downstream costs that can affect everyone on the crew. The thing you're afraid to admit is almost always cheaper to fix the day you caused it than the day someone else finds it.

Fourth thing: the debrief ask. At the end of the day, or at the end of a task, get into the habit of asking your journeyman for a thirty-second check-in. Not a formal thing, not a performance review, just "hey, anything on my end today that I should do differently tomorrow?" Most journeymen, if you ask that genuinely and regularly, will tell you something useful. And here is the ownership piece — when they tell you, you write it down. You don't explain why you did it the way you did. You don't remind them about the revised plans or the supplier repacking or the framer's door. You write it down. You say thank you. And you do it differently tomorrow. That loop — ask, receive, apply, repeat — is how an apprentice actually shortens the gap between where they are and where a journeyman is. The asking is the ownership. You are saying "my growth is my responsibility and I am actively managing it."

Alright. Let's head over to the other half of this week's yard.

If Horsepower Electric is pulling wire and chasing framers, Wells Cutting Edge is doing something that looks completely different from the outside but runs on the same engine — high-end residential, demanding clients, tight schedules, coordination with a dozen other trades and vendors, and the constant possibility that one missed detail turns into a very expensive phone call. Let's go over there and see what Extreme Ownership looks like when you're holding a shovel instead of a drill.

So here we are. It's early on a Wednesday, the kind of morning where the dew is still on everything and the site looks actually beautiful for about twenty minutes before it becomes a construction zone again. We're on a backyard renovation job — custom paver patio, drainage system, the whole thing. The homeowners have a very specific vision. Magazine-worthy, that's the phrase that got used in the sales meeting and was then repeated approximately eleven more times by the project manager so that everyone understood the stakes.

Our apprentice — let's call her Jordan — is part of the crew doing the base prep for the main patio area. Jordan has been with Wells Cutting Edge for about seven months, which is enough time to know the work and not quite enough time to have fully internalized all the moments where you should double-check something before proceeding. The journeyman on this job is a guy named Ray, who is excellent at what he does and has a very particular thing about grade. Grade — for anyone listening who doesn't do this work — is the slope of a surface. In landscaping, especially in drainage work, getting the grade right is not optional. Water goes where physics tells it to go, and if you have set your grade incorrectly, physics will route that water somewhere deeply inconvenient, like along the foundation of a house, which is the kind of problem that costs tens of thousands of dollars and ends careers of people who were responsible for preventing it.

Ray had told the crew at the start of the job, explicitly, clearly, with no ambiguity: "The patio needs to fall away from the house at a minimum of one inch per eight feet. We check grade on every lift, not just at the end." Every lift. He said it twice.

Jordan heard this. Jordan understood this. Jordan also had a lot of tasks going simultaneously that morning, because Wednesday had become the day where two other things got front-loaded — they were receiving a plant delivery and the drainage pipe for the French drain needed to get staked before the paver crew arrived at seven-thirty. So Jordan was moving fast. And when it came time to compact and verify the base grade in the far corner of the patio, Jordan ran the level, got a reading that looked okay, thought "that's probably fine," and moved on to the next thing.

I want to stay in Jordan's head for a second because I think this is important. That thought — "that's probably fine" — is a very specific thought. It is not the thought of someone who is being careless or lazy. Jordan was working hard. Jordan was moving fast because there was a lot to do. The thought "that's probably fine" is what happens when a competent, well-intentioned person doesn't slow down long enough to verify, because verifying feels like slowing down, and slowing down feels like falling behind, and falling behind on a magazine-worthy job with Ray watching feels like failing.

This is the setup for almost every ownership problem in the trades. It's not usually negligence. It's usually someone moving fast enough that checking feels like a cost they can't afford, right at the moment when checking would have saved them everything.

So the grade in the far corner was off. Not dramatically. Not in a way that was visible to the eye. But enough. About half an inch too flat, which on a twelve-foot run means the water, instead of moving away from the house, kind of considers its options and eventually decides to drift sideways toward the basement egress window, which was exactly the kind of outcome the drainage system had been designed to prevent.

This was not discovered on Wednesday. It was discovered on Friday, after the base was compacted, after the bedding sand was laid, and after approximately forty-eight pavers had been set, which is not nothing. That discovery happened because Ray was doing a final grade check before the crew started on the next section and he ran his level across the transition zone and made a face. Not a good face. The kind of face that causes everyone within fifteen feet to stop talking.

Ray says, quiet but very clear, "Who checked grade on this corner section?"

And here is the moment. Here is the Extreme Ownership moment. There are four people who could plausibly answer that question. Two of them definitely didn't check it. One of them might have been working in the vicinity. Jordan is the one who actually ran the level. And Jordan knows this.

Jordan could say nothing and let the moment slide. She could say "I ran a level but something must have shifted in the compact." She could mention the chaos of the plant delivery and the drainage pipe staking and the things that were competing for attention that morning. She could say "I thought it looked okay" in a tone that implied the instrument was maybe reading a little funny.

Instead Jordan says, "I did. I checked it and I moved on without confirming it was in spec. I think I was rushing. I'm sorry."

Ray looks at her for a second. And then he says, "Okay. Let's figure out how many pavers we're pulling."

And here is the thing. It was bad. Pulling and resetting pavers is not a fun afternoon. It's labor-intensive, it has material risk, and it takes time that nobody had budgeted. Jordan knew this. She knew it when she said "I think I was rushing" and she knew it all afternoon when they were pulling up forty-eight pavers and correcting the base. But here is also what happened. Ray walked her through the correction process in a way he might not have if she'd deflected, because when you deflect, the person you're deflecting to spends their energy figuring out what actually happened rather than fixing the problem. When you own it immediately, the senior person can move straight to "here is how we fix this and here is what you need to understand so it doesn't happen again." Jordan learned more about grade verification in that afternoon than she had in seven months, because Ray was teaching rather than investigating.

The other thing that happened — and this matters — is that the rest of the crew saw it. They saw Jordan own the mistake clearly and without drama. They saw Ray treat that honesty with respect. And that does something to a crew. It tells everyone watching that this is a place where it's safe to be honest, which means problems get surfaced faster, which means problems get fixed before they compound. The culture of a jobsite is made in exactly those small moments where someone decides whether to be honest or to deflect. Jordan made the culture better by choosing honesty, even though honesty was the harder thing.

Now let's talk about what this looks like in practice for a Wells Cutting Edge apprentice.

First: the spec-before-you-proceed habit. For Jordan, this means having the grade spec written down somewhere accessible before you start a task that requires a grade check, not just in your head, and then reading the number off the paper when you run the level, not just confirming that the bubble looks roughly centered. This is the landscaping version of counting your boxes. You anchor your verification to something external — a written spec, a string line, a measurement — rather than to your sense that it "looks okay." The spec is the spec. Your eyes are not the spec.

Practical version: at the start of any task that involves a precision outcome — grade, irrigation pressure, paver spacing, planting depth — you ask your lead or your journeyman "what's the number I'm checking for and how do I know if I've hit it?" If they give you a number, write it down. If they say "you'll know when it looks right," that is actually a moment to ask one more clarifying question, because "you'll know when it looks right" is an invitation for the kind of judgment call that creates expensive Fridays. Ask until you have something you can measure.

Second: the flag-and-hold. This is the landscaping equivalent of catch-and-report from the electrical section, and it is especially important in any work that involves sequencing — meaning, work where one thing needs to be right before the next thing can go on top of it. Paver installs are a perfect example. Base and bedding have to be right before pavers go down. Irrigation rough-in has to be done before sod goes in. Grading has to be correct before drainage pipe gets covered.

In a sequenced workflow, there is always pressure to keep moving, because the crew behind you is waiting. That pressure is real. But Extreme Ownership means that if you're not certain the previous step is right, you flag it before the next crew comes in, not after. "I want Ray to confirm grade on this section before we lay bedding sand" is a sentence that might feel like slowing things down. It is actually the sentence that prevents pulling up forty-eight pavers on a Friday. You have to get comfortable saying it even when it feels like you're the one hitting the brakes, because on a sequenced job, flagging early is the fastest thing you can do.

Third: the plant-and-material verification. Wells Cutting Edge does a lot of jobs where plant species, material colors, and paver patterns have been approved by a design process that can be elaborate and very specific. When a delivery arrives, someone needs to verify that what came off the truck matches what was ordered. On a busy Wednesday morning, this is easy to rush, easy to assume, easy to set aside because there's other stuff happening. But if you're the apprentice who signed off on receiving the delivery, you own what came in. That means you check the tag on every flat, you count the pallets, you confirm the species against the plant list, you look at the paver color in daylight before the sample goes back in the truck.

If something is wrong, you say something before the truck leaves. Before the materials get staged. Before anyone assumes the delivery is complete and correct. A wrong plant species discovered at install is a two-week problem. A wrong plant species caught at delivery is a thirty-minute phone call. The delivery is your moment of ownership and it happens once, so you do it right.

Fourth thing, and this is the one that I think matters most for Wells Cutting Edge apprentices specifically, because of the nature of the work: end-of-phase walk. On a landscape job, there are natural breaks in the workflow — end of the week, end of a phase, end of a section. Before you leave, before you move on to the next thing, you do a walk of what you just did. Not a casual "yep, looks good" walk. A deliberate, check-against-spec walk. You bring the plan or the spec sheet. You confirm the things that will be hardest to fix later — grade, spacing, material placement, drainage outlet locations. And if you find something that's off, you say so, right then, even if it means staying a little late, even if it means getting Ray on the phone while he's trying to get home.

The reason this is ownership rather than just quality control is the timing. Anyone can find a mistake eventually. The quality of your ownership is measured by how quickly you find it, because faster means cheaper, faster means easier to fix, faster means the client never knows it happened. An apprentice who does an end-of-phase walk is an apprentice who is taking responsibility for their output before someone else has to. That is the whole thing. You are saying "I am accountable for this, and I am not waiting for someone else to verify it for me."

So here we are. Two crews, two trades, two very different stories, one principle.

A Horsepower Electric apprentice didn't count his boxes and spent forty minutes driving back to the shop. A Wells Cutting Edge apprentice rushed a grade check and spent a Friday afternoon pulling up pavers. The details are completely different. The shape of the mistake is exactly the same. Someone moved faster than they verified, and when the problem surfaced, they had a choice about what to do with it.

Both of those apprentices, in the version of the story we told today, made the right choice. They told the truth, cleanly and quickly, without dressing it up or pointing fingers. And in both cases, the problem got solved, the senior person taught instead of punished, and the crew got stronger because of it.

That is what Extreme Ownership actually does when it works. It doesn't make you look weak. It doesn't invite people to take advantage of you. It doesn't mean you absorb blame that isn't yours. It means you are the person who is honest about their piece, fast, and then immediately focused on the solution. And over time, that person is the one who gets trusted with more. That person is the one who moves up. Not because they never make mistakes — everyone makes mistakes — but because when they do, the mistake stops there. It doesn't compound. It doesn't linger. It doesn't become a story that gets told in the truck on the way home about the thing that went sideways and nobody admitted to it.

Your name is on your work. Own it.
