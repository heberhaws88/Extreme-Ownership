The wire ran the wrong way? Marcus pointed to that path. The box got set at the wrong height? He was just matching what the blueprint showed, and nobody told him the blueprint was already revised. Every one of those things is technically defensible. That's the tricky part. Danny isn't lying. He really did put the wire where Marcus pointed. He really did match the old blueprint. They leave out the part where Danny saw the blueprint had a revision cloud on it and didn't ask. They leave out the part where he wasn't sure about the foreman's label but didn't say "wait, say that again." They leave out the part where his gut told him that wire path looked a little off but he figured Marcus knew better so he didn't mention it.

Now, the wire thing. Let's follow that one all the way through, because it gets worse before it gets better.

Danny runs a home run from the master bedroom back to the panel location. Marcus had gestured — genuinely gestured, with his hand, in a general direction — and said, "Take it through the closet, down the wall, and across." What he meant was: through the walk-in closet on the west wall, down that stud bay, and then across through the utility chase. What Danny did was: go through the smaller linen closet — which is technically also a closet, it has a door and everything — down that wall, and across through a load-bearing header space.

Is it Danny's fault that he didn't know which closet Marcus meant? Maybe not entirely. Is it Danny's fault that he didn't ask? Yes. Completely. But here's the thing — at the time, it didn't feel like a question worth asking. He made a reasonable inference, he did the work, and it looked fine. Wire's in, stapled clean, everything looks professional. Danny feels good about it.

Phil the super finds it the next morning because the framing crew is now trying to run a header that's blocked by a wire that runs directly through where a structural member needs to go. Not a tragedy. Not a dangerous situation. But it means the wire has to come out and get rerouted, and it means the rough-in schedule just got a little tighter on a week where there was no extra room.

Phil asks who did it. Marcus isn't there. Danny is.

"That's where Marcus said to put it."

Now let's freeze right there, because this is a moment that happens a hundred times a day on jobsites everywhere, and it passes so fast that most people don't examine it. Danny said a true thing. Marcus did say to go through the closet. But what Danny communicated — and what Phil heard — was this: I was following someone else's instructions, so this is not my problem. And the thing that Phil actually needed to hear — what would have changed everything about how that conversation landed — was something more like: "I pulled that home run yesterday and I picked the wrong path. I can have it rerouted by end of day."

Two different sentences. Both available to Danny. One of them closes a door. One of them opens one.

Danny goes with the one that closes the door, because it's easier in that exact second. It doesn't feel easier — it actually feels accurate and fair — but it is easier, because it doesn't require Danny to sit in the discomfort of being the one who made the mistake. And Phil writes something on his clipboard and walks away, and the day continues, and nothing dramatic happens.

But here's what does happen, slowly, over the next few weeks. Marcus stops telling Danny which jobs are coming up before they're assigned. Phil, when he has a question about rough-in, starts walking past Danny to find whoever else is on site. Not because they hate Danny. Not because of a policy. Just because — without anyone saying it out loud — the small signal Danny sent in that bedroom registered. He's the kind of guy who, when something goes sideways, points outward. And that means he's not quite the kind of guy you loop into things early, because early means uncertain, and uncertain means there's no one to point at yet if it goes wrong.

Danny doesn't know this is happening. He thinks things are fine. He's doing his work, he's getting better with tools, he's learning the trade. And he is. But there's a layer of the job — the layer where you get trusted with information before you need it, the layer where people ask your opinion, the layer where you're included in the plan instead of just handed the plan — that Danny is not accessing. And it's invisible to him, which is the cruelest part. He doesn't know what he doesn't know he's missing.

Okay. So that's the story. Let's talk about what's actually going on.

The principle Jocko and Leif write about in the first chapter of Extreme Ownership is the simplest and the hardest thing in the book. It's the idea that a leader — and they're very clear that leadership isn't a title, it's a behavior — owns everything in their world. Everything. Not just the things they personally did. Not just the things that were clearly their call to make. Everything. If the mission fails, the leader looks at themselves first. Not as a formality. Not as a self-flagellation ritual. But because it's the most useful place to look.

Here's why. When something goes wrong and your first move is to find where the fault lives outside you, you've taken yourself out of the equation. You've made yourself a spectator to your own situation. And spectators can't change outcomes. They can only watch them happen.

When something goes wrong and your first move is to ask "what did I do or not do that contributed to this" — even when the answer is "honestly, not much, this one really was mostly on someone else" — you stay inside the problem. You stay as someone who can act on it. You stay as someone whose actions, decisions, and communication are variables that can be adjusted. And that is enormously more powerful than being right about whose fault it was.

Here's the thing that trips people up, especially when they're new. Extreme Ownership sounds like it means taking the blame for everything forever, falling on every grenade, being the person who says "my fault" even when it genuinely isn't. And if that's what it meant, it would be a bad idea. That's not ownership, that's martyrdom, and it doesn't help anyone.

What it actually means is that you own your response to everything. You own the communication you did or didn't do. You own the question you did or didn't ask. You own the assumption you made when you could have gotten clarification. You own the moment where something felt off and you let it slide. Not because you're supposed to punish yourself for it, but because those are the levers you actually control. Those are the places where different choices lead to different outcomes. And if you're always pointing at what someone else did, you've let go of every lever you have.

For an apprentice, this is especially tricky, because there is a real power dynamic at play. You ARE following other people's instructions most of the time. Your journeyman IS the one setting the approach. The foreman IS the one calling the shots on how the job runs. And it can feel genuinely unfair to own problems that trace back to someone else's direction. But Extreme Ownership isn't about blame — it's about influence. The question isn't "whose fault was this." The question is "what could I have done differently that might have changed this outcome." And almost always, for an apprentice, the honest answer to that question is something in the communication column. A question not asked. A hesitation not voiced. A check not made. That's where the ownership lives.

Okay, so let's make this concrete. Let's talk about what Extreme Ownership actually looks like for a Horsepower Electric apprentice — not in theory, not as a philosophy, but as specific behaviors on a specific week.

First thing: before you start any task, confirm the full picture. Not the general direction, the full picture. If Marcus says "run the home run through the closet," and there are two closets, you say "which one — the walk-in or the linen?" before you pull any wire, not after. This sounds obvious when you're reading a script about an apprentice who didn't do it. It feels much less obvious in the moment, because in the moment, you don't want to seem like you weren't listening. You don't want to slow Marcus down. You don't want to be the person who needs everything spelled out. But here's the reframe: the electrician who asks one clarifying question before starting a task is not the person who looks slow. That's the person who doesn't have to redo the task. That's the person who builds trust fast. Make the question quick and specific — not "what do you mean?" which sounds like you zoned out — but "just to confirm: you want me going through the west walk-in, not the linen closet, right?" Specific, fast, confirms you were listening and that you're thinking ahead. That's what ownership sounds like at the front end of a task.

Second: when something goes wrong — even something small — lead with what you're going to do about it, before you lead with the explanation of how it happened. Not because the explanation doesn't matter. It does. But the order signals everything. "I set that box at the wrong height — I can swap it before the drywall crew gets here" lands completely differently than "the blueprint I had didn't show the updated ceiling height." Both things can be true. But which one you lead with tells everyone in earshot whether you're inside the problem or outside it. Practice this. Literally practice it. When you're driving to the job, think about what you're working on that day, and if there's anything uncertain, script out in your head how you'd say it if it went wrong. "I made a mistake with X. Here's what I'm doing about it." Out loud. In your truck. It sounds ridiculous but it trains the instinct.

Third: start paying attention to the moments where you have a small doubt and let it pass. These are the moments that matter most for an apprentice and that are almost totally invisible until you start looking for them. You're stapling wire and something about the path feels a little tight — not wrong, just a little tight — and you move on. You're labeling breakers and the foreman's call sounds like it might have been "kitchen counter north" or maybe "kitchen counter port" and you write down what you thought you heard. You're setting a junction box and you realize you're not a hundred percent sure where the rough-in spec puts the max height in this jurisdiction and you figure it's probably fine. Each one of those is a small fork in the road. Each one of them has a version where you stop for ninety seconds and verify, and a version where you don't. Extreme Ownership, at the apprentice level, is mostly just learning to stop at the forks. Not every fork — you'll never get anything done if you second-guess every staple. But the ones where you're aware of the doubt. If your gut noticed it, it was probably worth sixty seconds.

Fourth: get ahead of problems with your journeyman before they get to the GC. This is a big one. If you're working and you realize something is off — a wire's in the wrong place, a box is at the wrong height, you're not sure if a circuit got labeled correctly — tell Marcus before Phil finds it. Not because you're covering yourself, but because the earlier in the process a problem gets caught, the cheaper and faster it is to fix. An apprentice who surfaces problems early becomes the most valuable person on a crew, because they're the one adding information to the system instead of hoping someone else finds what they missed. "Hey Marcus, I want to flag something before we go further — I'm not sure that home run path is right, can you look at it?" That sentence, said early, on almost any job, is worth more than a week of clean stapling.

Now let's head over to the other side of the yard.

If Horsepower Electric is the kind of work where the stakes hide inside walls — mistakes that get covered up by drywall and only come back to bite you at inspection — then Wells Cutting Edge is the kind of work where the stakes are literally in the ground. You grade it wrong, water runs the wrong direction. You lay it wrong, it's concrete and it's permanent. Let's meet our second apprentice.

Her name for our purposes is Rosa. She's been with Wells Cutting Edge for about five months, which puts her right in that window where she knows enough to be genuinely useful but not quite enough to know how much she doesn't know, which is, honestly, the most dangerous window in any trade. Rosa's a hard worker. She asks decent questions. She's good with the physical side of the work — she can follow a grade string, she can set pavers clean, she's not afraid of the irrigation side even though a lot of apprentices are. But Rosa has the same habit Danny has, just wearing different clothes.

When something goes sideways on a Rosa job, Rosa's first response is to look for where the thing came from that caused the problem. And she's usually right. The problem usually does come from somewhere identifiable. That's not the issue. The issue is that she stops there. She finds the cause, she locates it, she names it — and she stays there. At the cause. Watching it. Waiting for it to become someone else's problem to fix.

Here's the job. Late summer install, a high-end residential backyard, the kind of project where the design package has a name. The clients — let's call them the Davenports — have been waiting since spring for their patio installation. Custom cut travertine pavers, an irregular pattern, coordinated with a pool contractor who's finishing coping on the same timeline. The HOA has a design review sign-off pending. There's a planting install scheduled to follow the hardscape by three weeks because the nursery has the plant material on hold. Everything is sequenced. Everything is timed. It is a very satisfying project plan until it isn't.

Rosa is part of the crew doing the base prep and initial paver layout. The lead on site is a guy named Trevor — experienced, fast, a little impatient with questions he thinks you should already know the answer to, which means Rosa sometimes doesn't ask questions she should ask because she doesn't want to look like she doesn't know things Trevor thinks she should know. This will become relevant.

The site has a slight natural grade running roughly northwest to southeast. The drainage design — which Rosa was walked through in the morning tailgate, fairly quickly, while Trevor was also answering a text — calls for a positive slope away from the house foundation, draining to a catch basin at the southeast corner of the patio. Rosa understood this. She did. The slope away from the house, southeast, catch basin in the corner.

What Rosa maybe understood slightly less clearly — or, more accurately, what Rosa registered as a detail and then didn't flag when she wasn't sure — is that the intermediate section of the patio, the section between the pool deck and the main sitting area, has a very slight counter-grade built into the design. Just barely. Like a valley that channels water specifically toward one of two area drains set into the paver field. It's subtle. It's on the plan. Rosa saw it on the plan. She thought she understood it. She was not certain she understood it. She did not ask.

The base work for the first section goes fine. Clean, correct, well-compacted. Trevor checks it, says nothing critical, moves on. Rosa takes that as confirmation. She carries the same logic into the intermediate section. The slope feels right to her. The string lines look right to her. She compacts the base, checks her level, and it reads consistent with what she did on the first section, and she moves on.

They set pavers for two days. It looks incredible. The travertine is beautiful, the irregular pattern is coming together cleanly, the GC does a site walk and is happy. Rosa is, understandably, proud of the work.

Then it rains. Not a lot — just a normal late-summer afternoon rain, maybe forty minutes. And when the rain stops, there is a shallow puddle sitting on the intermediate section of the patio. Not a lake. Not a disaster. Just water that is not where the design said water was supposed to go. It's sitting in exactly the place where the counter-grade should have directed it toward the area drains, because the counter-grade isn't quite there. Because Rosa's base in that section, which felt right, which her eye told her was right, is just slightly off from the design intent.

Trevor finds it. Trevor shows it to Rosa. And Rosa says — again, with total accuracy — "I ran it the same way I ran the first section, and the first section passed. And the grade change in the middle wasn't really explained clearly in the tailgate."

Trevor's face does the thing faces do when they hear an accurate sentence that isn't actually an answer. He nods. He walks away. He calls the foreman.

Now the patio has to be pulled in the middle section. Not all of it. Just the middle section, which is not a small amount of work, because the middle section is set with the same material that's on either side of it, and getting it up without chipping travertine is its own project. The pool contractor's timeline is now in question. The planting install is going to slip. The HOA review that was two weeks away is now in a holding pattern because the patio isn't done.

None of this is purely Rosa's fault. The counter-grade detail was genuinely not clearly explained. Trevor was distracted during the tailgate. The design document could have flagged the transition more explicitly. All of that is true. And it changes nothing about the puddle on the patio, or the timeline, or the cost of pulling and resetting the middle section. And Rosa — standing there looking at the puddle, with Trevor already on the phone — is exactly as useful to fixing any of it as Danny was standing in that bedroom with Phil.

She's a spectator to her own situation. She found the cause. She named it. She stopped there. And the water is still sitting on the patio.

Let's talk about what Extreme Ownership looks like for a Wells Cutting Edge apprentice, specifically, in practical terms you can use this week.

First thing: when you're in a tailgate or a site walk and something in the plan isn't fully clear to you, get it clear before you touch dirt. The travertine isn't going back in the ground any easier the second time. Rosa's version of the Danny problem — making a reasonable inference when she could have gotten clarification — is incredibly common in landscaping because the physical nature of the work creates a false sense of momentum. Dirt is moving, base is going down, pavers are getting set, and it feels like progress. It looks like progress. But if the grade is wrong, all of that motion is building toward a problem, not away from one. Before you start a section you have any uncertainty about, stop Trevor, stop the foreman, stop whoever, and say: "I want to make sure I've got this grade transition right before I compact the base — can you walk me through exactly what the design wants in the middle section?" That question, asked before the base is compacted, is worth about two days of reset work.

Second: use your level and your string lines against the plan, not against your previous section. This is a specific technical point, but it's also an ownership point. Rosa checked her level and found it consistent with the first section. The first section was correct. But the intermediate section wasn't supposed to match the first section — it was supposed to do something different. If you're using your previous work as your reference point, you're closing the loop on yourself. The plan is your reference point. On any section where the design says the grade changes behavior — a counter-grade, a transition to a new drainage direction, a shift in slope percentage — mark it on the plan, and physically verify it against the plan numbers, not against your eye or your previous work.

Third: if you're mid-task and you realize you're not sure, stop before you compound the problem. Rosa could have stopped at any point during those two days of paver setting. The uncertainty she had about the counter-grade didn't disappear after the tailgate — it was still there, in the background, that low-frequency "I think this is right" that's different from "I know this is right." Extreme Ownership, for an apprentice, means learning to respect that frequency. When you're working and you notice you're in "I think" territory instead of "I know" territory, that's the moment to surface it. Not when Trevor is checking the completed section. Not when the rain reveals it. In the middle. "Hey Trevor, I'm setting this transition section and I want to make sure I've got the counter-grade right — can you check my string line against the plan?" Said in the middle of the work, that's not an interruption. That's quality control. That's the job.

Fourth: when something does go wrong — and it will, because it does for everyone, including the people who've been doing this for twenty years — own the rework conversation, not just the mistake conversation. What this means in practice: don't wait for Trevor or the foreman to figure out the fix and hand it to you. Come to that conversation with something. "I've been looking at the middle section, and I think if we pull from these joints here, we can get the middle portion up without disturbing the edge pavers on either side. I want to check with you before I start." You might be wrong about the specific approach. That's fine. But you showed up to the problem as someone who's inside it, thinking about how to resolve it, instead of someone who's waiting to be told what to do next. That's what the difference looks like, from the outside, between someone who's going to grow in this trade and someone who's going to be doing the same thing in five years that they're doing today.

And here's one more for Rosa specifically, because it connects to the Trevor dynamic in a way that matters. When you're working with someone who doesn't love questions — who signals, through their impatience or their tone or just their general energy, that questions slow them down — the temptation is to stop asking them. And that's exactly backwards. The answer is not to ask fewer questions. The answer is to ask better questions. Faster, more specific, asked at the right moment. "Quick check before I compact this section — does the plan show a two-percent counter-grade here or one-point-five?" That's a question that takes fifteen seconds to ask and fifteen seconds to answer. It signals competence, not confusion. It signals that you're reading the plan, you're paying attention, and you just want to confirm one number. Trevor can handle that, even on a bad day. And if he can't, that's information you also need — and the foreman is a resource, the plan is a resource, and your own ability to slow down and read the document is a resource. Rosa had the plan. The answer was in the plan. She just needed to trust herself enough to verify it against the plan instead of assuming she had it right.

So here's where we land, for both crews.

Danny stood in a master bedroom and said, "That's where Marcus said to put it." Rosa stood next to a puddle on a travertine patio and said, "The grade change wasn't really explained clearly." Both of them said true things. Both of them were spectators. Both of them had, somewhere in the hours before those moments, a fork in the road where they noticed something uncertain and let it pass.

The principle is the same whether you're pulling wire through a framed wall or setting a paver on a compacted base. You cannot control every variable on a jobsite. You cannot control which direction your journeyman points or how clearly the foreman explains the grade transition. What you can control is your communication, your questions, your willingness to stop at the fork and verify before you move forward. That's where ownership lives, at the apprentice level. Not in falling on every sword. Not in saying "my fault" so reflexively that it becomes meaningless. But in staying inside the problem — before it's a problem, during it, and after — as someone whose choices are still in play.

Every single door that closes in this trade closes quietly, the way Phil's closed for Danny. No announcement, no reprimand, just a small shift in who gets told what and when. And the doors open the same way. Quietly. Because someone asked the right question at the right time, or surfaced a problem early enough to fix it clean, or showed up to a rework conversation with a plan instead of a defense.

There are two trades in this podcast, one principle, and one line that works for both of them.

If you own it before it breaks, you don't have to own the pieces.
