Here is the core of what this chapter is about. It's not about optimism. It's not about team spirit. It's not about believing in yourself in the way that a motivational calendar would have you believe. The idea is simpler and harder than that.

Believe is about whether you, the leader, actually understand why the mission matters — the full reasoning behind it, not just the what — and whether you've made sure your people understand it too. It's about whether your crew has enough context to make good decisions when you're not standing next to them. Because if they don't understand why something matters, they will fill in the blanks themselves. And they will fill them in wrong. Not because they're bad at their jobs. Because nobody told them.

Jocko and Leif put it this way in the book: a leader who doesn't believe in the mission can't get their people to execute it, and a leader who believes but doesn't transmit that belief has just kept a secret. The crew can tell. They can tell when you're just passing down orders you haven't thought through. They can tell when you're assigning work without explaining stakes. And when they can tell, they do what Phil did — they execute the work they understood, not the work that actually mattered most that week.

Here's why this is easy to get wrong. When you're a Lead who's been doing this for a while, you have context that your crew doesn't have. You talked to the GC. You read the revised plan set. You did the math in your head. And all of that context lives in your head, and it feels obvious to you because you've been walking around with it. But the person you're sending into that bonus room to rough in a theater that wasn't on last week's list — they don't have your mental map. They have the information you handed them, plus whatever they inferred. And inference is not a construction technique.

The other way it goes wrong is this. Sometimes, as a Lead, you get handed a direction from above that you don't fully agree with or don't fully understand. Maybe it's a schedule you think is too tight. Maybe it's a budget you think is too lean. Maybe it's a decision from management you wouldn't have made yourself. And you have a choice in that moment. You can either push back up the chain and say "help me understand why we're doing it this way," get the reasoning, internalize it, and then transmit it down to your crew with genuine conviction — or you can shrug, pass it down without the reasoning, and let your crew wonder why they're being asked to do something that seems to make no sense. The first version is hard. The second version feels easier in the moment and is catastrophically expensive later.

Because here's the thing about a crew that doesn't have the full picture: they're not going to tell you they don't have the full picture. Dustin doesn't walk up to you and say "I believe I may be missing strategic context." Dustin says "good" when you ask how it's going, because Dustin is trying to seem like he's on top of it. The person who's supposed to have the full picture is you. And getting it to them is your job.

Done right, this looks like: a Lead who can explain not just what they need from each person this week, but why it matters in the sequence, and what happens downstream if that piece is off. Done right, it means your crew can make a reasonable call in a situation you didn't anticipate because they understand the goal well enough to navigate toward it. Done right, it means Phil knows when his list changes, he knows immediately, because he understands that his section affects the inspection timeline which affects the GC's schedule which affects the whole project — not because you recite that to him every morning, but because you told him once, clearly, and he has it.

Alright. Let's talk about what this looks like in practice for a Horsepower Electric Lead. What can you do this week — not someday, this week — that actually builds the Believe principle into how you run your jobs?

First thing. Scope changes get a crew conversation, not just a task reassignment. When the plan set changes — and it will change, it always changes — the instinct is to quickly redistribute tasks and get back to work. Resist that. Take ten minutes. Bring your crew together, or get to each person individually if the site doesn't allow a group, and say: "Here's what changed. Here's why the homeowner or GC made this call. Here's what that means for our sequence this week. Here's what we cannot let slip because of it." Ten minutes. That's the investment. And what you're buying with that ten minutes is a crew that understands their work in context, which means when you're on the other site dealing with the conduit conflict, Phil is making the right call without you because he knows what this week is actually about.

Second thing. Build a check-in that surfaces real information, not just status updates. "How's it going?" is not a check-in. "How's it going?" is a social nicety. A real check-in sounds like this: "Walk me through what you got done since this morning and what's between you and finishing your section by end of day." That's a question that requires specifics. That's a question that will catch the backwards breakers on Monday instead of Tuesday. You don't need to do this every hour — you need to do it once, seriously, at a natural midpoint in the day. And if the answer you get is vague, you ask a follow-up. "Walk me through what you mean by that." You're not interrogating, you're teaching someone how to report accurately, which is a skill they will use for the rest of their career and they need someone to coach it.

Third thing. If you get a direction from above that you can't explain to your crew — go find out why before you pass it down. If Marcus hands you a Thursday inspection date that feels impossible, before you go tell your crew "Thursday, that's the date, make it work," go back to Marcus and say "help me understand what's driving Thursday." Maybe it's a draw schedule. Maybe it's the framing crew that's coming Friday. Maybe it's something you can push back on with a good case. And maybe you can't push it back, and Thursday it is — but now you can go to your crew and say "Thursday is the date and here is specifically why, here is what happens to the project if we miss it." That's the difference between a direction landing as an arbitrary order and landing as a reason to actually care.

Fourth thing. At the end of a job, or at the end of a phase, have the debrief conversation that includes "what did you not know that you needed to know?" Not "what went wrong" — that's backward looking in a blaming way. "What information were you missing that would have helped you make better calls?" That question builds a culture where your crew tells you when they're working blind, because they know you're going to treat that as useful information rather than a failure. Dustin's backwards breakers are a lot less likely to be a Tuesday morning surprise if Dustin has learned that saying "I'm not sure I fully understand how to terminate this correctly" is welcomed. That's the kind of crew you're building toward. And it starts with asking the question.

First thing. When the project kicks off — before any dirt moves — walk the full plan with your crew as a story, not a task list. Don't just hand out phase assignments. Second thing. When the plan changes — and the plan will change, materials will delay, weather will shift, a designer will modify a detail two weeks in — do not absorb the change alone and redistribute tasks on the fly. Stop, even for fifteen minutes, and redo the kickoff conversation for the changed portion. That conversation is what turns a stressful schedule change into a coordinated pivot instead of three people working in three different directions that happen to conflict.

Third thing. Build a culture where your crew surfaces problems early and specifically. The laborer who installed the creek bed — he saw something that didn't match his gut about how the grade was reading. He probably didn't say anything because he wasn't sure, and because nobody had told him that saying "I think there might be a grade issue, can you check this?" is valuable and wanted. That's on you as the Lead to establish. You do it by responding well the first few times someone brings you an uncertainty. Not "yeah I'll look at it later" in a way that feels dismissive, and not "how did you miss this" in a way that makes the next person stay quiet. You do it by saying "good catch, let's check it now." You do it by treating a question as a faster path to the right result than a mistake that shows up at eleven in the morning three days later.

Fourth thing. When you are handed a direction from above that your crew is going to have to execute — a schedule that got compressed, a budget that got trimmed, a change that came from the designer or the homeowner or the project manager — before you just pass it down as a mandate, ask yourself: do I understand why? And if the answer is no, go find out. Because your crew is going to work harder and smarter for a reason than they will for an order. That is not a soft observation, that is a practical reality of how human beings function. Brandon will coordinate better with Ramon if he understands why the sequence matters, not just that the project manager said to hold the schedule. And you can only give him that understanding if you have it yourself.

One more thing on this, because it comes up a lot on landscape jobs specifically. The designer makes a call you don't fully agree with. The drainage run is longer than you would have done it. You say something like "that's what the plan says" in a tone that communicates you think the plan is wrong. And your crew hears that, and they internalize it, and now they're executing a plan that their Lead has signaled is questionable, and their effort level is going to reflect that.

If you think the designer is wrong, go to the designer. Have the conversation. Make your case. Maybe they'll adjust the plan. Maybe they'll explain why the choice was made and you'll understand it differently. And then, whichever way it goes, you go to your crew with genuine conviction. "Here's the plan, here's why we're doing it this way." Not performed conviction — real conviction, because you either changed the plan or you understand it now. That's Believe. That's the standard.
