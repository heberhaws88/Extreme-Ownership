So it's a Tuesday morning, middle of rough-in on a four-thousand square foot custom build in the hills, and I am standing in what's going to be the primary bedroom closet holding a spool of twelve-two that I absolutely, one hundred percent did not order enough of. I know this because Marcus — he's the apprentice on this job, good kid, asks too many questions, exactly the kind of guy you want on a crew until you're embarrassed and he's standing right there watching — Marcus holds up his phone showing me the material list and goes, "Journeyman, this says we needed six spools for this floor alone." And I'm holding two. Two spools. And we're behind the framers by a day and a half already, and the GC has already texted me twice this morning, which means he's texting the boss by lunch.

And my very first instinct — I want you to know this about me, I want you to hold it — my very first instinct was to figure out whose fault this was. Not how to fix it. Whose fault it was. Because if I could identify the person responsible for the material order being wrong, I could at least go into that conversation with the GC with something. A name. A reason. A human shield made of someone else's mistake.

That is what we're talking about today.

Let me set the scene properly because the material shortage was actually the third thing that went wrong that morning and I had already used up most of my goodwill with my own internal monologue by the time Marcus showed me that phone.

The job is a high-end custom home, the kind where the GC sends you a fourteen-page finish schedule before you even pull the permit. Smart home integration, whole-house audio, custom lighting zones in every room, the trim carpenter alone probably costs more per day than our entire crew. The homeowner is one of those people who has a vision board. I know this because she mentioned it on the walkthrough. She showed it to us. On an iPad. There were clippings from Architectural Digest. One of the pages was just labeled "vibes." I'm not making this up.

So the stakes are high. The schedule is tight. We are running a four-man crew — me, Marcus, a second-year apprentice named Denny who moves like he's always slightly confused about what time zone he's in, and our senior guy Ray, who's been doing this for eighteen years and has the posture of a man who has seen every possible version of a job go sideways and survived all of them.

First thing that went wrong that morning: I had sketched out the rough-in layout for the great room the night before on a yellow legal pad, and I left it on my kitchen counter. At home. So we started the morning working off my memory of what I'd sketched, which is confidence I had not fully earned. Ray looked at me when I described the layout from memory and he did this thing where he nods slowly and says nothing. That nod is the most expensive nod in the trade. It means: I will help you, and I will not say I told you so, and we both know you're going to wish you had that paper.

Second thing: the panel location had been flagged by the inspector on a preliminary walkthrough because the clearance on the right side was tight — like, within an inch of the framing — and someone had noted it in the inspection file and that someone was me and I had not communicated it to the crew, so when Denny started blocking in around the panel he did it in the worst possible direction and we lost forty minutes undoing that before the inspector showed up for anything official.

Third thing: two spools of twelve-two.

Now here's what I actually did, because I want to be honest about this. I asked Marcus where the material order came from. He said it came from the list I'd handed to the supplier. I said are you sure? He said yes. I said let me see it. He showed me. It was my handwriting. It was my list. And I had written five spools for the lower floor and had simply not written the upper floor quantities at all. It was a clean, confident mistake. No ambiguity. My handwriting, my omission, my problem.

What I did with that information — and this is the part I'm not proud of — is I spent about three minutes trying to find a technicality. Was there a standard I could point to where the supplier should have flagged an incomplete order? Was there a note I could find where someone on my crew had confirmed the quantities before I sent it? Had the architect changed the room count and maybe that was why the numbers felt off? I was in that closet with two spools of wire and a legal pad full of blame I was trying to distribute and Marcus was just standing there holding his phone like evidence at a trial.

And then Ray walks in. Ray takes one look at the situation. He doesn't ask what happened. He says, "What do you need and how fast can we get it?" And I said, "Four more spools, probably by noon if I call right now." And he said, "Then call right now." And he walked back out.

That's it. That's the whole thing from Ray. No autopsy. No "well how did this happen." Just: what do you need and how fast can we get it. And the reason that hit me like a bucket of cold water is because Ray has forty more years of experience than me and he has never once, in the time I've known him, spent a single second in the blame phase. Not because he doesn't know who made the mistake — he always knows — but because he's figured out that the blame phase costs you time you don't have and doesn't produce a single linear foot of wire.

I called the supplier. We got the wire by eleven-thirty. We were back on schedule by mid-afternoon. The GC never found out. The vision board homeowner remained unaware that her "vibes" had been briefly in jeopardy.

But I kept thinking about those three minutes in the closet. Three minutes looking for a technicality. Three minutes that accomplished nothing except making me feel slightly less responsible for a situation I was entirely responsible for.

And that is exactly what this chapter is about.

The principle is called Extreme Ownership. Not regular ownership. Not "I accept some responsibility for this." Not "I take ownership of my part in the situation." Extreme. All of it. Every piece. Even the parts that feel genuinely unfair.

Here's the core idea as Willink and Babin lay it out: the leader — and by leader they mean anyone responsible for getting a job done with other people, which is you, which is every journeyman on every crew — owns everything in their world. Every outcome, good and bad. The successes belong to the team. The failures belong to the leader. Not as a punishment. Not as a ritual self-flagellation. As a practical operating philosophy. Because the moment you start distributing blame, you've handed control of the outcome to whoever you're blaming. You can't fix what belongs to someone else. You can only fix what belongs to you.

This is the part that people get wrong. They hear "Extreme Ownership" and they think it means you have to walk around apologizing for everything and taking hits you don't deserve. That's not it. It's almost the opposite. When you own something completely — when you stop spending energy assigning fault and spend it on solving the problem — you actually become more effective, not less. Because now all the levers are in your hands. You're not waiting for someone else to admit a mistake so you can proceed. You're not building your defense while the clock runs out. You're just solving the problem.

The other thing they get right in this chapter is the ego piece. The reason we don't do Extreme Ownership naturally — the reason my first instinct in that closet was to find a technicality — is ego. Not arrogance necessarily. Just the basic human need to not be the person who screwed up. To have a story to tell where you were reasonable and someone else dropped the ball. And that need is understandable and completely useless on a jobsite. The GC does not care about your story. The inspector doesn't care. The homeowner with the vision board definitely doesn't care. They care about the outcome. And the fastest path to a good outcome is to own whatever stands between you and it, even if you didn't create it.

There's one more layer to this that matters for journeymen specifically. You're not the foreman. You're not the project manager. You're not the boss. You might be thinking, "This principle makes sense for leadership — but I'm still pretty early. What do I own exactly?" And the answer is: everything in your sphere. Your work. Your apprentice's work. Your communication up the chain. Your prep the night before. The material list you submitted. The notes you did or didn't pass along. You don't own what the GC decides. You don't own what the homeowner changes at the last minute. But you own your response to all of it. And for a journeyman, that sphere is bigger than you probably think.

Now let's talk about what this actually looks like on a Horsepower Electric job this week. Not concepts. Real behaviors.

The first one is the clearest: own your prep, all the way. The material shortage I described was a prep failure. Not a forgetfulness failure — I'm not stupid — a prep failure. I had a system, my system had a gap, and when I submitted that order I was working off an incomplete list and didn't catch it. The fix isn't to try harder. The fix is a better system. For a journeyman, that means before any material order goes to the supplier, you have one checkpoint where someone else confirms the quantities against the plan. Not because you can't be trusted. Because humans miss things, and the only person hurt by your missing it is you. So you build in the catch. You own the system, not just the task.

The second behavior is communication ownership. I didn't tell the crew about the panel clearance issue. That was flagged in my notes, in my head, and it stayed there. The crew is not responsible for knowing what's in your head. You are responsible for getting it out of your head and into theirs. For a journeyman, this looks like a five-minute tailgate at the start of every shift where you say out loud the things that could bite someone. Not because the guys don't know their jobs — they do — but because the flags you've seen that they haven't are yours to share. If you don't share them, the resulting mistake is still yours even though you weren't the one holding the tool.

Third behavior: when something goes wrong mid-job, train yourself to ask the Ray question first. "What do we need and how fast can we get it?" Not "how did this happen" — you can figure that out later, at end of day, so it doesn't happen again. But mid-problem, your job is to shorten the gap between the mistake and the recovery. The autopsy is for after. The triage is for now. This sounds obvious but it is not instinctive. The instinct is to understand before you act. On a live job with a GC circling, acting comes first.

Fourth: own the conversation upward. This one's uncomfortable. If something went sideways on your watch — your material order was short, your rough-in layout had an error, you missed a coordination detail — tell your foreman before he hears it from the GC. Every time. I know the math in your head: if I tell him, he's going to be annoyed at me. If the GC tells him, he's going to be annoyed at the GC and maybe forget to be annoyed at me. That math is wrong. When your foreman hears it from you first, two things happen: he respects you, and he can help you. When he hears it from the GC, he's behind the information curve and managing up in the dark. You have made his job worse and yours worse at the same time. Own the conversation. Go first. Say "here's what happened, here's what I'm doing about it." That's it. That's the whole script. You don't need more than that.

Now let's head over to the Wells Cutting Edge side of the yard. Same week, same principle, completely different world — because over there, the chaos comes with a mulch delivery and a drainage plan that's about to make someone's Tuesday a lot wetter than they planned.

Okay. You're on a high-end residential landscape install. The scope is big — custom paver patio, full irrigation system, ornamental beds along the perimeter, a screening hedge along the HOA-mandated property line, and a drainage solution on the lower side of the lot where there's a grade change and the pool contractor's backfill has left a situation. By "a situation" I mean water is going to pool somewhere on this property, the question is where, and the answer should definitely not be "against the foundation" but right now it could go either way.

You are the journeyman on this crew. The foreman pulled off site yesterday afternoon for a family thing and left you holding it. You have four guys: Tommy, who's been on irrigation installs for three years and is genuinely good; Carlos, who runs the paver work and is an artist with a wet saw but communicates exclusively in thumbs up or thumbs down; Elena, who knows plants better than anyone on the crew but has been fighting a cold all week and is clearly running at about seventy percent; and a new guy everyone calls Sketch because the first thing he drew when asked to sketch a layout was something that looked like a modern art installation. Sketch means well.

The morning starts fine. The paver base is set and the crew is laying field stone in the main patio section. The irrigation trenches are open on the west side. The drainage situation at the low end is planned — there's a French drain mapped on the design sheet and the perforated pipe and gravel are staged. The plant delivery is scheduled for Thursday. Everything is sequenced and it is going to be fine.

And then at nine-fifteen, Tommy hits an irrigation line.

Not the main line — one of the lateral branches, smaller, but it's pressurized and it is absolutely fountaining. Muddy water going everywhere, into the open trench, getting into the base material on the edge of the paver section, and Tommy is standing there with a shovel in one hand looking at you with an expression that says: I found this. I did not make this.

And here's the thing — he didn't make it. The line was not on the as-built. It was an old lateral from a previous system that the original design didn't account for because nobody had current as-builts for this property. This is genuinely not Tommy's fault. It is also genuinely your problem.

Your first move is to get the water off. You find the zone shutoff, you kill it, and now you have a muddy trench, a compromised section of paver base, and a lateral line that doesn't appear on any document you have. And you have a client who is coming by at noon for a progress check, and a foreman who is off-site, and an HOA that has already sent one letter about the screening hedge timeline, and Elena is sniffling over in the ornamental bed area looking like she might need to go home.

Now here's where the journeyman version of the blame reflex shows up. Because nobody here is obviously at fault, so the temptation is to just distribute the situation. The irrigation line wasn't on the as-built — that's the prior contractor's issue. The design didn't account for it — that's on the design team. Tommy found it because he was digging the new trench — well, that's just what happens. Nobody did anything wrong. Isn't that kind of comforting? Nobody did anything wrong and everything is a mess. What a relief. Except you still have a fountaining hole, a ruined section of base material, a noon client visit, and a phone that you need to pick up and call your foreman on.

And this is the thing: "Nobody did anything wrong" is not an actionable statement. It doesn't fix the base material. It doesn't get you the repair coupling for the lateral. It doesn't tell Tommy what to do next or give Carlos a clear picture of how much of the paver work might need to pause. And it definitely doesn't help you figure out what to say at noon.

What happens next, in the version of this story where the journeyman has actually internalized this principle, is you stop thinking about whose fault it is entirely. You call the foreman — immediately, before you've solved it, before you know all the answers — and you say: "Here's what happened. Undocumented lateral line, pressurized, water in the trench and the base edge. I've got the zone shut down. I need to know if we have a repair coupling on the trailer, and I need to know how you want me to handle the noon walkthrough." You give him the situation, your current status, and the decisions you need from above versus the ones you're handling. That's an Extreme Ownership phone call. No prelude. No setup designed to make it sound like you managed the chaos before it was chaos. Just: here's the situation, here's what I've done, here's what I need.

Then you go back to the crew. You tell Tommy to document the line — photo it, measure its position relative to the property line, write it down — because now you own making sure the as-builts get updated so this doesn't happen again on this property or any other. You tell Carlos which paver sections are hold and which can proceed, so he's not standing around. You check the base damage and make a call on whether you need to re-compact or just top-dress. You send Sketch — who, despite the name, is enthusiastic — to get the repair coupling from the supply trailer. You check on Elena.

By noon when the client arrives, you have a repaired lateral, a drying trench, a crew back in motion, and a clear and honest explanation ready: "We found an undocumented irrigation line this morning. We got it shut down, repaired, and documented. Here's what it means for your timeline — it's a half-day adjustment on the irrigation side, nothing that changes your install date." Client nods. Client is not happy, but client is informed and confident that you're on it. That is a recoverable situation. The version where you hem and haw at noon and the client finds out two days later that you knew about it at nine-fifteen — that's the version that costs you the referral.

Let's talk about what Extreme Ownership looks like in practice for a Wells Cutting Edge journeyman this week. Because the irrigation line story hits a specific kind of chaos, but the principle shows up constantly in landscaping and it shows up in ways that are easy to rationalize away.

First behavior: own the pre-dig confirmation. Before any trench goes in the ground — irrigation, drainage, electrical conduit for the outdoor lighting — you have confirmed the as-builts, you have called 811, and you have walked the line with whoever is swinging the tool. Not because your crew can't dig a straight trench. Because undocumented lines, unmarked utilities, and "that's close enough" grade calls are where landscaping jobs go sideways, and the cost of five extra minutes before the dig is never as high as the cost of what happens when you skip it. You own that confirmation process. Every time. Even when you're behind.

Second behavior: own the grade and drainage check before any base goes down. This is the one that keeps me up at night on landscape jobs, because grading mistakes are slow to reveal themselves. You put base down, you lay pavers, it looks incredible, and then the first heavy rain comes and water runs straight at the foundation because the grade was off by half a percent in the wrong direction. The homeowner doesn't call you until three months after the job is done, and by then everyone's memory of who made the decision is fuzzy. Journeyman's job: before the base goes down, you have confirmed the drainage flow with a level and you have documented it. Photo, note, confirm. If there's a question, you stop and you ask. Stopping costs an hour. A drainage callback costs a reputation.

Third behavior — and this one's interpersonal — own your crew's communication gaps. When Elena is at seventy percent and the planting layout is in her head and not on paper, that's a risk that belongs to you. Not because she did anything wrong — being sick is not a mistake — but because if she goes home and nobody knows what goes where in the ornamental beds, you've lost a full day of planting progress and possibly planted the wrong thing in the wrong spot, which on a job with HOA design review is a real problem. So you spend twenty minutes with Elena before lunch and you get the layout sketched, photographed, documented — whatever level of detail you need so the information doesn't leave the site when she does. That's ownership of a gap you could see coming.

Fourth behavior: own the client communication before the problem gets to them through someone else. Landscaping clients talk to their GC. They talk to their pool contractor. They talk to their neighbor who recommended you. If something happened on your site today that might come up in conversation, you are the first one to bring it up. Not in a confessional, dramatic way — just matter-of-fact: "We ran into an undocumented line this morning, here's the status, here's the plan." That's the whole conversation. You're not falling on a sword. You're demonstrating that you know your job site and you're on top of it. That is the thing clients actually pay a premium for. Not a perfect job with no hiccups — nobody believes that's possible. A crew that they trust to tell them the truth and handle it. You earn that by going first.

So here's where both crews land.

The Horsepower Electric journeyman forgot to order enough wire and spent three minutes looking for a technicality. The Wells Cutting Edge journeyman found a pressurized line that wasn't on any drawing and had to decide whether to manage it or explain it away. Different trade. Different kind of mess. Same instinct on the way in, same principle on the way out.

Both situations wanted to offer you someone to blame. A supplier. A prior contractor. An incomplete document. And the principle doesn't say don't notice that. It says: noticing it doesn't help you. Owning it does. Not because you caused it — maybe you didn't — but because you're the one standing there with the tools and the crew and the clock running, and that means you're the one who gets to decide what happens next. Ownership isn't a punishment. It's a superpower. The moment you stop asking "who did this" and start asking "what do we need and how fast can we get it," you become the most valuable person on that job site.

That works whether you're chasing framers through a rough-in or watching a lateral line fountain into your paver base.

Own it all. Fix it fast.
