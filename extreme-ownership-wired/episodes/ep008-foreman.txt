Here's what nobody tells you when they hand you the clipboard and say "you're the foreman now" — they don't tell you that the job doesn't get harder because the work gets harder. It gets harder because now everything that goes sideways is somehow, in some way, on you. Every bad cut, every missed rough-in, every guy who shows up forty minutes late with coffee still in his hand and sawdust from yesterday on his boots — that's on you. And the first time you really feel that, I mean really feel it in your chest, it's a little bit of a gut punch. Because up until that point you had been very quietly, very comfortably blaming other people for things. And suddenly that door is closed.

So let me tell you about a Tuesday in October on a job that I still think about when I'm trying to fall asleep.

We were about three weeks into a rough-in on a custom build — big house, not enormous, but the kind of house where the GC has a laminated schedule on the wall of his trailer and he actually refers to it. The framing crew had just finished the second floor and we had a narrow window, maybe four days, to get our rough-in done before insulation came in. Four days, second floor, two-story open foyer, custom lighting package, smart home panel going in on the third day. It was tight but it was doable. I had run tighter schedules than that.

My crew that week was me, my lead guy Marco — not his real name, I'll call him Marco — and two journeymen I'll call Big Dave and Cody. And then a helper named, let's say, Terrence, who was new enough that he still asked permission before using the porta-john. Which honestly, ask permission or don't, just don't disappear for twenty-five minutes either way, Terrence.

Day one, we're moving. I'm feeling good. Marco's pulling wire on the second floor like a machine, Big Dave's drilling like we're getting paid by the hole, Cody's doing the box layout. Terrence is hauling material and actually keeping up. I'm thinking, you know what, four days is fine. We might even finish in three.

Then I walk past Big Dave's holes and something catches my eye.

Now Big Dave is a solid electrician. I want to be clear about that. Seven years in the trade, good instincts, he can read a plan. But Big Dave has a thing, and the thing is that when he gets into a rhythm on the drill, he stops checking his measurements. He just trusts where the last hole was and goes forward. It's like he's in a trance. And on a regular job, in a regular house, this is fine. It's fine. But on this job, the framing crew had done this thing on the second floor where the stud spacing was not perfectly consistent coming around the corner of the master bedroom hallway. There was an extra stud that wasn't on the plan, and Big Dave had not seen it, and so his hole series was now going to put three switch legs in places that would be completely inaccessible behind a built-in cabinet run that was coming in later that week.

I saw this and I said nothing. Not immediately. And that pause is the whole story, honestly. That pause is everything.

I walked over to Marco and I said, quietly, "Hey, check Dave's holes on the hallway, I think he might be off." And Marco walked over and looked and came back to me and said, "Yeah, those are off, like four inches consistently." And I said, and I want you to hear this in the absolute non-leadership voice I said it in, I said, "Okay well he's already drilled twelve of them, we'll figure it out at the end of the day."

Figure it out at the end of the day. I said that. I let it run for another two hours because I did not want to stop Big Dave's momentum, I did not want to have a conversation that might slow things down, and if I'm being fully honest, I didn't want to be the guy who pointed out a problem that I was now going to have to fix. So I told myself it was a big-picture management decision to keep the drill moving, and what it actually was, was me not doing my job.

End of day one, we counted it up. Big Dave had drilled and pulled wire through twenty-two holes that were going to have to be re-done. Twenty-two. And now we'd lost a day in a four-day window. I broke the news at the truck, and I watched the energy just fall out of my crew like air out of a tire. Marco looked at me. Just looked. Didn't say anything. Cody said, "So we're redoing the whole hall?" I said yeah. Cody did not say anything after that, which honestly felt worse.

That night I did what every foreman does in that situation. I called around in my head and I tried to figure out who was to blame. Big Dave didn't check his measurements. Marco should have caught it faster. Cody should have been watching the layout more carefully. Terrence — well, Terrence hadn't done anything wrong, but I thought about it for a second anyway. That is the natural human response to a problem: find the nearest person who isn't you.

But here's the thing. And this is the thing that the book that these two sister companies are working through right now — Extreme Ownership by Jocko Willink and Leif Babin — this is the thing they put right in front of you in one of the most direct chapter titles in the whole book: No Bad Teams, Only Bad Leaders.

And when I first heard that chapter title, my gut reaction was, well, that can't be entirely true. Because I have worked with some very bad teammates in my career. And that is still my gut reaction, honestly. But here is what the principle is actually saying, and it's more precise than it sounds. It's not saying your team is full of geniuses who just need inspiration. It's not saying talent doesn't matter or that some people aren't difficult to work with. What it's saying is this: when a team consistently underperforms, when the same mistakes happen over and over, when the crew can't seem to get a clean run on anything — the variable to examine is the leader. Not because the leader is necessarily a bad person, but because the leader is the one with the ability to see the whole picture, to set the standard, to correct problems before they compound, and to own the outcome regardless of who physically made the error.

The authors frame it around an exercise where they take the best-performing team in a training scenario and swap its leader with the leader of the worst-performing team. And what happens is predictable and uncomfortable: the performance follows the leader. The good leader walks into the struggling team and they start producing. The original team, now led by someone who had been coasting on their talent, starts to drift. Because the leader sets the ceiling and the floor. The leader establishes what's acceptable. The leader decides, consciously or unconsciously, how many wrong holes you're going to let get drilled before you say something.

That last one's mine, not theirs. But you see how it applies.

The hard part of this principle — the part that actually stings when you sit with it — is that it doesn't let you outsource accountability to the crew. You can have a guy like Big Dave who makes a real mistake. The mistake is real. But the question the principle forces you to ask is: what did you, the leader, do or not do that allowed that mistake to go on for two hours? Did you brief the crew on the non-standard stud spacing? Did you check behind them at the midpoint? Did you create a culture where anyone on your crew would feel comfortable saying "hey Dave, those look off to me" without worrying about the awkward silence after?

That last one is sneaky. Because the culture of a team is entirely set by the leader. If the foreman doesn't correct things early, the crew learns that early correction isn't the norm. If the foreman gets annoyed when someone flags a problem, the crew learns to not flag problems. If the foreman says "we'll figure it out at the end of the day," the crew learns that the standard is: finish first, accuracy later. You don't set that culture with a speech. You set it minute by minute, on every job, with every small decision you make about whether to say something or let it ride.

And this principle lands differently when you're a foreman versus when you're a superintendent or a project manager, because you are IN it. You're not looking at a report at the end of the day. You are physically present. Your standard is visible to your crew in real time. Which means you have more power to change things than almost anyone else in the chain — and fewer excuses for not seeing them.

So what does this actually look like in practice for a Horsepower Electric foreman? Let me give you four things you can do this week. Not theory. Actual behaviors.

First one: set the scope briefing as a non-negotiable at the start of every day, and make it specific enough to actually be useful. This is different from a tailgate talk where you say "okay, rough-in today, let's be safe." I mean a five-minute rundown where you say, "here is what's different about this area today, here are the things I need everyone to look for, here is the one thing that could go wrong." On that job in October, I knew about the non-standard stud spacing. I knew it and I didn't brief it. If I had said, "Hey Big Dave, heads up, the framing crew put an extra stud around the corner at the hallway, your spacing is going to be off from the plan when you get there, stop and measure before you drill," that's three sentences, and we don't lose a day. The briefing isn't about protecting yourself. It's about giving your crew the information they need to succeed. When you don't brief the specific hazard, and then the specific hazard bites someone, that's on you. Not on them.

Second behavior: build a midpoint check into your day. This sounds like project management talk, but it's simpler than that. Just identify, before the day starts, one thing that needs to be true at noon for the day to stay on track. Just one. Could be "we're in the master suite by noon." Could be "wire is pulled on the first floor before lunch." And then actually go check it at noon. Not to hover. Not to micromanage. Just to see where things are while there's still time to adjust. The problem with end-of-day accountability is that by the time you're counting holes at the truck, there's nothing to adjust. You're just doing math on how bad the next two days are going to be. The midpoint check gives you a window where you can say, "Hey Cody, you're going to be short on those boxes, let me call in a run before the supply house closes." That's a foreman move. Waiting until 4:30 to discover you're short is how you end up at Home Depot paying retail.

Third behavior: correct it the first time you see it, out loud, without making it a whole thing. This is the one that requires the most practice because it goes against the social instinct to avoid awkward moments. When I saw Big Dave's holes were off, the thing I should have done is walked over to Big Dave, not Marco, and said, "Hey Dave, I think you're off four inches here — that extra stud moved your reference point. Let me show you where I want the series to land." That's it. No audience, no drama, no calling him out in front of the whole crew. Just a direct, factual correction delivered once, clearly, by the person in charge. What I did instead was triangulate through Marco, which told Big Dave nothing, told Marco that I saw the problem and chose not to address it, and told Cody and Terrence that the foreman notices things but doesn't always act on them. One small redirection, done early and clearly, is worth two hours of re-work and a demoralized crew at the end of the day.

Fourth behavior, and this one matters a lot on high-end residential jobs where the expectations are premium: when something goes wrong, brief the crew on the solution before you brief them on the problem. What I mean is, the way you deliver bad news sets the temperature for what comes next. If you walk to the truck and say, "So, all those holes are in the wrong place, we've got to redo the whole hallway," what your crew hears is: this is a disaster and we're behind. If instead you say, "Here's the plan for tomorrow — we're going to knock out the hallway re-run first thing, I've already mapped the corrected series, we'll have it done by ten and we're back on schedule by afternoon," what your crew hears is: the foreman has a handle on this and I know what I'm doing tomorrow morning. Same bad news. Different starting point for tomorrow's energy. As a foreman you control that framing. And the crew that shows up believing they can recover performs better than the crew that shows up already feeling like they're losing.

Now let's head over to the Wells Cutting Edge side of the job. Same Tuesday energy, completely different crew, completely different chaos. But the lesson is going to land in the same place, so stay with me.

Over at Wells Cutting Edge, the high-end residential landscaping side of this operation, it's late April. The spring install season is starting to hit full stride, and there's a job on the books that the sales team has been excited about for three months. Big backyard transformation — custom paver patio, raised planting beds, a full irrigation system, boulder placement along a dry creek feature, and a perimeter planting package with some specimen trees and ornamental grasses. The kind of job that, when it's done and photographed, ends up on the company's social media and the homeowner sends their friends your way.

The foreman on this job — I'll put myself in this chair for the story, that's how these work — is managing a crew of five. I've got my irrigation guy, who I'll call Pete. Two paver guys, let's say Dom and Anton. A general laborer named Kyle who is strong, eager, and unfortunately has a relationship with "close enough" that I am constantly trying to end. And a new hire named Simone who is technically sharp but has been on the job eight weeks and is still learning the pace of how we run.

The first week goes well. Grading is done, irrigation sleeves are in, the base compaction on the patio area is looking right. The second week, the pavers show up. And this is where I need to tell you something about how paver installs go on high-end jobs, because context matters here.

The homeowner on this project had chosen a specific herringbone pattern in a two-tone blend — a lighter buff color and a charcoal grey, alternating in sections, with a border course in full charcoal. It's a sophisticated look. It takes planning. The design drawings had a very clear layout diagram with measurements from the house foundation as the reference point.

Dom is my lead paver guy. He's been doing this eleven years. He knows herringbone patterns the way Big Dave knows a drill. He gets in the zone. He sets his reference lines, he starts cutting and laying, and he moves fast. Anton is his second, keeping up with the cuts. Kyle is hauling material and trying to be helpful in ways that are sometimes helpful and sometimes not.

And I'm moving around the job, because that's what I do, checking in on Pete who's working on the irrigation heads in the back bed, checking where Simone is on the planting bed excavation, and I come back around to Dom and the patio at about eleven o'clock and something is wrong.

I can see it immediately, but I stand there for a second convincing myself I'm wrong. I'm not wrong. The reference point Dom started from is not the house foundation. It's the edge of the property, which is fine on a rectangular lot where those two things are parallel, but this lot has a very slight diagonal grade change and the house is not perfectly square to the property line, and what this means is that Dom's pattern, which is gorgeous, which is being laid with perfect spacing and beautiful cuts — Dom's pattern is going to run out of square by the time it hits the far end of the patio, and the border course on the far side is going to be visibly off-angle to the back door threshold.

How far off? Maybe three inches over thirty feet. Which sounds like nothing. On a patio you see from the kitchen every day for twenty years, it is very much something.

I stood there and I did the thing that I now recognize as a leadership failure in progress: I started doing math in my head about how hard the fix would be, and I let the answer to that math influence whether I was going to say something. The fix was going to be disruptive. Dom was going to be frustrated. Anton was going to have to reset cuts. We were probably going to lose the better part of a day.

So I walked over to Kyle, who was hauling sand, and I said, "Hey, you notice anything off about the layout?" And Kyle squinted at the patio and said, "It looks pretty good to me." And I said, "Yeah, okay," and I went to go check on Pete.

And that is, honestly, one of the more embarrassing things I can tell you about myself as a foreman. I asked the newest general laborer on the crew whether the layout looked right, got the answer I was hoping for, and used that as permission to walk away from a problem I already knew was real.

By the end of that day, Dom and Anton had laid roughly sixty percent of the patio. The pattern was beautiful. The spacing was perfect. The cuts were clean. And it was going to have to come up.

I found out at the end of the day because I actually went and measured it, finally, when the crew was loading up. And I stood at the far corner where the border was going to be and I held my tape to the back door threshold and I watched the number come up and I said a word out loud that I will not repeat here but that Terrence — who wasn't even on this job, but just for the sake of argument — Terrence would have found very educational.

I called the foreman at another crew to talk it through, because that's what you do. And he said, "So you saw it at eleven and you're calling me at four thirty?" And I said, "Yeah." And there was a pause. And he said, "Okay, so what's the lesson?" And I said some things about Dom and the reference point. And he let me finish and then he said, "What's the actual lesson?" And I didn't answer, because I already knew the answer, and the answer was uncomfortable.

Here is what the principle says again, because it deserves a second look from the landscaping side: no bad teams, only bad leaders. Dom didn't make a mistake because he was a bad paver. Dom made a mistake because nobody — not me during the morning setup, not me at eleven o'clock, not me at any point between setup and when I finally measured at four-thirty — nobody had checked his reference point and confirmed it was right. That is a leadership failure dressed up as a Dom failure. And I had let it go for the same reason I let Big Dave's holes go: because stopping it felt harder in the short term than letting it run.

And here is something specific to high-end landscaping jobs that makes this principle bite harder: on a job with a pattern install, a grading decision, a drainage slope — these are the kinds of mistakes that compound geometrically, not linearly. It's not that you lay one bad paver. It's that every paver after the first one builds on the error, and the further you go, the more you have to undo. The math on "not stopping it now" always gets worse. Always. A pattern problem you address at eleven o'clock costs you an hour. A pattern problem you address at four thirty costs you a day and a half and a very difficult phone call to the homeowner.

Same principle. Same silence. Same cost.

So what does this look like for a Wells Cutting Edge foreman this week? Let me give you four things.

First: on any install that uses a reference point — pavers, edging, plant spacing, drainage slope — before the first piece goes in, you confirm the reference out loud with your lead. You say, "Show me where you're running your line from." You physically walk to that point together. You look at it. You either confirm it or you don't. This takes four minutes. Four minutes at setup is worth four hours of re-work. And I want to be specific about WHY you do it out loud together, because this matters: it's not because you don't trust your lead. Dom has eleven years. Dom is excellent. You do it out loud because it creates a shared moment of confirmation, which means that both of you are now responsible for that decision. You're not standing over his shoulder watching him work — you're solving the problem together before it's a problem. That's a completely different relationship, and your lead guy will feel the difference.

Second behavior: know which crew member is most likely to rush and set your check-in cadence around them, not around the clock. Every crew has a rusher. Kyle, on my crew, is the guy who is always slightly ahead of "done" when it comes to judgment calls. Doesn't mean he's bad at his job. It means that when Kyle is doing something that requires precision, I'm checking it sooner, not waiting for the standard check. The foreman's job is to know the crew well enough to know where the risk is, and then be present at the risk point. Not hovering. Not second-guessing everything. Just knowing that certain people in certain situations need a second set of eyes earlier. Build that into your day.

Third behavior: when you see something wrong and you're not sure if it's wrong, go measure it before you find someone else to tell you it's fine. That is the thing I did with Kyle that I should never have done. When I saw Dom's pattern and felt something was off, I had a tape measure on my belt. I could have had a number in sixty seconds. Instead I asked a guy with eight weeks of experience whether it "looked pretty good" and accepted his shrug as data. Your tools are your tools. When your gut says something's off on a layout, your gut is usually pattern-matching to something real. Confirm or deny it with the instrument, not with the closest available person who will tell you what you want to hear.

Fourth behavior, and this one's specific to high-end jobs with homeowners who are very emotionally invested in what the finished product looks like: develop the habit of narrating your progress to yourself at midday in terms of "what would I be embarrassed to show the homeowner right now." This sounds a little dramatic, but hear me out. On high-end residential landscape work, the homeowner has been picturing this backyard for a long time. They have a photo saved on their phone. They have told their neighbors about it. When you stand at the midpoint of a day and ask yourself, "If the homeowner pulled up right now, what would I be quietly hoping they didn't look at," that question finds problems faster than any checklist. It makes you see the job through their eyes instead of through the lens of "is the crew moving." Because movement and quality are not the same thing. A crew can be moving fast and beautifully in exactly the wrong direction. Your job is to notice the direction, not just the speed.

And here's one thing that connects all four of those behaviors to the bigger principle: each of them requires you to be present in a specific way. Not just physically on-site, which any foreman can be. But mentally engaged with what's actually happening, not what you're hoping is happening. The gap between "I was on-site all day" and "I was leading all day" is exactly the gap between what I did on that patio job and what I should have done. I was on-site. I saw the problem. I walked away and hoped it would resolve itself or that I had been wrong. That is not presence. That is avoidance wearing a hard hat.

The principle — no bad teams, only bad leaders — is ultimately about this specific thing. It's not about being perfect. Dom is going to lay patterns from the wrong reference point sometimes. Big Dave is going to drill holes in a trance. That's not a moral failure, that's a human doing a physically demanding job at pace. The principle is asking something of the leader specifically: be the one who catches it. Set it up so it's less likely to happen. And when it does happen, own the catch.

Here's what's true about both of these crews — the Horsepower Electric guys and the Wells Cutting Edge guys — despite the fact that one is running conduit and pulling wire through a second-floor hallway and the other is laying herringbone pavers in someone's backyard. The pressure is the same. The timeline is the same. The GC, the homeowner, the design review board — they don't care which guy made the mistake. They care whether the job is right. And the person who is responsible for the job being right is the foreman. Not because the foreman touches every outlet, lays every paver, or drills every hole. Because the foreman is the one who sees the whole picture, sets the standard, and says something the moment the job starts going sideways — not at the truck at the end of the day when everyone is tired and the damage is already done.

There's a version of the foreman job where you're really just the most experienced worker on-site. You do your tasks, you let everyone else do their tasks, and you see how it all adds up at four thirty. And then there's the actual job. The actual job is that you are watching, you are present, you are catching things early, you are correcting them clearly and without drama, you are keeping the crew informed about what's in front of them, and when something goes wrong — and it will go wrong, it always goes wrong — you are the first to say "that's on me" and the first to lay out what happens next.

The principle doesn't promise that leadership makes every job easy. It promises that the quality of the team is a reflection of the quality of the leader. Which means if your crew keeps making the same mistakes, the question to ask yourself is not "what is wrong with these people" but "what am I not showing them."

No bad teams, only bad leaders. Now go check your reference point before the first piece goes in.
