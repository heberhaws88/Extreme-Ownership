Alright, so picture this. It's a Tuesday morning, seven-fifteen, and you're standing in the garage of a forty-two-hundred square foot custom home in the middle of rough-in. The framing crew just cleared out on Friday, which means you've got maybe a week, week and a half before drywall shows up and makes your life significantly more complicated. The GC has already texted you twice this morning, and it's not even seven-thirty. Your apprentice, we'll call him Derek, is standing next to a half-open box of Romex looking at you like a dog that heard a strange noise, head tilted, waiting for direction. And the other guy on your crew, a second-year apprentice named Marcus, is doing something with a drill in the master bedroom that you cannot identify from here but does not sound right. And you are the journeyman. You are the guy who's supposed to have this handled.

You've got six hours until the inspector is scheduled to come by for a rough-in walk on the guest suite addition, which was supposed to be the easy part of this job. And you just realized, standing there in that garage with your coffee going cold, that nobody — not you, not Derek, not Marcus — actually confirmed the location of the dedicated circuit for the sauna that the homeowner added to the scope three weeks ago. It's in the notes somewhere. You've seen the email. You remember thinking, okay, I'll figure that out when we get to that wing. Well. You're at that wing.

This is the part of the story where a lot of journeymen start doing the mental math on how this is someone else's problem. The designer didn't mark it clearly on the plans. The GC should have flagged it in the scope meeting. The homeowner added it late. Derek should have asked. Marcus probably knew and didn't say anything. There's a whole cast of characters available to absorb the blame here, and the math is tempting. But here's the thing — and this is what this whole episode is about — the fact that you're standing there doing that math instead of running that circuit is itself the problem. And that problem has a name, and we're going to get into it.

But first, let's let this situation get a little worse. Because it's going to, the way these things do.

Derek, to his credit, is not just standing there anymore. He has found the email about the sauna circuit and is reading it out loud to you off his phone. The news is mixed. The good news is the homeowner wants a two-forty circuit with a sixty-amp breaker for a premium sauna unit they haven't purchased yet. The bad news is the email says, quote, "location TBD pending final sauna dimensions," and that email is dated three weeks ago, and nobody — not the GC, not the designer, not you — ever circled back to make it D. It is very much still TBD. The inspector is coming in six hours. The wall where the sauna is probably going is currently full of someone else's plumbing rough-in that the plumbers ran last week. And Marcus has just appeared in the doorway of the garage to tell you that the three-way switch setup in the master hallway is, and I quote, "a little weird."

So now you've got three problems, and you are the journeyman. You've been in the trade long enough to know how to run every one of these circuits in your sleep. You know exactly what a sixty-amp two-forty rough-in looks like, you know how to sort out a funky three-way, and you know how to have a conversation with a GC about scope that's still floating. You have all the technical knowledge. What you maybe don't have is a clear idea of whose job it is to do what right now, and whether the people working with you actually understand what done looks like, and whether anyone has been set up to succeed this morning or just sort of pointed at the house and told to get after it.

Because here's what actually happened in the days leading up to this moment. You showed up Monday, you did a quick walkthrough, you split the work up kind of verbally — Derek, you're on the bedroom circuits, Marcus, you're doing the hallway switches, I'll handle the main panel work and the kitchen. And that was the plan. That was the whole plan. No written list. No checkpoint at lunch to see where things were. No conversation about what to flag immediately versus what to figure out as you go. Just three guys in a house doing their best impression of a coordinated crew.

And Derek, who is a solid kid and genuinely works hard, doesn't know what he doesn't know. He ran his bedroom circuits. He did it mostly right. He hit a question on the sauna rough-in location and, because there was no clear process for what to do with a question like that, he did what people do when there's no clear process — he kept moving on the stuff he was sure about and quietly set the sauna question aside. Not because he's lazy. Because nobody told him that a floating scope item like that was a stop-everything conversation, not a figure-it-out-later detail. That's not Derek's failure. That's an information failure, and the information failure starts at the top of this tiny three-person crew.

And Marcus, who has been in the trade long enough to have opinions, ran the three-way switches based on how he's seen it done before — which might be fine, or might be "a little weird," and the reason you don't know yet is that there was never a moment where you looked at his work before it became a problem for you to diagnose at seven-forty in the morning with an inspector coming at one.

So let's pause here. Because this is exactly the situation the book is built for.

Jocko Willink and Leif Babin make an argument in Extreme Ownership that sounds simple when you first hear it and gets more uncomfortable the longer you sit with it. The argument is this: there are no bad teams, only bad leaders. And when they say that, they're not being cute or provocative just to sell books. They mean it in a very literal, operational way. The performance of a team is a direct reflection of the leadership that team is receiving. Not the talent of the team. Not the effort. Not the conditions. The leadership.

They illustrate this in the book with a story from BUD/S, the Navy SEAL training program, where boat crews compete against each other and the results are almost always predictably lopsided — the same crews win, the same crews struggle. And then the instructors do something interesting. They swap the leaders. They take the leader of the winning crew and put him with the losing crew, and they take the leader of the losing crew and put him with the winners. And what happens is almost immediate. The crew that was losing starts getting better. The crew that was winning starts slipping. The people didn't change. The boat didn't change. The ocean didn't change. The leader changed. And that was enough.

Now, most of us listening to this are not Navy SEALs doing boat races in the Pacific. We are journeymen running two-man and three-man crews through high-end residential work on a schedule that does not have a lot of sympathy for surprises. But the principle maps perfectly, because what a leader actually does — whether that's a lieutenant in BUD/S or a journeyman on a rough-in — is set the conditions for the people around them to succeed or fail. You set the conditions. Your apprentices execute within those conditions. If the conditions are clear, specific, and well-checked, good things happen. If the conditions are vague, assumed, and unchecked, you get a Tuesday morning in a garage with cold coffee and three open problems and an inspector six hours out.

The thing that makes this principle uncomfortable is that it removes the exit ramp most of us use when things go sideways. The exit ramp is blame. When Derek didn't flag the sauna circuit, the comfortable move is to be frustrated with Derek. When Marcus ran a weird three-way, the comfortable move is to sigh heavily in his direction and fix it yourself without really explaining what was wrong. But those moves don't make your crew better. They make your crew nervous and unclear, which makes your next Tuesday morning just as bad as this one.

What the principle asks you to do instead is harder, because it starts with a question you have to ask yourself. And the question is: did I set my people up to succeed? Did they know what to do with ambiguity? Did they know what done looks like? Did they know when to stop and ask versus when to use their judgment and keep moving? If the answer to any of those is no, that's not their failure to own. It's yours. And once you own it, you can fix it. Which is actually the good news buried in an uncomfortable idea — if the problem is yours, so is the solution.

So let's talk about what that actually looks like for a Horsepower Electric journeyman this week. Not in theory. In practice. Here on the job.

The first thing is the morning setup, and it sounds almost too simple, which is why it gets skipped. Before the first drill turns on, before anybody pulls wire, you take five minutes — five actual minutes — and you run through the day with your crew out loud. Not a monologue where you tell them what you're doing. A conversation where you tell them what each of them is doing, what done looks like for their piece, and what to do if they hit something unexpected. That last part is the one that usually gets left out. You tell Derek he's on bedroom circuits, great. Do you tell Derek that if he hits a scope item that doesn't have a confirmed location he stops and brings it to you before he moves past it? Probably not. But that one sentence is the difference between a sauna circuit that gets flagged at seven-fifteen and one that gets discovered at seven-forty with an inspector on the way.

You're not micromanaging when you do this. You're setting parameters. There's a difference. Micromanaging is hovering over someone while they work and correcting every decision in real time. Setting parameters is spending five minutes at the start of the day defining what the decision-making rules are so you don't have to hover. One of those is exhausting for everyone. The other one creates a crew that can actually move independently because they understand the framework.

The second thing is the mid-morning check. Pick a time — ten o'clock, ten-thirty, whatever makes sense for your job — and do a lap. Not a performance review, not a gotcha tour, just a quick eyes-on of where each person is against where you expected them to be. You're looking for two things: work that's ahead of schedule, because that sometimes means someone is moving fast and cutting corners, and work that's behind, because that sometimes means someone hit a problem they haven't told you about yet. Both of those are worth a conversation. "Hey, how's it going, what are you running into" is the whole script. You'd be amazed what comes out of that question if you ask it before it becomes a crisis rather than after.

The third thing is the feedback loop, and this is where a lot of journeymen leave money on the table. When Marcus runs a weird three-way switch, you have a choice in how you handle it. You can fix it yourself quietly and move on, which is fast and feels efficient. You can sigh and fix it while Marcus watches, which communicates your frustration but doesn't actually teach anything. Or you can stop for three minutes, show Marcus what's off, explain why it matters — maybe it's a code thing, maybe it's a troubleshooting-later thing, maybe it's just a this-GC-is-detail-oriented thing — and make sure he walks away knowing how to do it right next time. Option three takes the longest in the moment. It takes the least time over the course of a project, because you're not fixing the same thing twice.

And the fourth thing — and this one is specifically for the floating scope situations, because high-end residential work is basically a steady stream of floating scope situations — is that you need to be the one closing the loop with the GC, not waiting for the GC to close it with you. When Derek found that sauna email with "location TBD," the right move was for you to already have that conversation with the GC three weeks ago. But since you didn't, the right move now is for you to call the GC before the inspector shows up, have a direct conversation about where the sauna is landing and what the rough-in needs to look like, and document it. Not a long email. A text that says "Confirmed sauna location is the north wall of the spa room, running a sixty-amp two-forty from the sub-panel, marking for inspection this afternoon" and a thumbs up back. That's your paper trail. That's your scope confirmation. And that habit — of being the person who closes the floating items rather than waiting for someone else to — is what separates a journeyman who's always in reaction mode from one who's actually running the job.

None of those four things require you to be a different person or to have a different crew. They just require you to understand that your job as the journeyman is not just to run wire. It's to run the conditions under which good work gets done. That's what a leader does. And like it or not, when you're the most experienced person on that crew, you're the leader. Whether you asked for it or not.

Because about two hundred yards from where Derek and Marcus are sorting out that sauna rough-in, there's a Wells Cutting Edge crew on a landscape install that is having what you might charitably call a character-building morning. Let's go meet them.

It's eight-ten on a Wednesday, and the crew is working on a backyard renovation for a homeowner who has opinions. Lots of opinions. The kind of opinions that come with a printed Pinterest board and a design consultant who sends emails at eleven at night. The scope is substantial — natural stone patio, built-in planter walls, a full irrigation system with drip zones for the planting beds, and a full-depth gravel drainage swale along the back fence line that will eventually connect to a channel drain. It's a good job. It's a big job. And the journeyman on this crew — let's call him Ray — has been in landscaping long enough to know that big jobs with opinionated homeowners require a lot of things to go right in sequence.

The crew has four guys: Ray, a second-year apprentice named Carlos who is excellent with the irrigation layout, a newer guy named Briggs who came from a general landscaping background and is still learning hardscape, and a guy named Terrence who is fast with a plate compactor and absolutely cannot be trusted to make independent decisions about gravel depth. This is a known thing. Ray knows this about Terrence. The GC knows this about Terrence. Terrence, to his enormous credit, is aware of it himself and generally tries to ask before he goes too deep on anything.

The natural stone for the patio came in last Thursday. It's beautiful, it's expensive, and the design calls for a specific pattern — a random ashlar layout with a twelve-inch border course all the way around in a contrasting color. The homeowner has seen a rendering. The homeowner has approved the rendering. The homeowner has a neighbor coming over on Saturday to see the progress, and she mentioned this to the GC, who mentioned it to Ray, and now there is a soft but real Saturday pressure on a Wednesday morning.

Here's where things start going sideways in the way that landscape jobs go sideways, which is usually quietly, over the course of several hours, until someone walks around a corner and goes oh no.

Ray's plan for the morning was straightforward. Carlos starts running the drip zone irrigation in the planting beds on the west side, because those need to be in before the gravel goes down and before the plants go in next week. Briggs starts dry-laying the border course on the south end of the patio so Ray can confirm the layout before anybody starts setting stone in mortar. And Terrence — and this is where the seeds of the problem get planted, no pun intended — Terrence is going to start on the drainage swale along the back fence.

The problem is that Ray told Terrence to start on the swale the same way you tell someone to start on the swale when you assume they know what you know. Which is to say: he said "go ahead and get the swale started, we need it rough-graded before the patio area is closed off." And Terrence, who is fast with a compactor and eager to contribute, interpreted "rough-graded" the way he's seen it done on a couple of other jobs, which is a slightly different interpretation than what the drainage design actually calls for in this case.

The drainage design on this job has a specific bottom elevation that needs to hit a catch basin at the southeast corner. It's in the plans. The plans are in Ray's truck. Ray knows what the plans say. What Ray did not do this morning is walk Terrence to the southeast corner, show him the catch basin, confirm the slope they're targeting, and establish a stake or a grade pin that Terrence can work back from. Ray said "rough-grade the swale" and Terrence said "got it" and Ray went to check on Briggs and the border course.

Two and a half hours later, Ray does his first real check on the swale. And it's moving. Terrence has been moving dirt with genuine commitment for two and a half hours, and the swale looks great in the sense that it looks like a swale. It does not look great in the sense that the slope is running toward the southeast corner but not quite steeply enough, and at the midpoint there's a low spot — a subtle dip that Ray can see immediately because he knows what he's looking for — that is going to pond water if it rains before they get the drainage channel installed. And if it rains while the back fence area is still open and they haven't gotten the gravel in yet, that low spot is going to pond water directly adjacent to the homeowner's new raised planting bed. Which is adjacent to the back corner of the house.

Water pooling against a foundation is not a Saturday-neighbor-visit kind of problem. It's a warranty call, a potential HOA flag, and in the worst case, a callback that costs more than the job made. So this is actually a real problem, not just a tidy-up situation.

But here's the part where Ray, to his credit, doesn't do what a lot of journeymen do in this moment. He doesn't walk up to Terrence and say "man, what did you do here." He doesn't make a big show of pulling out a level and demonstrating the slope issue in a way that's more about Ray being right than Terrence understanding what happened. He takes a breath, he pulls out the plans, and he walks Terrence to the southeast corner and shows him the catch basin. And he asks Terrence what grade he was working toward.

Terrence gives him an answer that makes perfect sense if you've seen a couple of other drainage installs but doesn't account for the specific catch basin elevation on this job. And Ray realizes, in that moment, that Terrence wasn't making a bad decision. He was making the best decision he could with the information Ray gave him, which was not enough information.

Which is a rough feeling. Because the problem isn't Terrence's grade call. The problem is that Ray handed Terrence a task without a target. "Rough-grade the swale" is a task. "Rough-grade the swale so it's dropping four inches over thirty feet toward the catch basin — come get me when you hit the midpoint so I can check the grade before you go further" is a task with a target, a checkpoint, and a clear success condition. Those are very different things, and the difference between them is now sitting in Ray's front yard in the form of a low spot that Terrence is going to spend the next hour correcting.

Meanwhile, Carlos has run into a situation on the west-side irrigation. One of the emitter locations the designer specified is landing directly on top of a tree root that's a lot bigger than anyone expected from the original site survey. It's not a crisis, but it's a decision point — do you move the emitter, do you relocate the whole lateral line, do you call the designer? Carlos is smart. Carlos has good instincts. But Carlos also does not know what the designer's priorities are, or whether the homeowner has expressed particular attachment to that tree, or whether the design-review board that this HOA apparently takes extremely seriously has any opinion about irrigation layout relative to protected trees. Carlos does not have that context. And because there was no "here's what to do when you hit a decision point" conversation at the start of the day, Carlos does what Derek did on the electrical side. He sets the question aside and keeps running the other laterals, planning to ask Ray when Ray surfaces.

Which is fine, except Ray is now regrading the swale with Terrence, and the afternoon is moving, and the irrigation decision that felt small in the morning is going to get to end of day without a resolution if nobody catches it, and next week when the plants go in Carlos is going to be working around a lateral that should have been moved.

So by eleven-thirty, Ray has a swale that's being corrected, a border course that Briggs has been dry-laying all morning on a section Ray hasn't checked yet, and an irrigation question sitting in Carlos's head that hasn't surfaced. He's managed to create, on a crew of four experienced guys, a situation where nobody really knows what the other people know, nobody knows exactly what decisions are in their lane, and the work that's happening is happening in relative isolation from the conditions that would make it go well.

And again — not a bad crew. Terrence works hard. Carlos has great hands. Briggs is learning and trying. Ray knows the trade. The problem isn't talent or effort. The problem is leadership structure, which is to say, the problem is that the conditions for success were not set before the work started.

So let's talk about what the Wells Cutting Edge journeyman does with this, because the applications are a little different from the electrical side, even though the principle is identical.

The first thing is the pre-work site walk, and for landscaping this matters even more than in electrical because so much of landscape work is about understanding what the ground is telling you before you start moving it. Before the crew splits up in the morning, the journeyman walks the site with the crew — even just ten minutes — and physically points to the things that matter. You walk to the catch basin and you say: this is what we're grading toward, this elevation, this slope. You show Terrence the grade pin, or you put one in together. You don't assume that everyone's mental picture of the finished grade matches yours, because it probably doesn't, and the only way to know is to walk it out loud. Landscaping is inherently three-dimensional and topographic in a way that's hard to convey with words alone. The site walk is how you get everyone into the same mental model of what this yard is supposed to become.

The second thing, and this one is big for irrigation and drainage work specifically, is the tier-one decision list. Before work starts, you tell your crew explicitly — not generally, explicitly — what the decisions are that they need to bring to you before moving past. Not every decision, because that's how you create a crew that can't function without you hovering. Specific decisions. "If you hit an obstruction on the lateral line that moves the emitter more than two feet from the design location, come get me before you reroute." "If the grade is reading more than a quarter inch off at any five-foot span, come get me before you continue." Those are objective, specific, easy-to-apply rules. Carlos hits a tree root, he checks the rule — does this emitter need to move more than two feet from the design location? Yes. Okay, that's a Ray question. He goes and gets Ray. That's not a failure of Carlos's judgment. That's Carlos using a framework that Ray gave him to make a good decision about when to stop and escalate. That's a crew working well.

The third thing is the mid-morning check on hardscape, because hardscape is unforgiving in a way that a lot of landscape tasks aren't. Once you've set stone in mortar, you've set it. Once a paver pattern is cut and laid over compacted base, changing it is not a small thing. So when Briggs is dry-laying that border course, you don't wait until he's been at it for four hours to go look at it. You check it early. You're not second-guessing Briggs — you're verifying that the starting point is right before the whole sequence builds off it. In high-end residential hardscape with a pattern that the homeowner has a rendered image of in her head, the border course is the thing that sets the whole visual. If the south border is off by half an inch on the layout, the whole pattern drifts. You catch that at forty-five minutes, it's a five-minute correction. You catch it at four hours, you're resetting half a morning's work and explaining to the homeowner why her stone is off-pattern.

The fourth thing — and this is the one that's hardest for landscape journeymen because landscape work feels more fluid than electrical work, more organic, more like you figure it out as you go — is real-time scope flagging with the GC and the designer. Landscape projects at the high end involve a lot of people with a lot of opinions: the homeowner, the landscape designer, possibly an HOA or design review board, sometimes a pool contractor or outdoor lighting crew that you're coordinating around. The journeyman on the ground is the person closest to the actual conditions. If there's a tree root that changes the irrigation layout, or a soil situation that affects the grading, or a material that isn't behaving the way the design assumed — that information needs to move up fast, because decisions made downstream of that information will all be wrong. You are not bothering anyone by making a call. You are providing the field intelligence that the design and GC side of this project cannot get any other way. Get comfortable picking up the phone. "Hey, hit a significant root on the west lateral, designer should probably know before I reroute — you want me to send you a photo or just note it on the as-built?" That call takes ninety seconds and it protects everyone.

What all four of those things have in common is this: they put you, the journeyman, in the role of the person who is actively creating the conditions for your crew to succeed, rather than the person who is reacting to the conditions your crew has created. That's the shift. It sounds small. The experience on the ground is very different.

When Ray goes back and looks at the morning — at the swale, at the irrigation question floating in Carlos's head, at Briggs going for four hours without a check — he's going to see a series of moments where the information was available and the people were capable and the work still went sideways. Not because the crew was bad. Because the crew was unled. And "unled" doesn't mean Ray was absent. It means Ray did not do the leadership things — the setup, the checkpoints, the clear decision rules — that convert a group of capable people into a crew that performs like a team.

You can have the best stone mason in the county on your crew and still end up with a patio that's off-pattern if nobody confirmed the layout at the start. You can have an apprentice with great irrigation instincts and still end up with a lateral in the wrong place if he didn't know when a decision was his to make versus yours. The talent is necessary but not sufficient. The leadership is what makes the talent produce the result.

And here's the thing that both Ray and the journeyman over at Horsepower Electric need to sit with. This principle — no bad teams, only bad leaders — it's not a criticism of the leader. Or rather, it is, but it's a useful one. It's not "you're bad at your job." It's "the performance of your team is in your hands, which means you actually have the power to change it." That's an enormous amount of leverage if you use it right. It means a crew that's struggling isn't a crew that needs different people. It might just be a crew that needs different leadership.

Willink and Babin's point is that the performance conversation always goes upward before it goes outward. Before you look at what your crew is doing wrong, you look at what you're doing — or not doing — that's creating the environment where they're doing it wrong. That's a more uncomfortable conversation to have with yourself, but it's also a more productive one, because it's a conversation that ends with actions you can take rather than frustrations you can't resolve.

Both of those crews this morning — the three guys running rough-in for Horsepower Electric and the four guys grading and planting for Wells Cutting Edge — they came to work wanting to do good work. That's almost always true, by the way. People show up wanting to succeed. They need to be led into the conditions where success is actually possible.

Derek wasn't trying to leave a floating scope item. He just didn't know that floating scope items were always his journeyman's conversation to have. Terrence wasn't trying to get the grade wrong. He just didn't have the elevation target in his head when he started moving dirt. Marcus wasn't trying to wire a confusing three-way. He just reproduced the pattern he'd been taught and nobody checked before it became a diagnosis problem at seven-forty in the morning. These aren't bad apprentices. They're apprentices working in conditions that weren't quite right. Set the conditions right, and you'll be amazed how capable they already are.

That's what leadership looks like from the journeyman seat. It's not a title. It's not a hard hat with a different color. It's the five-minute morning setup. It's the mid-morning lap. It's the three-minute feedback conversation instead of the silent fix. It's the call to the GC before the problem sits overnight. It's the site walk where you put your hands on the grade pin together. It's the conversation where you tell your crew what decisions are theirs to make and which ones come to you. None of it is dramatic. All of it compounds.

Because the team you lead is a reflection of the leadership you give. And that reflection is something you can change, starting tomorrow morning, starting with your first cup of coffee before the first drill turns on.
