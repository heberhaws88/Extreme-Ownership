It is not fine.

And now the room is moving, and the re-route got bigger, and the secret got heavier, and Marcus is standing there thinking: I have no idea why we did it this way in the first place, and I have no idea whether the new way is even legal, and I am absolutely not going to ask because asking means admitting I don't know, and I've been here two years and I should know.

That is where we're starting today. That moment. That feeling. Because that feeling is the enemy of what today's episode is actually about.

Welcome to Extreme Ownership: Wired. Episode nine. This week's chapter is Believe. And if you are an apprentice at Horsepower Electric or a ground-level crew member at Wells Cutting Edge, this one is going to hit close to home. Because believing in what you're doing — really believing in it, not just going through the motions — is harder than it sounds, especially when you're new, especially when you're confused, and especially when the plan feels like it came from someone who's never touched a wire or a shovel.

Let's stay with Marcus for a while, because his Tuesday is just getting started.

So Marcus does not immediately come clean about the three circuits. He pockets his phone, unrolls some more wire, and starts mentally calculating what he can quietly undo and redo before his journeyman, let's call him Ray, gets back from the panel pull. The math is not great. The math involves about forty feet of Romex that is already stapled, two j-boxes that are screwed into studs that are now, courtesy of Big Dave, in the wrong room, and one very inconveniently located smoke detector pre-wire that is going to require him to go back into the attic.

Marcus does not like the attic. It is July. The attic is a casserole.

But here's the part that compounds all of this. Marcus has been low-grade annoyed for about three weeks. Not at anyone in particular, not at anything he could point to and name, just annoyed. Because here is what his experience of this job has been: he shows up, Ray tells him what to run, he runs it, sometimes Ray changes it, nobody really explains why, and Marcus is basically a two-legged wire dispenser with a staple gun. He's been doing the work but he hasn't been connected to the work. He doesn't know why they stub up in this corner instead of that corner. He doesn't know why they specify this conduit on outdoor runs when the other stuff would technically be code. He doesn't know why they triple-check for panel clearance on every single inspection even though it's always fine.

He just does it because Ray says so, and he shows up because it pays, and that is a very different thing from believing in what you're doing.

And so when the room flip happened, his first instinct wasn't "let me call Ray and get this figured out correctly." His first instinct was "let me hide it and fix it myself." And that instinct — the hiding, the solo problem-solving, the refusal to ask — comes directly from not being bought in. When you believe in the mission and you trust the structure around you, your first instinct when something goes sideways is to communicate, because you understand that the structure is there to help you. When you don't believe in it, your first instinct is to work around it, because the structure feels like overhead. It feels like people waiting to catch you.

So Marcus is in the attic. It is extremely hot. He is re-routing a smoke detector pre-wire by himself based on a box schedule that may or may not reflect the room flip that Big Dave just announced verbally with zero paperwork. He is sweating through his shirt. He pulls the wire. It's about eight inches short.

And then Ray calls.

Ray says, "Hey, how's the rough-in going?" And Marcus says, "Good." And Ray says, "Big Dave texted me about the room flip. You get the message?" And Marcus says, after a pause that lasts just slightly too long, "Yeah, just working through it." And Ray says, "Okay, what's your plan?" And Marcus, from inside a July attic, gives a plan that is a collection of partially true statements arranged to sound more complete than they are. And Ray, who has been doing this for fourteen years, hears it immediately.

Ray shows up forty minutes later. He doesn't come in hot. He doesn't embarrass Marcus in front of the framers. He climbs up into the attic, looks at the situation, looks at Marcus, and says, "Walk me through what you did this morning before Dave told you about the flip." And Marcus does. And it is uncomfortable. And when he's done, Ray just nods and says, "Okay. Here's what we're actually going to do." And they fix it together in about ninety minutes.

But Ray does one more thing before they move on. He sits down on a bucket — they're always sitting on buckets for the serious conversations, that's a universal law of the trades — and he explains to Marcus why they do it the way they do. Why this particular GC requires written confirmation on any scope change before they touch anything. Why the panel clearance check matters even when it looks fine, because three jobs ago they nearly failed an inspection on a house with a tight utility room and it cost a day of work and a re-inspection fee and a very bad afternoon. Why they stub up in the corner near the window on bedrooms for this builder because their rough-in inspection guy always checks that corner first and if it's clean there he's in a good mood for the rest of the walk.

Marcus didn't know any of that. And none of it is written in any manual. It's in Ray's head, and Ray had just never thought to share it, because Ray had forgotten what it felt like to be two years in and just following instructions without understanding the why behind any of them.

Here is the principle. And this is the part where we pull up from the story and look at the bigger picture, so hang with me.

Jocko and Leif's chapter on Believe is about something that sounds simple but is actually a real test of character. The idea is this: before you can lead others, before you can give an order or ask someone to do something hard or keep a team moving when things go wrong, you have to believe in the mission yourself. Not pretend to believe in it. Not comply with it. Actually believe in it, which means understanding why it matters and deciding, for real, that it's worth doing.

But here's the part that people miss. Believing isn't just a leadership problem at the top. It's not just the owner or the project manager who needs to believe. Everyone in the chain needs to be able to trace the line from what they're doing with their hands right now to why it matters. And when someone in the chain can't trace that line — when they're just executing instructions without any understanding of the reason behind them — they become brittle. They can't adapt. They can't make good judgment calls when the situation changes, because all they have is the instruction, not the principle behind it. So when the room flips, they hide. When the wire comes up short, they patch it wrong. When the GC changes scope, they nod and say "good" when they mean "I have no idea."

The other half of the principle is the flip side. If you have done the work of understanding why — if you've asked the questions, listened to the answers, and still have a genuine problem with what you're being asked to do — then you are supposed to say so. Not in the middle of a job. Not in front of the GC. You take it up the chain, in private, directly, and you make your case. And then — this is the important part — if the decision stands, you commit. Completely. Because half-committed is the worst of both worlds: you're not changing the outcome and you're not doing your best work.

The reason this is hard for apprentices specifically is that the culture around you sometimes makes asking questions feel dangerous. It can feel like admitting you don't know something is the same as admitting you're incompetent. And the version of toughness that gets celebrated on a lot of jobsites is exactly the wrong version: the guy who never asks, never flags, just grinds through by himself. That guy isn't tough. That guy is an isolated liability waiting for something to go wrong. The actually tough thing is being willing to say "I don't know why we're doing this" to someone who's been doing it for fifteen years, and actually listen to the answer.

So what does this look like in practice for a Horsepower Electric apprentice? Let's get specific, because "believe in the mission" is not an action item you can take to work tomorrow. These are.

First one. Before you start your section of rough-in each morning — even if it's a section you've done twenty times — know the one thing that could get it flagged on inspection. Not the list of everything. The one most likely thing on this job, on this GC's watch, in this inspector's jurisdiction. If you don't know it, you ask Ray before you pull wire, not after. The question is simple: "What's the inspection flag I should be watching for on this section?" That question does two things. It gives you information you need to do your job right, and it signals to your journeyman that you're thinking about the outcome, not just the task. Two minutes of conversation in the morning is worth an hour of backtracking in the afternoon.

Second one. When the scope changes — and it will change, because it always changes — you do not process that change alone. Ever. The instinct to just handle it, to just quietly adjust and move on, feels like competence but it usually isn't. What it usually is, is a way to avoid a slightly uncomfortable conversation. The move is to stop what you're doing, write down exactly what Big Dave or whoever just told you, and then call or text Ray with the specific question: "Dave said we're flipping the bedroom and office. Before I touch the rough-in I've already run, can we talk through the scope?" That's it. That's the message. You're not asking for permission to breathe. You're creating a checkpoint so that two people are thinking about the problem instead of one. On a high-end residential job, one-person decisions about scope are where re-inspection fees come from.

Third one. At some point this week, probably on a slow moment — a lunch break, a drive to the supply house, whatever — ask your journeyman to explain one decision you saw them make that you didn't understand. Not to challenge it. Actually to understand it. "Hey, why do we pull the home runs to this corner instead of the panel corner? Is that a code thing, or is that a this-GC thing, or is that something else?" And then actually listen. Don't be thinking about what you're going to say next. Just take in the information. What you're doing is building your own internal model of why this company does things the way it does, and every piece of that model makes you better at adapting when things go sideways — which they will, probably by Thursday.

Fourth one. If you have a real problem with a direction you've been given — and I mean a real problem, not just "I would have done it differently" but an actual concern about code compliance, safety, or something that doesn't add up — you say something. Specifically, you say it to Ray, and you say it like this: "I want to make sure I'm understanding this right before I run it. Can you walk me through why we're doing it this way?" That's not a challenge. That's a question. And if the answer makes sense, great, now you believe in it for a real reason. And if the answer doesn't land, you follow up once more and then you let the person with the license make the call. What you don't do is silently disagree and half-ass the work. That is the worst outcome for everyone.

Alright. Let's head across the yard now — figuratively speaking — over to the Wells Cutting Edge side of the operation, where the equipment is bigger, the mud is real, and the principle is exactly the same even though the work looks completely different.

Okay. Different crew, different trade, same general situation. And same general Tuesday morning energy.

Jordan is about eighteen months into his time at Wells Cutting Edge. He came in knowing how to run a plate compactor and not much else. By now he can read a grading plan — sort of — he understands the basics of how a drainage system is supposed to work, and he has developed strong opinions about which brand of paver gloves actually holds up. He is assigned to a crew doing a full backyard renovation: new hardscape, new irrigation, new planting, and a drainage system that the designer spec'd out specifically because the homeowner has a finished basement and a history of getting water against the foundation when it rains hard.

The lead on his crew is a guy named Carlos, who is methodical and experienced and not particularly talkative. Carlos is the kind of lead who gives clear instructions but doesn't explain them much, not because he doesn't know why, but because he's been doing this long enough that the why seems obvious to him. This is relevant.

On this particular Tuesday, Carlos has given Jordan a task that Jordan does not understand. They're getting ready to set the edge restraints for the main patio, and Carlos has marked a line that is noticeably — visually, obviously — not parallel to the back of the house. The patio is going to look crooked to a person who doesn't know what they're looking at. Jordan knows what he's looking at. He thinks. He stares at the line. He looks at the house. He looks at the design sheet. The design sheet has a lot of lines on it and he's not totally sure which one he's looking at. He does not ask Carlos because Carlos is on the phone with the supplier about a plant delivery that is apparently going sideways, and Jordan doesn't want to interrupt.

So Jordan makes a decision. He adjusts the line. Just slightly. He pulls it two inches to the left, which in his head makes it more parallel to the back of the house, which in his head means it looks right. He sets the stakes. He starts running the edge restraint. He gets about twelve feet in before Carlos gets off the phone, walks over, and stops.

Carlos looks at the restraint. He looks at Jordan. He looks at the restraint again. And very quietly, he says, "Why did you move the line?"

And Jordan says, "It looked crooked."

And Carlos says, "It's supposed to look that way."

Here is what Jordan did not know. The patio is not square to the house on purpose. The grade runs slightly away from the foundation along the back, and the drainage system is designed so that the patio surface sheds water toward the rear right corner where there's a channel drain. To get the surface to drain correctly, the patio needs to slope two percent toward that corner, which means the pavers have to be set at an angle that accounts for that slope. If you make the patio visually parallel to the house, you eliminate the slope. And if you eliminate the slope, you have a flat patio — or worse, a patio that pitches water back toward the house — and in a finished-basement situation, that is a very expensive problem that shows up six months later during the first heavy rain.

Carlos had marked that line with extreme precision. He had checked it against the grading plan. He had checked it with a level. He knew exactly what he was doing and why. And Jordan, in about four minutes of unsupervised decision-making, had undone it.

To Carlos's credit, again — and apparently good leads in both trades have this in common — he doesn't make Jordan feel like a complete disaster. He corrects the work. He explains the drainage logic. They fix the restraint in about twenty minutes. But Carlos also asks Jordan a question that is actually kind of hard to answer: "When did you decide to move it, and why didn't you come ask me first?"

And Jordan's answer, honestly, is something like: "I didn't want to bother you, and I thought I understood what I was looking at."

And Carlos says, "Those are two different problems."

He's right. They are.

The first problem — not wanting to bother someone — is a social anxiety problem. It's manageable. The second problem — thinking you understand what you're looking at when you don't — that one is more interesting. Because Jordan isn't dumb. He's not careless. He's been paying attention. He has learned real things in eighteen months. The issue is that he doesn't know what he doesn't know. He has a partial map, and he's been using it as if it's a full one. And the reason he's been using it as a full map is that nobody has ever really shown him what the full map looks like, so he doesn't know what's missing from his own.

The drainage plan, the slope percentage, the reason a finished basement changes everything about how you design a surface drain — none of that was in any conversation Jordan had been part of. It wasn't secret information. Carlos knows it. The designer knows it. It's in the notes on the plan. But Jordan's experience of this job, like Marcus's experience across town, had been: show up, get told what to do, do it. And that produces someone who is competent in a narrow lane and dangerous just outside it.

There's another layer here that's worth naming, and it connects back directly to the Believe chapter. Jordan had also been, quietly, a little skeptical of some of Carlos's methods. Not vocally. Not in a way he'd ever say out loud. But in the back of his head he'd had a few moments of "I don't know why we're doing it that way" that had never turned into actual questions. They'd just turned into a low-grade sense that maybe Carlos was being overly complicated about things. And that low-grade skepticism meant that when Jordan saw the line that looked wrong to him, he trusted his own eye over Carlos's mark. Not because he had information that contradicted Carlos. Because he had a vibe. And vibes are not a drainage plan.

That's the dangerous version of not believing in the mission. It's not rebellious. It's not loud. It's quiet and it looks like confidence, and it leads to two inches of edge restraint in the wrong place and a homeowner with a wet basement six months from now.

So let's talk about what a Wells Cutting Edge apprentice can actually do with all of this, in the same way we did for the electric crew.

First thing. Before you touch anything that has a mark or a stake or a grade pin on it, know what that mark means. Not approximately. Specifically. If you can't read it off the plan, you ask. The question is: "Before I start setting this, can you walk me through what we're trying to achieve with this line?" You are not asking because you think it's wrong. You are asking because you need to know the outcome you're working toward, not just the instruction. On a drainage or grading project especially, moving a stake two inches in the wrong direction can change the whole system. The cost of a three-minute conversation is zero. The cost of fixing a finished-basement drainage problem is very much not zero.

Second thing. When you see something that looks wrong to you, the protocol is not to adjust it yourself. The protocol is to flag it. There's a specific way to do this that doesn't come across as second-guessing: "Hey, this line looks like it's not parallel to the house — is that intentional, or do you want me to double-check it?" That's it. You're not saying Carlos is wrong. You're asking if it's intentional. That one question, if Jordan had asked it, would have gotten him the whole explanation about the drainage slope in about ninety seconds and they'd have never had to fix anything. The adjustment instinct — just quietly correct it and move on — feels helpful but it's actually just self-reliance in a situation where the stakes are too high for self-reliance.

Third thing. On any project with a drainage or grading component — and for Wells Cutting Edge that's a lot of projects — take five minutes at some point before the install starts to understand where the water is supposed to go. Not the technical details, just the basic story: water comes from here, flows across this surface, exits there. If you can tell that story in three sentences, you have enough to catch yourself before you make a change that breaks the system. If you can't tell that story, ask Carlos or whoever your lead is. "Can you give me the thirty-second version of how this drainage is designed to work?" Most leads, if you ask it like that, will actually enjoy explaining it. It's one of those questions that signals you're thinking beyond your immediate task.

Fourth thing. This one is about the bigger picture stuff — the company practices that might not make immediate sense to you. Why does Wells Cutting Edge require a photo of every irrigation line before it gets covered? Why do they always do a water test on the full system before any planting goes in? Why do they submit the grading plan to the HOA liaison before they even order materials? If you don't know why any of those things happen, find out. Not to challenge them. To understand them. Because right now, if you're honest with yourself, you probably have at least two or three company procedures you follow because you were told to, not because you understand what they protect against. And the day something goes sideways — which is every Tuesday, eventually — those are the first procedures that feel optional when they are very much not optional.

Ask your lead: "Can you help me understand why we always do the water test before planting?" And then listen. And then actually let the answer change how you feel about doing the water test. That's the whole thing. That's believing in the mission. Not compliance. Understanding that produces real buy-in.

Now let's bring both crews back together for a second, because the thing that Marcus and Jordan have in common is not the trade and not the mistake, exactly. It's the underlying condition. They both arrived at the moment of crisis — the room flip, the misread grade line — without enough context to make a good decision. And in both cases, the lack of context came from the same source: nobody had ever really explained the why behind the what. Ray hadn't. Carlos hadn't. Not because they were bad leads, but because when you've been doing something for a long time, the reasoning behind your decisions becomes invisible to you. It becomes instinct. And instinct doesn't automatically transfer.

So part of believing in the mission, especially when you're newer, is actively seeking out the context that explains why your company does what it does. It's not going to come to you automatically. You have to ask for it. And the reason that feels awkward — the reason Marcus didn't ask Ray why they stub up in that corner, the reason Jordan didn't ask Carlos why the line looked the way it did — is that asking feels like admitting you don't know. And not knowing feels like vulnerability. But here's the reality: you don't know. You're an apprentice. You're eighteen months in, two years in. Not knowing is not a character flaw. It's just a fact about where you are in the process. The flaw is pretending to know when you don't, because that's where the attic re-routes happen, and that's where the wet basements come from.

The other piece — the commitment piece — matters just as much. If you have asked the question and gotten the answer and you still have a problem with it, say so, once, to the right person, and then let the decision land. Half-measures are the enemy. A guy who's ninety percent in and privately thinks the plan is dumb is less useful to the crew than a guy who was skeptical but made a real decision to trust the process and now is actually all in. Real commitment isn't the absence of doubt. It's the decision you make on the other side of doubt.

Marcus went back on that job and started asking Ray one question every day about why they were doing something the way they were doing it. Not to be annoying. Just to build the map. After a couple weeks, Ray started volunteering explanations before Marcus had to ask, because he noticed the questions had a pattern. They were building something together — a shared understanding of the work — and that is actually what a functional crew looks like. Jordan asked Carlos to walk him through the drainage plan on the next job before they ever set a stake, and Carlos looked at him a little differently after that. Not in a sentimental way. In a "this guy might actually be useful when things get complicated" way. Which is exactly the way you want to be looked at when you're still learning the trade.

If you want to be trusted with more, start by understanding what you've already been trusted with, and why.
