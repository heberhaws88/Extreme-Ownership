It's seven-fifteen on a Tuesday morning, and Marcus is already losing his mind.

Not loudly. Marcus doesn't do loud. He does this thing where he gets very quiet and starts reorganizing the back of the van by size, which is how you know something has gone sideways. Wire nuts by diameter. Tape by width. And he's doing that right now, which means somebody on the rough-in crew has done something Marcus considers personally offensive to the craft of electrical work.

You walk up, you look in the back of the van, you see the reorganizing happening, and you say the only sensible thing: "What happened?"

Marcus closes his eyes for exactly one second. "You know the panel room off the garage? The one we spent forty-five minutes last week talking about clearance requirements because the GC wanted to push the mechanical equipment closer?"

You know the panel room.

"Danny ran the branch circuits."

You still don't see the problem yet. Danny's been with you two years. Danny is fine.

"Danny ran the branch circuits, and then Danny — and I want you to understand I am not exaggerating — Danny stapled the home run for the sub-feed directly across the face of the panel clearance zone. Like, not close to the clearance zone. Not brushing the edge of the clearance zone. Across the middle of it. In a diagonal line. Like he was trying to make it worse."

You walk over to the panel room. You look at Danny's work. And Marcus is, to his credit, not exaggerating even a little bit.

Danny is on the other side of the house pulling wire for the kitchen. You can hear him whistling something. He is having a perfectly good morning, completely unaware that he has just handed the inspector a reason to fail the whole rough-in and potentially kicked off a conversation with the GC that neither of you wants to have three days before the framing inspection.

This is the part of the story where you have to decide who you're going to be about it.

Here's what you want to do: you want to walk over there, and you want to say, "Danny, buddy, pal, you ran a home run directly through a panel clearance zone, which is not a gray area, it is a code requirement we talked about last week, what exactly were you thinking?" And then you want to make sure everyone within earshot knows that you, the Lead, absolutely knew better, and this was Danny's mistake, Danny's oversight, Danny's problem to fix.

Here's what actually happened the first time I was in that situation — not this exact situation, but close enough that it hurts to tell — I did exactly that. I went and found the guy. I made the correction very publicly. I made sure the point landed that I had nothing to do with this. And the rough-in got fixed, and the inspection passed, and I thought that was the end of it.

But here's the thing nobody tells you about the construction business, and frankly about any business where a crew of people has to function like a machine every single day: a crew that's afraid of getting called out in public doesn't get faster. It gets quieter. It starts hiding problems instead of surfacing them. The guy who made the mistake doesn't come to you next time there's a gray-area question — he just makes a call and hopes nobody notices. Because the last time he brought a problem to your attention, what he got was a public correction and a story that got retold in the break trailer.

So you end up with a crew that is technically functional but operating with this low-grade fear underneath everything. They're not bringing you the small problems before they become big ones. They're not asking questions when they're not sure. They're not telling each other "hey, I think that's wrong" because the culture has made it too risky to be the one who doesn't know something.

And then one day the inspection fails, or the GC calls, or a homeowner notices that three recessed cans in the master bedroom are half an inch off the layout line that was drawn on the plans, and you find out about it the bad way, from the outside, when it's already a problem.

That is the world this principle lives in.

The chapter is called No Bad Teams, Only Bad Leaders, and the first time you read that you might push back on it a little. You might think: okay, but what about the guy who genuinely doesn't care? What about the apprentice who came in already checked out? What about the crew you inherited from someone else, where the habits were already baked in bad?

And the honest answer — the one that stings a little — is that the principle doesn't let you off the hook for any of those scenarios. Because the underlying idea isn't that every single person on your crew is secretly excellent and just waiting for permission to show it. It's something more specific and more useful than that. It's this: the standard your crew performs to is the standard you establish, reinforce, and model every single day. If your crew is inconsistent, that's a training and accountability issue, and the training and accountability are yours. If your crew is hiding mistakes, that's a culture issue, and culture comes from the Lead. If your crew does great work on the days you're physically present on the job and mediocre work on the days you're not, that's not a crew problem — that's a leadership gap.

The way the book frames it, and this is paraphrasing, is that a leader who looks at a struggling team and asks "what's wrong with these people" is asking the wrong question. The right question is "what about how I am leading this team is producing this result?" That shift in the question changes everything, because one of those questions has no productive answer — you can't fix "these people" — and the other question has a very productive answer: you can change what you do, what you say, what you check, what you let slide, what you correct, and how you correct it.

This is also not the same as saying everything is always the Lead's fault in some punishing, the-buck-stops-here-and-crushes-me way. It's not about blame. It's about ownership. You own the outcome. Which means you own the inputs. The crew's performance is an output. Your leadership is an input. When the output is bad, a good lead looks at the inputs first.

And what makes this hard — genuinely hard, not just philosophically uncomfortable — is that most of the time when a crew is underperforming, the Lead doesn't know it's happening. They think they've been clear. They think the crew understands the standard. They gave the instruction, the crew nodded, and now everyone's on the same page. Except the nod doesn't mean the same thing to the guy nodding as it does to the person who gave the instruction. And the gap between what you think you communicated and what actually landed in the other person's head is where most jobsite problems are born.

So let's get practical. Let's talk about what a Horsepower Electric Lead can do this week — not someday, not in a theoretical perfect world, but at the next job, maybe even at this job — to actually live this principle out.

The first one is about briefings, and it's simpler than it sounds. When you assign work, don't just say what needs to happen. Say what right looks like when it's done. There is a difference between "run the branch circuits in the panel room" and "run the branch circuits in the panel room, keep everything clear of the thirty-six inch clearance zone in front of the panel, and if you're not sure whether something is inside that zone, stop and come get me before you staple anything down." One of those instructions leaves room for a very creative interpretation. The other one doesn't. The Lead's job is to shrink the gap between the instruction and the execution, and the fastest way to do that is to be specific about what done-right actually looks like before the work starts, not after.

The second one is about how you handle mistakes when they happen — and they will happen, because you're running a crew of humans in a trade where conditions change every day. When someone makes a mistake, the correction should be private if at all possible, specific about what happened and why it matters, and forward-looking about what to do differently. Not "Danny, what were you thinking, that's a clearance violation" — that's a conversation where Danny learns to feel stupid but doesn't necessarily learn to not do it again. Instead: "Hey, walk with me for a second. I want to show you something in the panel room, and I want to make sure I explained this clearly enough last week because it's important for the inspection." You are not pretending the mistake didn't happen. You are owning a piece of it — because here's the truth, the Lead always owns a piece of it — and you're making it possible for the person to actually hear the correction instead of just surviving it.

The third one is about presence and walkthroughs, and this one is a discipline thing for the Lead, not a discipline thing for the crew. At a minimum, once a day, walk the job. Not to check up on people, not to catch mistakes, but to stay calibrated on what the crew's version of "acceptable" looks like. Because the standard drifts. On every job, on every crew, the standard drifts a little bit every day, and it always drifts toward easier, not harder. The walkthrough is how you reset it. And when you see something that doesn't meet the standard — a box installed a little sloppy, a staple pattern that isn't code-compliant, a junction box that's going to be impossible to access behind that soffit — you address it at that moment, casually, not as a big deal, just: "Hey, let's fix that before we move on." You catch ten small things that way and you prevent two big things. Every time.

The fourth one, and this is maybe the hardest one because it requires you to be honest with yourself about something uncomfortable: if you have a crew member who is consistently underperforming, ask yourself whether you have actually given them a fair chance to succeed. Have they been trained, specifically, not just shown once? Have they been given clear expectations in writing or at least in a conversation where both people walked away understanding the same thing? Have you actually had the performance conversation, or have you been hoping the problem would self-resolve? A lot of underperforming crew members are not underperforming because they don't care. They are underperforming because they don't know exactly what the standard is, and nobody has made it crystal clear, and the Lead has been privately frustrated without actually communicating the frustration in a way that the crew member can act on. If you can answer yes to all of those things — trained, clear expectations, direct conversation — and the performance is still not there, then you have a different problem, and it has a different solution. But most of the time, when you ask yourself those questions honestly, you find out that at least one of them is a no. And that's your work to do first.

Now let's head over to the Wells Cutting Edge side of the yard, where the stakes involve slightly less electricity but roughly the same amount of human chaos.

It is eight o'clock on a Wednesday morning, and a paver crew is staring at a pile of cut travertine like the travertine has personally wronged them.

The job is a full backyard overhaul. We're talking twelve hundred square feet of paver patio, a new irrigation zone, a perimeter garden bed with specimen plantings, low-voltage pathway lighting, and a dry creek drainage feature running along the back fence line. It's a big job, beautiful design, the kind of job you photograph for the portfolio. The homeowners — let's call them the Fairchilds, because every client on a job like this feels vaguely like they should be named the Fairchilds — have a hard deadline because they're hosting a garden party in six weeks and have already sent the invitations.

You, the Lead, have a three-person paver crew, an irrigation sub coming in on Thursday, and a planting crew that needs the beds rough-graded before they can do anything useful. In theory, this all fits together. The schedule is tight but achievable.

On Tuesday, the homeowner called the office.

Not about the schedule. Not about the plantings. About the pattern.

The Fairchilds had originally approved a running bond pattern for the travertine. Clean, classic, timeless. The designer signed off. The materials were ordered, delivered, and staged. And then Mrs. Fairchild — who had, by all accounts, been completely happy with the running bond right up until she saw a photo of a herringbone patio on a home renovation account she follows — decided she'd like it in herringbone instead.

You get this news on Tuesday afternoon. Herringbone is more cut-intensive, more labor-intensive, and requires going back to the material yard for additional cuts and potentially additional pieces depending on how the layout works out at the perimeter. The paver crew has already started screeding the base.

Now here's the thing: you inherited this crew six weeks ago when the previous Lead on this job took another position. And you've noticed, in six weeks, that this crew gets things done, but they get things done quietly. Nobody flags problems before they turn into problems. Nobody says "hey, I think we need to check this with the boss." They just push through, and when something goes wrong they handle it internally and then tell you about it after. Which sounds like self-sufficiency, and sometimes it is, but on a job like this with a tight timeline and a design-review submission due to the HOA, what it actually means is that you find out about issues after the window to address them cleanly has passed.

So when you call the crew together Wednesday morning to tell them about the pattern change, you get three faces that tell you three different things. Rodrigo, who has been paving for eleven years and has the kind of forearm strength that suggests he's done this more or less every day for all eleven of them, immediately does the math on what herringbone means for the cut count and the blade time, and his expression suggests the math does not make him happy. Tyler, who is twenty-three and has been doing landscape work for two years, nods like he's agreeing with something but his eyes say he is not totally sure what he's agreeing with. And Devin, who is actually quite good but tends to go invisible whenever the energy in a conversation gets complicated, just looks at the travertine pile.

You lay out the change. You explain the timeline impact. You tell them you've already called the material yard and confirmed the additional pieces can come Thursday morning, which means they lose the back half of today and the front half of tomorrow on the paver installation, but if they pick up the pace on the secondary cuts they can make it up.

Rodrigo says: "We could've told you Monday there was going to be a change."

You blink. "What do you mean?"

"She came out here Monday morning. Walked the screeded base. Asked me what herringbone would look like. I figured it was just a question."

This is the moment. This is the moment you either own or deflect. Because what Rodrigo just told you is that there was information on Monday — information that, if it had come to you on Monday, would have given you two additional days to address the pattern change before it hit the critical path of the schedule. That information existed. It was on the jobsite. And it sat there because nobody had established a clear expectation that client interactions of any kind get reported up immediately, and also because in six weeks you haven't yet built the kind of trust where Rodrigo feels like flagging a "probably nothing" to the Lead is worth the effort.

So you have a crew that's behind schedule because of a change order they saw coming. And you have a material shortage because nobody escalated. And you have a HOA submission deadline that is now uncomfortable to look at directly.

And here's what you absolutely cannot do, even though part of you wants to: you cannot look at Rodrigo and say "why didn't you tell me?" in the tone that makes it Rodrigo's fault. Because here is the thing about that crew that you inherited, the one that doesn't flag problems before they turn into problems — that is not a Rodrigo problem. That is a culture problem. And you inherited the culture along with the crew. And in six weeks, you haven't changed the culture. Which makes it, at this point, your culture.

This is exactly where "no bad teams, only bad leaders" lands in the world of high-end residential landscaping. Because a paver crew that stays quiet when they see a potential change order coming is not a bad crew. They are a crew that has learned — from this job, from previous jobs, from the cumulative experience of working in an environment where raising a concern sometimes meant getting told to mind your own business and just lay the pavers — that the path of least resistance is to keep your head down and do the work in front of you. And that lesson was taught to them by whoever was leading them before you, and reinforced by whoever was leading them before that, and you have been in place for six weeks without changing it.

So what does this week look like for a Wells Cutting Edge Lead who wants to actually turn this crew into something that functions the way it should?

The first thing is what you do right now, in this moment, with Rodrigo's comment still hanging in the air. You don't make it a big deal. You don't make it a blame moment in either direction. You say: "Okay. That's on me. I should've made it clearer that any client conversation — doesn't matter how small it seems — comes to me same day. Starting today, that's the rule. You guys talk to the client on-site, I hear about it within an hour. Phone call, text, whatever works. I need those eyes and ears because I can't be here every minute." You are setting a new expectation, clearly, in the moment, connected to a real situation that just cost everyone something. That's when it lands. Not in a pre-job meeting three weeks ago that everyone half-listened to. Now.

The second thing is about Tyler and Devin, because there's a thing that happens on crews with a strong veteran — and Rodrigo is absolutely the strong veteran on this crew — where the other guys defer to him so completely that they stop being independent contributors. Tyler and Devin have both essentially been waiting for Rodrigo to decide things and then following him. Which means the crew is running effectively as one-and-a-half people and a veteran. And a Lead who doesn't address that is leaving capacity on the table, which on a tight timeline is the last thing you can afford. So you start giving Tyler and Devin direct assignments with clear outcomes — not "help Rodrigo with the herringbone cuts" but "Tyler, your job today is the layout strings for the east and north perimeters, and when you've got them set I want you to verify the square before Rodrigo starts cutting. Here's what you're checking for." You're giving Tyler something to own, something to be responsible for, something where his judgment matters. You build the crew by building each person in it, not by running everything through the strongest member.

The third thing is the walkthrough, and it works the same way here as it does on the electrical side. Once a day, you walk the job with intention. Not to check on people, but to stay in front of the work. On a landscape job this big, there are always things that are about to become problems but aren't problems yet. The grade on the dry creek bed looks like it might be running slightly toward the house rather than away from it — you catch that in a walkthrough on day two instead of finding out after heavy rain fills the client's basement. The gap pattern on the travertine border is drifting a millimeter and will be noticeably off by the time you hit the far corner — you catch it in a walkthrough on day three instead of tearing out sixty square feet of finished paving. The walkthrough is cheap. The fix is expensive. The Lead who walks the job regularly is the Lead whose crew delivers clean work on tight schedules, because small corrections cost almost nothing and large corrections cost everything.

The fourth thing is specific to the situation with the HOA submission, but it generalizes pretty well: when you're behind, tell the people who need to know immediately, with a plan. Don't sit on it hoping to make up the time and avoid the conversation. Call the project manager, explain the change order, explain the new timeline, and have at least a rough plan for how you're going to close the gap before you make the call. "We're behind because of a client-initiated pattern change, we've got materials coming Thursday, and I think we can make up most of the time if we run the irrigation rough-in parallel to the paver install on Friday instead of sequential. I need to know if the sub can flex." That's a conversation where you're behind but you're in control. The alternative — where you don't make that call and the project manager finds out from someone else, or finds out when the schedule misses — is a conversation where you're behind and you've also lost credibility. And credibility is the one thing a Lead can't afford to run out of.

Alright. Let's bring it home.

Danny with his home run stapled across the panel clearance zone. Rodrigo with his Monday morning information sitting quietly in his pocket. Tyler nodding at things he doesn't fully understand. Devin going invisible when the conversation gets complicated. These are not bad crew members. They are crew members performing exactly at the level of the leadership they've been given.

That's the through-line of this principle, and it's the same whether you're pulling wire on a high-end custom home or setting travertine on a backyard showpiece. The crew is a mirror. When you look at what the crew is doing — the quality, the consistency, the communication, the willingness to surface problems before they become disasters — you are looking at a reflection of your leadership. Not a perfect reflection, not one-for-one, but close enough that you can't explain it away.

And what makes this principle actually liberating rather than just heavy — and it can feel heavy the first time you really let it land — is that it puts the solution in your hands. A bad crew that you can't fix is a helpless situation. A leadership gap that you own and can improve is a project. And Leads are good at projects. You solve problems every single day in conditions that would make most people's heads spin — scope changes at eight in the morning, materials that didn't show up, inspectors who want to have a philosophical debate about whether this is technically a panel clearance violation. You handle all of that. You can handle this too.

The question is never "what's wrong with this team." The question is always "what kind of leader does this team need right now, and am I being that?"

That's the job.
